## Korea

### 1. HMM-operated ship Namu hit by explosion and fire in Hormuz

- **Date**: May 4–10 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: At about 3:30 p.m. (local time) on May 4, an explosion went off and a fire broke out in the engine room of the Namu, a Panama-flagged bulk carrier operated by HMM, while it was anchored near the United Arab Emirates inside the Strait of Hormuz. Six Korean and 18 foreign crew members were on board, and no one was hurt. It was the day the U.S. began Project Freedom, an operation to escort merchant ships trapped in the strait out to safety. At the time, 26 Korean-flagged ships were stuck inside the strait, and 160 Korean seafarers were in the strait.

  On the 10th, a joint government investigation team said two unidentified flying objects had struck the outer hull of the ballast tank on the left side of the stern twice, about one minute apart. The hull was broken across about 5 m and to a depth of about 7 m into the ship, and the team found it unlikely that a mine or torpedo was involved. The objects were caught on CCTV, but the team could not identify who launched them or what type they were. On the same day, the foreign ministry called in Iranian Ambassador Saeed Koozechi to explain the findings. Asked by reporters for his position, the ambassador only told them to ask Iran’s foreign ministry.

  On the day of the attack, U.S. President Donald Trump said Iran had fired on a Korean cargo ship and that it might be time for South Korea to join the operation. On the 5th, he claimed the Korean ship had been attacked after leaving the convoy and acting alone. On the 6th, the Iranian embassy in Seoul denied that Iran’s military was involved, but on the same day a column on Iran’s state Press TV wrote that targeting the Korean ship, which had broken new maritime rules, was a “physical action” by Iran. After the U.S. paused the operation, National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac said on the 6th, “Since the operation has been halted, a review is no longer needed.”
- **Why it matters**: It is a case of a Korean ship being directly hit since the Iran war began. Without knowing who carried out the attack, the U.S. president used it to publicly call for the Korean military to join the escort operation. The government had been reviewing the request in light of military readiness on the Korean Peninsula and the procedures required by Korean law, but the operation stopped, so it did not decide whether to take part.

### 3. First attempt in 39 years to amend the Constitution fails

- **Date**: May 7–8 (Thu–Fri)
- **What happened**: On May 7, the National Assembly put a constitutional amendment to a vote at a plenary session, but People Power Party lawmakers did not vote, so the required number (two-thirds of all members, 191) was not reached and the vote was declared void. The amendment would require the president to get the National Assembly’s approval to declare martial law and would add the spirit of the Bu-Ma Democratic Protests and the May 18 Democratization Movement to the preamble of the Constitution. It was proposed on April 3 by 187 lawmakers from six parties, including the Democratic Party, and independents. At least 12 People Power Party lawmakers would have had to vote for it for it to pass.

  National Assembly Speaker Woo Won-shik held another plenary session on the 8th, but dropped his plan to put the amendment to a vote again after the People Power Party announced a filibuster against it, the first ever against a constitutional amendment. Woo declared, “As of today, the procedure to hold a national referendum on June 3 has stopped.” The People Power Party said it did not object to what the amendment says, but opposed it as a party, calling it “a political ploy for the election.”
- **Why it matters**: The attempt to change the Constitution, unchanged for 39 years since 1987, in time for the June 3 local elections ended at the National Assembly. The effort to write conditions for declaring martial law into the Constitution after the martial law of December 3 also stopped.

### 4. Ex-Marine commander Lim Sung-geun gets three years

- **Date**: May 8 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On May 8, Criminal Division 22 of the Seoul Central District Court (presiding judge Cho Hyung-woo) sentenced Lim Sung-geun, former commander of the 1st Marine Division, who was detained and charged with professional negligence causing death and injury and with disobeying orders under the Military Criminal Act, to three years in prison. That is less than the five years sought by the special counsel team for the Marine’s death. Park Sang-hyun, former commander of the 7th Brigade, and Choi Jin-gyu, former commander of the 11th Artillery Battalion, were sentenced to 18 months in prison without labor, and Lee Yong-min, former commander of the 7th Artillery Battalion, to 10 months in prison without labor, and all three were taken into custody in the courtroom. The ruling came 1,024 days after Corporal Chae Su-geun died on July 19, 2023, at a flood site in Yecheon, North Gyeongsang Province.

  The court found that even after operational control had passed to the Army, Lim gave search orders that had nothing to do with the conditions on the ground, saying, “Go down to the waterside, push through the bushes, poke around and search,” and did not make sure safety rules and equipment were in place. In setting the sentence, the court pointed to his efforts to hide evidence and avoid responsibility. About a long email Lim sent to the bereaved family saying the order for an underwater search had not come from him, the presiding judge said, “In my long career of trials, I have never seen anyone like this.”
- **Why it matters**: It is the first court finding that a senior commander’s orders caused the accident. The family said the sentence was disappointing.

### 5. Han Duck-soo gets 15 years on appeal for insurrection role

- **Date**: May 7 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On May 7, Criminal Division 12-1 of the Seoul High Court (presiding judge Lee Seung-chul), a panel dedicated to insurrection cases, sentenced former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo to 15 years in prison on charges including taking an important role in an insurrection. That is eight years less than the 23 years he received at the first trial. The court found that Han took part in giving martial law the look of proper procedure by calling a Cabinet meeting before it was declared. It also accepted the charge that he afterwards had a cover page made for the martial law proclamation, signed it and then had it shredded.

  The court said that Han had lived through the martial law of 1972 and 1980 himself and knew how serious it was, yet “abandoned his heavy duty.” It also rebuked him for repeatedly saying “I don’t remember,” from the investigation through the trial. As reasons for the shorter sentence, the court cited the lack of evidence that he planned or led the insurrection in advance, the fact that he called and chaired the Cabinet meeting to lift martial law after the National Assembly demanded it, and his more than 50 years in public service.
- **Why it matters**: The appeals court also found that the second most powerful official in the executive branch took part in the insurrection during the martial law of December 3. The court said the prime minister had a duty to check the president’s illegal use of power, and the shorter sentence came from its view of how far he took part.

### 1. Trump rejects Iran’s reply as unacceptable

- **Date**: May 4–10 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On May 4, the U.S. began Project Freedom to escort merchant ships trapped in the Strait of Hormuz out to safety, but on the 5th U.S. President Donald Trump paused the operation, citing requests from Pakistan and others and great progress toward a final deal. The naval blockade of Iranian ports stayed in place. By then, two merchant ships had left by the route escorted by the U.S., and hundreds of ships carrying up to 23,000 crew members were still in the Persian Gulf. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Epic Fury, the large-scale operation from the early part of the war, was over.

  On the night of the 7th, the most serious fighting since the ceasefire broke out. U.S. Central Command said Iran had attacked three destroyers passing through the strait with missiles, drones and small boats, and struck back at Iranian military sites. Iran accused the U.S. of attacking two ships and civilian areas. Trump called it a light tap and said the ceasefire was holding. Brent crude rose to about $101 a barrel.

  A week earlier, the U.S. had sent Iran, through Pakistan, a one-page memorandum with 14 points setting out a framework for reopening the strait and holding nuclear talks. On the 10th, Iran sent a reply demanding that sanctions be lifted, the blockade ended, the war stopped on all fronts and frozen assets released. The U.S. plan reportedly called for Iran to stop enriching uranium for up to 20 years, ship its highly enriched uranium abroad and dismantle its nuclear facilities, while Iran’s plan reportedly offered a shorter stop to enrichment, shipping out part of the uranium and diluting the rest, and refused to dismantle the facilities. Trump rejected Iran’s reply as totally unacceptable.
- **Why it matters**: A month into the ceasefire, fighting and the risk that talks would collapse came at the same time. The key issue is what to do with Iran’s highly enriched uranium, and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the war is not over as long as that uranium stays in Iran.

### 2. Hantavirus outbreak on cruise ship Hondius

- **Date**: May 5–10 (Tue–Sun)
- **What happened**: An outbreak of Andes hantavirus was confirmed on the MV Hondius, a polar expedition cruise ship that had left Argentina. A Dutch couple and a German passenger died, and on May 7 the World Health Organization (WHO) counted five of eight suspected cases as confirmed. The Andes type is a hantavirus that can spread between people through very close contact, and there is no vaccine. It can take up to six weeks for symptoms to appear.

  On the 7th, it came out that at least 29 passengers from 12 countries had already left the ship on the British island of Saint Helena on April 24, after the first death, and countries began tracing where they had been. The ship was held off Cape Verde, then arrived on the 10th at Tenerife in Spain’s Canary Islands with 146 people on board. After testing, passengers and crew boarded charter flights to more than 10 countries. Britain isolated returning passengers in hospital and then had them isolate at home for 45 days. One of the five people who went to France and two of the 17 heading to the U.S. had symptoms or tested positive.
- **Why it matters**: Some passengers had scattered before the infection was confirmed, so many countries started tracing at the same time. The WHO said this was not the start of a pandemic and rated the global risk as low, but said more cases could appear because of the long time it takes for symptoms to show.

### 5. U.S. court rules Trump’s 10% global tariff illegal

- **Date**: May 7 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On May 7, a panel of the U.S. Court of International Trade ruled 2 to 1 that the 10% tariff the Trump administration put on all imports under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 was illegal. Section 122 allows tariffs of up to 15% without the approval of Congress only when there is a large and serious balance-of-payments deficit, and the court found that the presidential proclamation did not show such a deficit. The administration itself had admitted in court that a balance-of-payments deficit is different from a trade deficit.

  The ruling applies only to the two importers that sued and to the state of Washington. The government must stop collecting the tariff from them and refund what they paid, while for other importers the tariff stays in place until it expires in July. The administration is expected to appeal, and Trump said he would do it another way.
- **Why it matters**: After the Supreme Court ruled in February that Trump’s broad emergency tariffs were illegal, the 10% global tariff that took their place was also ruled illegal in court. The government had been about to start refunding more than $166 billion for the tariffs ruled illegal earlier. The main tariffs left now are those on specific products, and the administration has started the process of putting new tariffs on specific countries.
