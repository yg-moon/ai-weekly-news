## Korea

### 1. KOSPI plunges 8.95%; new leveraged product listings banned

- **Date**: Jul 13–16 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On July 13, the KOSPI fell 669.01 points (8.95%) to close at 6,806.93. SK hynix closed down 15.37% at 1,845,000 won and Samsung Electronics down 10.70% at 254,500 won. At 10:34 a.m., a sell-side sidecar, a short trading pause, was triggered on the main market. Foreign investors and institutions were net sellers of about 3.9 trillion won, and individuals net buyers of 3.8809 trillion won. Over the weekend, the ceasefire between the U.S. and Iran ended and oil prices jumped, and a brokerage report said SK hynix’s second-quarter operating profit would come in about 8% below market forecasts.

  On the 14th, Goldman Sachs said that 2x leveraged exchange-traded funds (ETFs) based on Samsung Electronics and SK hynix had fallen more than 30% in a day, and that managers selling more of the underlying shares to keep the leverage ratio made the drop bigger. It estimated that 62% of net selling by Korean institutions the day before was ETF-related liquidation.

  On the 16th, the Financial Services Commission announced measures to protect investors in single-stock leveraged products. The minimum deposit needed to buy them for the first time or buy more rises from 10 million won to 30 million won, and only cash counts. No new products can be listed beyond the 16 already listed, and advertising and promotions for existing products were banned from that day. From around November, the minimum trading unit rises from 1 share to 20. Suspending trading in existing products or lowering their leverage was not included. On the 19th, Kim Yong-beom, the presidential chief of staff for policy, said, “More than 10 trillion won has gone into these products, and delisting them would itself be a huge shock to the market.”
- **Why it matters**: Single-stock leveraged products launched in late May, and the financial authorities tightened the entry rules a month and a half later. As of the 16th, it was the 37th KOSPI sidecar this year alone. The higher deposit is set to take effect around August 5 and the cash rule around August 19, so until then trading continues under the current rules.

### 2. Bank of Korea raises rates for the first time in 3½ years

- **Date**: Jul 16 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On July 16, the Monetary Policy Board of the Bank of Korea raised the benchmark rate by 0.25 percentage points from 2.50% to 2.75% a year. It is the first increase in three and a half years, since January 2023, and ends eight holds in a row. The decision by the seven board members was unanimous. The board said, “It is necessary to continue the rate-raising stance.”

  Governor Shin Hyun-song said, “Growth, prices and financial stability have all developed in a direction that supports the need to raise rates.” Consumer prices rose 3.2% in June, above 3% for the second month in a row. Real gross domestic product (GDP) in the first quarter grew 3.8% from a year earlier, while real gross domestic income (GDI) grew 13.2% because of higher chip prices. Shin said the 2.6% growth forecast for this year released in May was “too low.” Household loans from financial institutions are growing by 8 trillion to 9 trillion won a month.
- **Why it matters**: The Bank of Korea raised rates for the first time in three and a half years and said it would keep raising them, turning monetary policy toward tightening. The bank said that if higher incomes spread into spending and service prices, inflation pressure could be stronger than expected. It said it would decide on another increase next month after looking at second-quarter output and income data and July inflation. The gap between Korean and U.S. benchmark rates has narrowed to 1.00 percentage point at the top of the range.

### 3. Homeplus closes all its stores

- **Date**: Jul 13–16 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On July 13, Homeplus temporarily closed all 67 of its remaining large supermarkets and its headquarters. Homeplus said, “Our operating funds have run out completely, and we cannot pay for goods or even cover the operating costs to keep stores open.” When the Seoul Bankruptcy Court decided on the 3rd to end its rehabilitation process, it said it could review the decision again if Homeplus secured 200 billion won in emergency operating funds by the 20th, the deadline for an immediate appeal. Its largest shareholder, MBK Partners, said it would guarantee only up to 100 billion won, while the largest creditor, Meritz Financial Group, said support of more than 100 billion won would be difficult.

  On the 15th, the two companies reached an agreement. Meritz Fire & Marine Insurance, Meritz Securities and Meritz Capital each held board meetings on the 16th and approved a loan of 200 billion won, and MBK Chairman Kim Byung-ju agreed to guarantee the full amount. Homeplus said it would file an immediate appeal with the court on the 20th and, if the rehabilitation process is extended, would set a schedule for reopening after talks with its suppliers.
- **Why it matters**: Without the funding, Homeplus would very likely have gone into bankruptcy. In May, Homeplus closed 37 of its 104 stores. Even if the court accepts the appeal, Homeplus must finalize a revised rehabilitation plan by next month.

### 5. Court rejects arrest of ex-prosecutor general Shim Woo-jung

- **Date**: Jul 16 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On July 16, Bu Dong-sik, a senior warrant judge at the Seoul Central District Court, rejected an arrest warrant for former Prosecutor General Shim Woo-jung. The court said, “The explanation of the risk of destroying evidence is insufficient,” and that it was hard to see a risk of flight. A warrant for Jeon Moo-gon, former head of planning and coordination at the Supreme Prosecutors’ Office, who faces the same charges, was also rejected. He is also accused of abuse of power for having the prosecution’s special investigation headquarters give up an immediate appeal when a court canceled former President Yoon Suk Yeol’s detention in March last year. When the Seoul Central District Court sentenced Park to 25 years in prison on June 22, it noted in the ruling the claims that Shim took part.
- **Why it matters**: The comprehensive special counsel has failed again and again to take key suspects into custody, with its investigation period ending on the 24th. The National Assembly is discussing a bill to extend the special counsel’s investigation period.

The blockade started again at 4 p.m. (U.S. Eastern time) on the 14th. On the 13th, the price of Brent crude rose more than 9%.

  The U.S. military struck Iran every night during this period, and on the 17th it hit bridges and power facilities in Hormozgan Province and the Iranshahr airport. On the 18th, Iran’s health ministry said U.S. strikes had killed at least 50 people and injured more than 500 since July 6. It was the first time since March that U.S. troops were killed by an Iranian attack. The U.S. military immediately launched retaliatory strikes. On the same day, Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the U.S. had broken the Islamabad memorandum of understanding and that Iran had also stopped carrying out all its commitments.
- **Why it matters**: As of the 14th, nearly half of the 60-day negotiating period under the memorandum signed in June had passed with no progress on passage through the strait or on the nuclear program. ### 4. Canada fire smoke puts 100 million Americans on air alert

- **Date**: Jul 15–18 (Wed–Sat)
- **What happened**: Smoke from wildfires in northern Ontario, Canada, and northern Minnesota spread across eastern North America. On July 15, Toronto’s air quality was ranked the worst in the world, and the temperature in downtown Toronto hit 37.3°C, breaking a record from more than 30 years ago. ### 5. Hungary changes constitution to end president’s term

- **Date**: Jul 13–18 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: On July 13, Hungary’s parliament passed the 17th amendment to the constitution, which ends the president’s term on the grounds of a “serious loss of trust.” The amendment bars lawmakers who have served three terms from running again and sets a retirement age of 70 for constitutional court judges. As a result, more than half of the lawmakers of Fidesz, the party of former Prime Minister Viktor Orbán, cannot run in the next election, and the head of the constitutional court will also have to step down.

  President Tamás Sulyok signed the amendment on the 18th, the deadline for signing, and agreed to step down. His term ends at midnight on the 19th, and Speaker of Parliament Ágnes Forsthoffer will act as president from the 20th. Sulyok criticized the amendment as a turning point for constitutional democracy and said it trampled on the rule of law. Orbán said tyranny was no longer a threat but a reality.
- **Why it matters**: The new government is using constitutional changes to remove people Fidesz had placed in state institutions. Prime Minister Péter Magyar’s Tisza party won a two-thirds majority in the April general election and removed Orbán after 16 years in power. Sulyok was elected president in 2024 by Fidesz lawmakers.
- **Sources**: [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cpd7q7eev7po) / [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/18/hungarys-president-agrees-to-stand-down-after-law-change-ends-his-term)

### 3. Thinking Machines Lab releases its first model, Inkling

- **Date**: Jul 15 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On July 15, Thinking Machines Lab, founded by former OpenAI Chief Technology Officer Mira Murati, released its first model, Inkling, along with its weights. It is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with 975 billion total parameters, 41 billion of which are used at a time. It was trained from scratch on 45 trillion tokens of text, images, audio and video, and supports a context of up to 1 million tokens. - **Why it matters**: It is Thinking Machines Lab’s first model of its own, a year and a half after the company started. The product tests the company’s claim that models adapted by organizations themselves beat general-purpose models made by one company. Earlier, on July 12, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella warned that companies using closed models also hand over their business knowledge.

### 4. New York State halts new permits for large data centers

- **Date**: Jul 14 (Tue)
- **What happened**: On July 14, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order halting new permits for data centers of 50 megawatts or more. New York is the first U.S. state to actually stop data center construction. The state Department of Environmental Conservation will not issue permits that have not yet been granted, which could affect about 10 projects. The halt will last until the state sets up an environmental review process for data centers, which Hochul expects to take about a year.

  Hochul said progress should not come with higher electricity bills, drained water supplies or noise pollution, and that data centers would not be exempt from local permits. The state government is also considering making data centers pay into a power grid fund and blocking tax breaks for very large data centers.
- **Why it matters**: Pauses on data centers had been discussed at the state and federal levels, but no one had actually put one in place. A pause bill passed by the Maine legislature was vetoed by the governor. A stronger bill that would halt projects of 20 megawatts or more for a year is before the New York State legislature.

### 5. Suno found to have scraped YouTube music on a large scale
