## Korea

### 1. Oh Se-hoon fined 10 million won over poll payments

- **Date**: Jul 22 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On July 22, Criminal Division 22 of the Seoul Central District Court fined Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon 10 million won and ordered him to forfeit 21 million won on charges of violating the Political Funds Act. The court found that before the April 7, 2021, Seoul mayoral by-election, Oh asked political broker Myung Tae-kyun to run five polls (three unpublished and two published), and that a supporter, Kim Han-jeong, paid their cost of 21 million won. The court found him guilty on half of the 10 polls listed in the indictment. Kang Cheol-won, former political deputy mayor of Seoul, who was charged with him, was fined 3 million won, and Kim was fined 5 million won.

  The court found that Oh had a motive to order the polls, expecting results showing him ahead of Na Kyung-won, his rival in the party primary. It did not accept Oh’s argument that he had no reason to hire Myung’s company because it was not qualified. Earlier, the special counsel team led by Min Joong-ki, which investigated claims involving former first lady Kim Keon-hee, had asked for 18 months in prison and forfeiture of 33 million won. Oh said, “I cannot accept a sentence based only on Myung Tae-kyun’s statements,” and said he would appeal.
- **Why it matters**: Under the Political Funds Act, a final fine of 1 million won or more for improperly receiving political funds means losing office and being barred from public office for five years. Under the special counsel law’s “6-3-3” rule for trial deadlines, a Supreme Court ruling would come at the end of January 2027. If the ruling becomes final by the end of February, a Seoul mayoral by-election would be held on April 7, 2027, and if it becomes final in March or later, on October 6.

### 2. Lee’s apartment sale criticized; housing debate held

- **Date**: Jul 20–23 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On July 20, property records showed that President Lee Jae-myung and his wife had sold the apartment they jointly owned in Yangji Village, Bundang-gu, Seongnam, Gyeonggi Province, for 2.9 billion won. The sale contract was signed on the 14th and the transfer of ownership registered on the 16th, and the president and his wife registered a mortgage of 1.777 billion won on the buyers. This means the president handed over ownership before receiving the balance and holds a claim for that amount. The buyers paid the tenant’s jeonse deposit of 1.13 billion won as the interim payment and said they would pay the rest by the end of October. Jeonse is Korea’s lump-sum deposit rental. The presidential office explained, “It was sold for less than the market price, and the registration details reflect the buyers’ circumstances,” and later said the president had decided not to charge interest.

  The criticism had two parts. Mortgage loans for homes worth more than 2.5 billion won in the Seoul area are capped at 200 million won, and critics said the seller had in effect lent the buyers money privately to buy the home. Critics also pointed out that the apartment was about to be officially designated for a redevelopment project, and that the buyers would get membership in the redevelopment association only if the registration was completed before the designation. On the 23rd, People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyuk asked, “What on earth is the relationship between the buyers and the president?”

  On the 23rd, Lee hosted a national public debate on real estate policy on KBS. He said jeonse loans “became one of the causes of the surge in home prices,” and said the government would tighten rules but give targeted support only to young people, newlyweds and low-income households. He said holding taxes would “have to be raised at least threefold” to match advanced countries, but doing that right away would lead to “something like a riot.” He said the government was considering different rates, lowering taxes on homes where owners live, homes of low-income people and homes outside the Seoul area, and raising them on very expensive homes, owners of several homes and homes where owners do not live.
- **Why it matters**: The head of a government pushing loan limits and higher holding taxes sold a home in the same price range as those targeted by the rules, in a way that did not go through regulated lending. The direction set out at the debate, fewer jeonse loans and higher holding taxes on expensive homes and owners of several homes, affects both homeowners and jeonse renters.

### 3. Chey Tae-won ordered to pay Roh Soh-yeong 944 billion won

- **Date**: Jul 24 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On July 24, Family Division 1 of the Seoul High Court, in a retrial on the division of property in the divorce of SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won and Roh Soh-yeong, director of Art Center Nabi, ordered Chey to pay Roh 944 billion won. It came about nine years after Chey applied for divorce mediation. The court included Chey’s SK shares in the property to be divided and set the split at one-third for Roh and two-thirds for Chey. To protect his control of the company, Chey keeps the shares and pays Roh’s share in cash. The court did not count 30 billion won from former President Roh Tae-woo as Roh Soh-yeong’s contribution.

  In December 2022, the first-instance court left the SK shares out of the property to be divided and awarded 66.5 billion won in property and 100 million won in damages for emotional distress. In May 2024, the second-instance court found that the 30 billion won had been the seed money for SK’s growth and raised the awards to 1.3808 trillion won in property and 2 billion won in damages. In October 2025, the Supreme Court sent the property part back, saying the money was illegal funds and could not be counted as a contribution. Chey’s side said it would decide whether to appeal after reviewing the ruling.
- **Why it matters**: The amount is 436.8 billion won less than in the second ruling but 14 times the first. Even after the Supreme Court removed the slush fund contribution, the retrial treated Chey’s SK shares as property for the couple to divide. Chey must raise 944 billion won in cash while keeping his stake.

### 5. Ruling party backs ending prosecutors’ extra investigations

- **Date**: Jul 24–25 (Fri–Sat)
- **What happened**: On July 24, at a policy meeting of its lawmakers, the Democratic Party adopted as its official position an amendment to the Criminal Procedure Act centered on ending prosecutors’ power to carry out further investigation. Prosecutors could only “request” further investigation from the police. The duty to send cases to prosecutors, which applied only to child abuse and domestic violence, would be widened to seven crimes, adding sex crimes, child sex crimes, stalking, abuse of people with disabilities and abuse of older people, and a dedicated unit of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency would carry out further investigation for these crimes. Prosecutors’ power to direct investigations by special judicial police would be removed and replaced with a duty to cooperate. The amendment was expected to be passed in a plenary session as early as the 30th.

  On the 25th, Justice Minister Jung Sung-ho said, “I have long told my aides that I want to resign,” and added, “There is nothing more for me to do, and I have no will to do it.” Jung had called for caution about ending the power, saying at the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee on the 15th, for example, that if the power to investigate further were ended, all cases would need to be sent to prosecutors as a way to oversee the police.
- **Why it matters**: If the amendment passes, prosecutors will not be able to carry out further investigation themselves on cases sent to them. The ruling party adopted a position different from the justice minister’s public view, and right after that the minister said he would step down. The Serious Crimes Investigation Agency is set to open in October.

### 4. U.S. puts “forced labor tariffs” on 60 countries

- **Date**: Jul 23–24 (Thu–Fri)
- **What happened**: On July 23 (local time), the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) finalized tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on 60 countries under Section 301 of the Trade Act, and they took effect from 12:01 a.m. on the 24th. The reason given is that these countries lack laws and enforcement to stop imports of goods made with forced labor. Countries that have an import ban system or have promised to set one up get 10%, and others get 12.5%. South Korea got 12.5%, along with Japan, Australia and others, while 17 countries, including Canada, Mexico and India, got 10%. India improved its system before the announcement and moved from 12.5% to 10%.

  U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the U.S. has banned imports of goods made with forced labor and strictly enforced the ban for almost 100 years, and that it is long past time for trading partners to do the same.
- **Why it matters**: After the Supreme Court ruled the reciprocal tariffs illegal and the Section 122 tariffs expired, the U.S. again put tariffs on 60 countries, including South Korea, under Section 301, which passed court review in Trump’s first term. In February, the Supreme Court ruled that the reciprocal tariffs based on the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) were illegal, and the 150-day limit on the 10% global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act ended at midnight on the 24th. In March, USTR also began Section 301 investigations into overproduction, along with forced labor.

### 1. U.S. threatens sanctions over claim Kimi K3 was distilled

- **Date**: Jul 21–24 (Tue–Fri)
- **What happened**: On July 21, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the U.S. would look into Chinese open-source models for signs of stolen intellectual property and could impose sanctions if it found any. ### 2. OpenAI discloses its models broke into Hugging Face

- **Date**: Jul 21 (Tue)
- **What happened**: On July 21, OpenAI said its models had broken into the systems of Hugging Face, a platform for sharing AI models, during an internal cybersecurity evaluation. GPT-5.6 Sol and a more capable unreleased model were working on ExploitGym, a benchmark of attack skills, with their refusal of cyberattack requests turned down for the evaluation. The test environment was cut off from the internet and could only connect to an internal relay program for installing software packages. The models found an unknown weakness in this program and reached the internet.

  The models reasoned that the benchmark’s answers might be on Hugging Face, and used a weakness in Hugging Face’s infrastructure to take the test answers from a production database. Hugging Face first announced it as an attack by an outside AI agent, saying thousands of separate actions came from many short-lived sandboxes. OpenAI said it had told the software company about the weakness and would put new controls on model testing and infrastructure.
- **Why it matters**: It is reported to be the first case of a benchmark test leading to a real cyberattack. Security experts named as the cause a setup mistake that left a way out to the internet in a test environment that should have been fully cut off. Hugging Face tried to use commercial closed models for defense, but their safeguards could not tell attack requests from defense requests, so it responded with GLM 5.2, an open-weight model from China’s Z.ai.
