## Korea

### 1. National Assembly passes 26.2 trillion won ‘war budget’

- **Date**: Apr 10–11 (Fri–Sat)
- **What happened**: On the night of April 10, the National Assembly passed a 26.2 trillion won supplementary budget to help with damage from the war in the Middle East. Of the 244 members present, 214 voted in favor, 11 against and 19 abstained. It came 10 days after the government sent the budget to the National Assembly. The ruling and opposition parties agreed that day to keep the total as the government proposed, while cutting and adding about 790 billion won each in specific programs. Funding for a temporary 50% discount on the K-Pass, a public transit fare refund card, rose by 100 billion won, and support for naphtha supply rose by 200 billion won.

  At the center of the budget are 4.8 trillion won in relief payments for damage from high oil prices. They give 100,000 to 600,000 won per person to 32.56 million people in the bottom 70% by income. During the review, the People Power Party called this “a vote-buying budget for the local elections” and demanded cuts, but it accepted the government’s version in negotiations. The 4.2 trillion won for the oil price cap, which makes up refiners’ losses, was also kept as the government proposed.

  On the 11th, the government announced its payment plan. Basic livelihood recipients get 550,000 won, and low-income households just above that level and single-parent families get 450,000 won, with 50,000 won more for people living outside the Seoul metropolitan area or in areas with shrinking populations. They apply first, from April 27 to May 8, and everyone else eligible gets 100,000 to 250,000 won from May 18, depending on where they live. Payments not used by August 31 expire.
- **Why it matters**: The decision to use state funds to share with households the burden of fuel prices pushed up by the war in the Middle East is now final. In the same week, from the 8th, a rule limiting cars to four days in five based on license plate numbers began at paid public parking lots run by public institutions. So the government’s response to high oil prices was carried out on two tracks at once: payments and cutting demand.

### 3. 26 Korean ships stuck in the Strait of Hormuz

- **Date**: Apr 6–9 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: Twenty-six Korean-flagged ships with 173 crew members are stuck inside the Strait of Hormuz. Even after the two-week ceasefire between the U.S. and Iran, there was no news as of April 9 of any Korea-linked ship getting permission from Iran to pass. ### 5. Labor Day becomes a public holiday after 63 years

- **Date**: Apr 6 (Mon)
- **What happened**: On April 6, the Cabinet approved the promulgation of an amendment to the Act on Public Holidays that makes Labor Day a public holiday. From May 1 this year, all workers can take the day off, whatever their type of employment and whether or not they count as workers under the Labor Standards Act.

  Labor Day has been marked since 1923, but its name was changed by a 1963 law establishing Workers’ Day. It became a paid holiday by law in 1994, but only for workers covered by the Labor Standards Act, so civil servants, teachers and workers in special types of employment, such as delivery drivers, could not take the day off. The government changed the name back to Labor Day last year, and the Ministry of Personnel Management plans to revise the rules on public office holidays as well.
- **Why it matters**: The split in which some people had the day off and others did not, depending on how they work, ended 63 years after the 1963 law.

### 1. U.S.–Iran two-week ceasefire; Islamabad talks fail

- **Date**: Apr 7–12 (Tue–Sun)
- **What happened**: On the night of April 7 (U.S. Eastern Time), the U.S. and Iran agreed to a two-week ceasefire, a little over an hour before the deadline U.S. President Donald Trump had set for destroying Iran’s power plants and bridges. Earlier that day, Trump had warned that if Iran did not meet his demands, a whole civilization would die that night. Iran’s Supreme National Security Council accepted the ceasefire on the condition that attacks stop, and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said ships could pass through the Strait of Hormuz for two weeks if they coordinated with the Iranian military.

  Pakistan, which brokered the ceasefire, said Lebanon was included, but Israel and the White House said Lebanon was not. Iran’s 10-point plan included passage through the strait in coordination with the Iranian military, the withdrawal of U.S. forces from the region, and the lifting of sanctions and compensation.

  A U.S. delegation led by Vice President JD Vance and an Iranian delegation led by Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf held 21 hours of face-to-face talks in Islamabad, Pakistan, starting on the 11th. But the talks ended without a deal on the morning of the 12th. Vance said Iran had refused to commit to not seeking nuclear weapons. Trump immediately announced that the U.S. Navy would blockade all ships going in and out of the strait and stop ships that had paid transit fees to Iran. U.S. Central Command said the blockade would begin at 10 a.m. (U.S. Eastern Time) on the 13th, and Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it would treat warships approaching the strait as a violation of the ceasefire.
- **Why it matters**: For the first time since the war began, both sides stopped attacking and sat down face to face, but they split over the nuclear issue, and a naval blockade emerged as a new means of pressure instead of talks. Right after the blockade was announced, the price of U.S. crude oil rose 8% to $104.24 a barrel.

### 3. Hungary election ends Orbán’s 16 years in power

- **Date**: Apr 12 (Sun)
- **What happened**: In Hungary’s general election on April 12, Tisza, a center-right, pro-European party led by Péter Magyar, was projected to win 138 of the 199 seats. A sitting prime minister whom the U.S. vice president campaigned for in person lost by a wide margin.

### 4. Artemis II returns after crew’s record-distance flight

- **Date**: Apr 6–10 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On April 6, NASA’s Orion spacecraft, carrying the four Artemis II crew members, went around the far side of the Moon and reached a point 252,756 miles (about 406,770 km) from Earth. That is 4,111 miles farther than the Apollo 13 record from 1970, and the farthest humans have ever traveled. Its closest approach to the Moon’s surface was about 4,067 miles. The crew observed about 35 sites on the Moon’s surface with their own eyes and watched a solar eclipse.

  The spacecraft splashed down in the Pacific Ocean off San Diego on the 10th (local time), and all four crew members were healthy. The flight lasted nine days, one hour and 32 minutes and covered 694,481 miles. On board were Commander Reid Wiseman, pilot Victor Glover, NASA astronaut Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.
- **Why it matters**: It was the first time people had gone to the Moon and back since Apollo 17 in December 1972. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said NASA would begin preparing the next mission, Artemis III, with the goal of landing on the Moon and building a base in 2028.

### 5. Russia and Ukraine hold 32-hour Easter ceasefire

- **Date**: Apr 9–11 (Thu–Sat)
- **What happened**: On April 9, Russian President Vladimir Putin declared a 32-hour ceasefire for Orthodox Easter, from 4 p.m. on the 11th to midnight on the 12th. It came after Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky proposed, through the U.S. earlier that week, that the two sides not attack each other’s energy facilities over Easter.

  On the 11th, when the ceasefire began, the two countries exchanged 175 prisoners each. Ukraine also got back seven civilians and said 163 of the soldiers who returned had been held since 2022. Russia’s defense ministry said the United Arab Emirates had mediated.

  Within hours of the ceasefire, each side accused the other of breaking it. Ukraine’s military said it recorded 469 violations on the 11th alone, and the governor of Russia’s Kursk region said a Ukrainian drone hit a gas station, injuring three people, including a baby.
- **Why it matters**: Three hundred fifty prisoners were actually exchanged, but the ceasefire itself was a 32-hour declaration by one side and did not lead to progress in talks to end the war. Russia has in effect rejected the 30-day unconditional ceasefire the U.S. and Ukraine proposed last year, and the U.S.-brokered talks have made no progress on the key issues. As with last year’s 30-hour Easter truce, claims of violations came from the first day.

### 1. Anthropic gives Mythos Preview only to security partners

- **Date**: Apr 7–8 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: On April 7, Anthropic announced that it would not release its new frontier model, Claude Mythos Preview, to the public and would instead offer it only to organizations taking part in Project Glasswing, a cyber defense project. It will be used by 12 partners, including Amazon, Apple, Broadcom, Cisco, CrowdStrike, the Linux Foundation, Microsoft, Palo Alto Networks and JPMorgan Chase, and by more than 40 organizations that build or maintain software infrastructure.

  Anthropic said that over the past few weeks the model had found thousands of serious vulnerabilities in every major operating system and web browser, and had even written attack code without human direction. Some of the vulnerabilities had survived decades of human review. Anthropic said it would provide up to $100 million in usage credits and $4 million in donations to open-source security groups. The model’s existence first became known through a data leak at Anthropic in March.

  Anthropic said it had briefed senior U.S. government officials on the model’s offensive and defensive abilities. The next day, the 8th, a federal appeals court in Washington turned down Anthropic’s request to pause the Defense Department’s labeling of the company as a supply chain risk. Anthropic remains shut out of new Defense Department contracts and systems.
- **Why it matters**: It is a case of an AI company itself saying it will not release a new model to the public because of what the model can do. Anthropic said such abilities will soon spread to people who will not use them safely, and that it limited access so that defenders could use them first. In the same week, Anthropic told the U.S. government about these abilities while continuing its legal fight with the Defense Department.
