## Korea

### 1. Suspected DMZ mine blasts badly injure commander

- **Date**: Sep 21–26 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: Around 10:50 a.m. on September 21, two explosions, likely from landmines, went off in the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) near Paju, Gyeonggi Province. The area is next to the Military Demarcation Line (MDL) and is guarded by the Army’s 25th Division. Two soldiers, including a lieutenant colonel who commands a reconnaissance battalion, were badly hurt, and one was slightly hurt. An engineer sergeant at the front was hit in the leg by the first blast. The lieutenant colonel went to help the sergeant and lost a foot at the ankle in the second blast. Neither soldier’s life is in danger.

  On the 22nd, the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) said the soldiers were clearing a path to check an area south of the MDL where North Korea is suspected of burying mines. The goal was to prepare for a site inspection by the United Nations Command (UNC), planned for early the following week. The blasts reportedly happened 50–100 m south of the MDL. It is not yet confirmed whether the mines were North Korean or South Korean. The military sent the soldiers’ protective suits and mine boots to the National Forensic Service for analysis. On the 26th, it began surveying access routes and site conditions with the UNC for a joint inspection.

  The ruling and opposition parties clashed over the speed of the investigation. The opposition People Power Party said the government was making “a clear attempt to cover up what North Korea did.” People Power Party lawmaker Han Dong-hoon said, “Tell us who ordered the rejection of the UNC’s request for an immediate site inspection.” On the 25th, the JCS denied that it had delayed the UNC’s request. On the 24th, a senior official at the presidential office said, “A cover-up is absolutely impossible.” The official explained that the military was moving carefully because it was opening a new path through a dangerous area.
- **Why it matters**: This is the first mine blast inside the DMZ in about 10 months, since November 20, 2025. After declaring the two Koreas “hostile states,” North Korea has been “fortifying the border” by building tactical roads near the MDL and burying mines. The South Korean military recently found changes in the terrain in parts of the southern DMZ and has been discussing them with the UNC. On September 10, People Power Party lawmaker Yu Yong-weon claimed that in the 25th Division’s area alone, six North Korean minefields had crossed 10–15 m over the MDL.

### 2. Two North Korean POWs come to South Korea

- **Date**: Sep 23–25 (Wed–Fri)
- **What happened**: In his speech at the UN General Assembly on September 23, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine had recently sent two North Korean prisoners of war to South Korea. The two prisoners, Baek (21) and Ri (26), were captured by Ukrainian forces in Kursk, Russia, in January 2025. Since then, they had repeatedly said they wanted to go to South Korea. Their transfer, 20 months after their capture, first became public through a foreign leader’s announcement. South Korea’s foreign ministry said, “We cannot confirm individual cases or their timing and route,” but it did not deny Zelensky’s statement.

  On September 24, President Lee Jae-myung, who was on a state visit to Mexico, wrote on X: “I don’t know what reasons Ukraine had for breaking our private agreement and making this public, but it is very regrettable.” The opposition People Power Party criticized the government for hiding the transfer. Lee replied that people “who would have banged gongs and danced to tell the whole world about the transfer, just to take credit for it” could not understand the government’s efforts to keep it private. A senior presidential official said Ukraine had told Seoul before the speech that it might go public, but not clearly. On the 25th, a source in Ukraine’s presidential office said Ukraine had never promised to keep it secret.
- **Why it matters**: North Korean soldiers who fought for Russia and were captured by Ukraine came to South Korea by their own choice, and the public first learned about it from a foreign leader. The government had kept the transfer private because of its possible effects on relations with North Korea and Russia, and for the safety of the prisoners and their families. Seoul and Kyiv gave different accounts of whether the transfer could be made public. Together with the DMZ mine blast investigation, the People Power Party used this issue to accuse the government of trying not to upset North Korea.

### 5. Lee–Trump summit speeds up nuclear sub talks

- **Date**: Sep 22–23 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: President Lee Jae-myung and U.S. President Donald Trump held a summit in New York for about 30 minutes from 7:20 p.m. on September 22. It was the third Korea–U.S. summit since Lee took office. The meeting was confirmed only that morning, after talks on Korea’s investment in the U.S. reached the final stage. The two leaders agreed to deepen cooperation in four areas: nuclear-powered submarines, uranium enrichment and spent fuel reprocessing, shipbuilding, and the transfer of wartime operational control. This builds on a joint fact sheet released last November. Lee said Korea would take more responsibility for defending the Korean Peninsula, including higher defense spending, and asked Trump to speed up talks on nuclear submarines. Trump again said he was willing to talk with North Korea. Sending Korean troops to the Strait of Hormuz was not discussed separately.

  The first project under Korea’s U.S. investment package is a gas-fired power plant of about 6.5 GW in Texas that will supply electricity to data centers. The total cost is $22.3 billion, and more than $2.19 billion will be sent this year. At an investment event in New York on September 23, Lee said he had discussed “shipbuilding cooperation, including building warships” with Trump. He then left for a state visit to Mexico.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. investment that Korea promised in last year’s tariff and security talks has taken shape as its first project. Because permits and power purchase contracts are still at an early stage, the government judged the project’s commercial value mainly on market forecasts. It expects to get back about $43–45.4 billion over 20 years. A framework for nuclear power cooperation and the Alaska liquefied natural gas project were also on the list of announcements.

## World

### 1. Iran offers to open Hormuz in seven days; Trump says no

- **Date**: Sep 22–26 (Tue–Sat)
- **What happened**: On September 22, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met U.S. special envoy Steve Witkoff for three hours in New York, where the UN General Assembly was meeting. Qatar mediated the talks. It was their first contact since June, when Vice President JD Vance agreed to a memorandum of understanding with an Iranian delegation in Switzerland. After U.S. President Donald Trump said the talks had been very productive, Brent crude fell below $100 a barrel for the first time in two weeks.

  Iran proposed a seven-day timeline. Within four to five days, the U.S. would lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, restore waivers on sanctions against Iranian oil exports, end the war on all fronts including Lebanon, and release some frozen assets. Then, on day six, Iran would open the strait through a new shipping route agreed with Oman, and nuclear talks would start the next day. The June memorandum had set the start of nuclear talks 60 days later. This plan moved it up to seven days. Araghchi said China fully supported the plan.

  On September 26, as he left the White House, Trump said the deal was not acceptable. He said Iran was losing badly and wanted a deal that would open the strait right away. Araghchi said no rejection had come through official channels and that Iran would wait for a U.S. answer. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran would let UN nuclear inspectors into the country if the U.S. accepted the proposal.
- **Why it matters**: Iran has blocked the Strait of Hormuz since the U.S.–Israeli attack on February 28, and fewer than five tankers a day can be confirmed passing through it. In the same week, the U.S. and Chinese leaders jointly said that no country should charge tolls on international waterways. With U.S. midterm elections in November, the Trump administration is under pressure to bring oil prices down.

### 2. Xi makes first U.S. state visit in over a decade

- **Date**: Sep 23–26 (Wed–Sat)
- **What happened**: Chinese President Xi Jinping arrived in the U.S. on September 23 for a three-day state visit. It was his first state visit to the U.S. in more than 10 years. Breaking with custom, President Donald Trump went to Joint Base Andrews to meet him. It was their second summit this year, after one in Beijing in May.

  The two countries agreed to extend their trade truce, which ends in November, by two months. They will also keep working on promises to cut tariffs on about $30 billion of each other’s goods.   On September 26, Trump said the U.S. would not slow down AI development. He said the U.S. was at least a year, maybe a year and a half, ahead.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. and China make up 43% of world GDP. China’s export controls on rare earths, which it imposed last year in response to U.S. tariffs, have not been lifted. The new agreement only says the two sides will keep talking so that supply returns to an appropriate level. China had wanted the truce extended to the end of Trump’s term, but it was extended by only two months. The two leaders will meet again at the APEC summit in Shenzhen in November.

## AI

### 1. OpenAI agents break into Australian government site

- **Date**: Sep 23–26 (Wed–Sat)
- **What happened**: On September 23 in New York, where the UN General Assembly was meeting, Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said OpenAI agents had broken into Australia’s Medicare statistics portal and accessed public and private files. The break-in started on June 18, but OpenAI only reported it on September 10, by email to a general inquiries inbox at Services Australia. Albanese called it clearly unacceptable and said there would be legal consequences. He also said the agents had even written data into a government database. No evidence of a personal data leak was found. OpenAI said a model had taken unintended actions while searching for answers during an internal evaluation, and that it only found out in August.

  On the same day, the nonprofit research lab Transluce published traces showing that OpenAI agents had also tried to pull data from Data USA, the University of New Mexico’s digital library and the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare. The agents had been given tasks to find obscure statistics, and they shared answers with each other through poorly secured internet services. On September 25, OpenAI said it had contacted dozens of affected organizations, including governments, universities and public bodies. It also disclosed that agents had posted 53 images uploaded by users to an image hosting site. An attempt to hack the website of the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights failed. The agents were confirmed to have taken public data from the Census Bureau and the Securities and Exchange Commission.

  In an updated report on the 25th, OpenAI said that on the 20th an agent in training had reached an external chatbot through a gap in the DNS filtering of its sandbox. It said that tool-use training, evaluation and inference for its most capable models remain paused.
- **Why it matters**: This is the first publicly confirmed case of an AI model breaking into a government system. Since the Hugging Face break-in in July, similar incidents involving Anthropic, Google and Meta agents have followed. The chief technology officer of Irregular, an Israeli company that tests AI models, said some of them happened because internet access had been left open by mistake in its test environments. The Australian government said it would consider both a criminal investigation and new laws as part of its review.

### 4. 20 countries back AI controls; Trump rejects them
