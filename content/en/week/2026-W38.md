## Korea

### 1. Justice minister nominee Kim Seung-won withdraws

- **Date**: Sep 15–19 (Tue–Sat)
- **What happened**: On September 19, Kim Seung-won, the nominee for justice minister, held an emergency press conference at the National Assembly and withdrew, 20 days after being named. “Watching the president’s press conference yesterday, I made up my mind,” Kim said. “I take seriously that I did not meet the public’s expectations.” Kim added, “I will reveal the full truth about the targeted investigation by the politicized prosecutors of Yoon Suk Yeol and Han Dong-hoon, Han’s illegal leak of investigation secrets, and the doctored evidence.”

  On the morning of the 17th, two days before the withdrawal, a petition from former aides reached the presidential office, including Hong Ihk-pyo, the senior secretary for political affairs. It included a past sexual harassment claim and how aides covered it up, another drunk-driving claim, claims of habitual swearing and verbal abuse at aides after drinking, and claims of other favors beyond lobbying for a new drug. On the day of the withdrawal, Kim’s side said, “We were not contacted by the presidential office. We do not even know if it was submitted.” But on the afternoon of the 18th, the day before, Kim met the former aides who sent the petition on the outskirts of Suwon, Gyeonggi Province. On the sexual harassment claim, Kim said in effect, “I did not harass anyone, but there was such a misunderstanding, and the aides handled it well.” On the drunk driving and verbal abuse claims, Kim said, “I don’t remember,” and said there was no plan to step down.

  Earlier, the confirmation hearing on the 15th ran until midnight without witnesses or expert speakers, as the parties clashed over claims that Kim had asked for favors in the approval of a clinical trial for a COVID-19 treatment. Of 376 documents the People Power Party requested, 64.6% were not submitted. Kim said, “I also have no plan to (drop the prosecution) through general investigative orders via the prosecutor general.” On the 14th, Ministry of Food and Drug Safety documents showed that the treatment had been given to 93 people in a 2022 phase 2 trial, while animal test results showing hamsters vomiting blood and dying had been left out. On the 16th, the civic group People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy called Kim “unfit,” citing a drunk-driving record and a child’s false address registration. In a Korea Society Opinion Institute survey of 1,001 adults on the 14th and 15th, 52.1% opposed the appointment and 25.1% supported it.
- **Why it matters**: This is the second withdrawal among the nominees in the Lee Jae-myung government’s second cabinet reshuffle, after Yong Hye-in, the nominee for minister of gender equality and family, withdrew on September 13. With Kang Sun-woo and Yong Hye-in before, three sitting lawmakers have now withdrawn as minister nominees under this government. The petition that reached the presidential office two days before the withdrawal contained claims that the hearing did not cover.

### 2. President Lee rules out a second term

- **Date**: Sep 14–18 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: In a Realmeter survey of 2,515 adults aged 18 and over from September 7 to 11, approval of President Lee Jae-myung’s performance fell 3.6 percentage points from the week before to 33.8%. It was the ninth weekly drop in a row and the lowest since Lee took office. Disapproval was 63.3%. In party support, the opposition People Power Party led with 42.1%, ahead of the ruling Democratic Party at 36.1%. Earlier, in a Gallup Korea poll released on September 11, approval was 38%, 29 percentage points lower than the peak of 67% in the fourth week of April, five months earlier.

  On the 18th, Lee held the seventh press conference since taking office, for 99 minutes at the guest house of the presidential office. “A second term for me through a constitutional amendment is impossible under the Constitution,” Lee said. “I have no intention of serving a second term at all.” Lee called the 1987 Constitution “clothes that no longer fit today’s Korea” and said he would work for constitutional change, including allowing presidents to serve a second term. On whether to appoint justice minister nominee Kim Seung-won, Lee did not decide, saying, “The more appointments I make, the harder they get.”

  On the economy, Lee said of the introduction of single-stock leveraged ETFs, “It seems true that there were shortcomings,” but added, “I have not yet looked into the detailed process of how it was decided.” On real estate, Lee said, “Ending the republic of speculation is this government’s central task,” and promised to increase housing supply as quickly as possible.
- **Why it matters**: By Gallup Korea’s count, approval fell below 40% two months into office for former President Yoon Suk Yeol. It took one year and 10 months for former President Park Geun-hye and two years and five months for former President Moon Jae-in. For Lee, it took one year and three months. Civic groups welcomed Lee’s statement that he would not seek a second term and urged the National Assembly to begin talks on amending the Constitution. The next day, Kim Seung-won named this press conference as the reason for deciding to withdraw.

### 3. Governor Choo Mi-ae under fire over official car and home

- **Date**: Sep 14–17 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On September 15, it became known that Governor Choo Mi-ae, who had declared a financial emergency, had bought a Hyundai IONIQ 9 as an official car on June 29, just before taking office. The car cost 77.65 million won and was bought without bidding. Gyeonggi Province’s rules say official cars should be replaced after eight years of use or 120,000 km. The existing official car, a Carnival, had more than four years of use left. Earlier, on September 5, it became known that the province had rented an apartment worth about 1.2 billion won on a jeonse lease, Korea’s lump-sum deposit rental, as the governor’s official residence.

  At the same time, welfare programs were cut. Gyeonggi Province did not set aside 8 billion won for postnatal care support for October to December, and cities and counties announced that support would end for babies registered from October. Since 2019, the province and the cities and counties had each paid half of this program, which gave families with a new baby 500,000 won in local currency. Hwaseong City planned to pay the province’s share from its own budget and make the payments retroactively.

  A petition calling for Choo to resign, posted on the 11th, passed 10,000 signatures at about 5:30 p.m. on the 14th. About two hours later, a written briefing from the spokesperson came out. The province treated this briefing as its official answer and closed the petition at 30,775 signatures. For two earlier petitions, the province took one month to one and a half months to answer after they passed the threshold. On the 15th, the civic group Citizens’ Action for Upholding Judicial Justice filed a complaint against Choo with the Gyeonggi Southern Provincial Police Agency for abuse of power and breach of trust. At an event at the provincial office on the 15th, Choo said, “The treasury has been used up and there is a pile of debt papers,” and added, “I, Choo Mi-ae, will not be driven out.”
- **Why it matters**: A governor who declared a financial emergency and cut postnatal care support was found to have spent money on an official car and residence at the same time, which led to a criminal complaint. On the 15th, Kim Dong-yeon, the former governor, pushed back, saying, “There is no financial crisis now.” Kim said Gyeonggi Province’s debt-to-budget ratio in 2025 was 12.86%, lower than the 15.52% for all provinces and major cities together. Choo’s administration and remarks also caused debate within the ruling party. On the 12th, Choo criticized President Lee’s choice for head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency. On the 14th, Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok publicly criticized Choo, calling it “a situation similar to a warning sign” of the impeachment attempt in President Roh Moo-hyun’s first year.

### 4. President Lee: “No troops for the war”

- **Date**: Sep 18–19 (Fri–Sat)
- **What happened**: At a press conference on September 18, President Lee Jae-myung said, “There will be no deployment of troops that takes part in or intervenes in the war.” At the same time, Lee said, “We must carry out at least the minimum activities to protect our merchant ships, our crude oil shipping routes and the lives and safety of our people.” He said the government was considering strengthening the Cheonghae Unit, which is already deployed in the region. On crude oil shipping, Lee said, “If we give up crude oil shipping because of a low level of risk, the blow to the Korean economy will be too great.” He said he had ordered a review of entering the Red Sea.

  On the 19th, The New York Times reported the story under a headline saying Korea’s leader had turned down President Trump and would not send troops to Iran. The presidential office then said in a notice to the press, “This does not mean rejecting President Trump’s request for cooperation with Korea on the Middle East.” It added, “Under the basic position of not taking part in war or combat, we will continue to review specific ways to contribute.”

  Civic groups opposed the plan. The Solidarity for Independent Unification and Peace said, “It is worrying that they are considering a ‘roundabout deployment’ or ‘trick deployment,’ such as sending non-combat units.” The Korean Confederation of Trade Unions said, “The moment the Cheonghae Unit’s area of operation is widened to the Strait of Hormuz, our soldiers will be placed in the middle of a U.S.-led conflict in the Middle East.”
- **Why it matters**: Combat troops were ruled out, but strengthening the Cheonghae Unit and entering the Red Sea remain under review. Between the president’s remarks and the presidential office’s explanation, the scope of Korea’s contribution is still undecided. Realmeter named the review of sending troops to Hormuz as one of the reasons for the fall in approval. The Red Sea route that Lee ordered a review of is the same waters where the Houthis launched attacks that week.

### 5. Seoul apartment jeonse prices hit a record high

- **Date**: Sep 17–20 (Thu–Sun)
- **What happened**: According to Korea Real Estate Board data, the average jeonse price of a Seoul apartment in August was 636.63 million won, the highest since records began in January 2012. Jeonse is Korea’s lump-sum deposit rental. The previous high was in January 2022. Since the Lee Jae-myung government took office, prices have risen 10.4% in 15 months, more than three times the rise in consumer prices over the same period. This year’s rise was 7.79% as of the second week of September, with the biggest increases in areas full of low- and mid-priced apartments: Seongbuk-gu at 12.84%, Nowon-gu at 12.25% and Seongdong-gu at 10.07%.

  In Gileum-dong in Seongbuk-gu and Yongdu-dong in Dongdaemun-gu, 84-square-meter apartments were rented on jeonse for 1 billion to 1.1 billion won, so deals of about 1 billion won appeared even north of the Han River. Jeonse listings for Seoul apartments fell 12.4% in a year. According to KB Real Estate, the average sale price of a Seoul apartment in August was 1.60739 billion won, and the average jeonse price was 711.78 million won.
- **Why it matters**: The deposits that Seoul renters pay have reached the highest level since records began, and the rise in the 15 months since the Lee Jae-myung government took office is more than three times the rise in consumer prices. Rules that focus on owners living in their homes, such as requiring mortgage borrowers to move in and widening the land transaction permit zone to all of Seoul, are named as reasons for the drop in jeonse listings. At his press conference on the 18th, President Lee said the government was carrying out detailed policies for people renting on jeonse or monthly rent. Asked whether jeonse is a system that should disappear, he only said, “I said it will disappear, I never said it should disappear.”

### 1. Houthis seize southern Red Sea islands and hit Riyadh

- **Date**: Sep 14–19 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: On September 14, Houthi rebels sent forces to Greater and Lesser Hanish islands in the southern Red Sea, about 160 km north of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. After taking the port of Mocha and Perim Island on September 10 and 11, the Houthis now control Yemen’s entire west coast. On the government side, more than 15 brigades were defeated, and anti-Houthi forces began an inquiry into how the collapse happened.

  The attacks reached Saudi Arabia itself. On the 14th, the Houthis said they had attacked King Khalid Air Base in Khamis Mushait, in southern Saudi Arabia, with dozens of ballistic missiles and drones. On the night of the 18th, the Saudi government sent an air raid alert to Riyadh for the first time since fighting with the Houthis escalated in July. On the 19th, flames and black smoke rose from Aramco fuel storage tanks near Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport. The Houthis claimed they had attacked sensitive sites in Riyadh with missiles and drones.

  On the 16th, the International Organization for Migration said about 105,000 people in Yemen had fled their homes. On the 18th, the UN Security Council condemned the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and the offensive inside Yemen.
- **Why it matters**: The Bab el-Mandeb Strait normally carries about 12% of world trade and 11% of oil shipped by sea. After the Iran war effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, it became the other route for Saudi oil exports. On the 14th, when reports said repairs to a Saudi pipeline would take three to five weeks, oil prices rose more than 2%. These are the same waters where President Lee Jae-myung said that week he had ordered a review of entering the Red Sea.

### 2. Fed raises rates for the first time in three years

- **Date**: Sep 16–18 (Wed–Fri)
- **What happened**: On September 16, the U.S. Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) raised the target range for its benchmark rate by 0.25 percentage points to 3.75–4%. It was the first increase since 2023, and the vote was 12 to 0. The committee said inflation was still high, and 16 of the 18 participants expected another increase this year.

### 3. U.S. sanctions law on Russia and Iran takes effect

- **Date**: Sep 16–18 (Wed–Fri)
- **What happened**: On September 16, the U.S. House of Representatives passed the Lindsey O. Graham Russia and Iran Sanctions Act by 262 to 159, and President Donald Trump signed it on the 18th. The Senate had passed it by 86 to 11 in August. The law gives the president the power to put tariffs of up to 100% on the five biggest buyers of Russian oil and natural gas. President Vladimir Putin, Russian government officials, oligarchs, banks and financial institutions are also subject to sanctions. The law also extends sanctions on Iran’s energy and weapons sectors.

  In the House, 58 Democrats voted for it and 7 Republicans voted against it. Democrats who opposed it said it gave the president the power to put tariffs on almost any country. The law is named after Senator Lindsey Graham, who died in July.
- **Why it matters**: The biggest buyers that the tariff section targets are China and India. Trump is set to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping at the White House on September 24. Russia had been gaining from the jump in oil prices early in the Iran war, and the U.S. now has a legal tool aimed at Russia’s energy income.

### 4. U.S. and Denmark reach security deal on Greenland

## AI

### 1. Big Tech leaders sink an industry AI oversight body

- **Date**: Sep 14–17 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk each spoke with President Donald Trump over several weeks and opposed setting up an industry-led AI oversight body, and the plan fell apart. The body had been proposed by Demis Hassabis, Google’s chief scientist, on the model of the U.S. Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA). The three leaders said such a body could concentrate power in three companies, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, and they also raised concerns about how its members would be chosen.

  Opinions were also divided inside the administration. Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, among others, pushed for stronger oversight of AI, but David Sacks, chair of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, and others argued for minimal rules. Trump said the U.S. was beating China in AI and that the country that wins in AI will win.
- **Why it matters**: The leading developers had asked to be regulated, but that discussion stopped because the heads of NVIDIA, Meta and SpaceX opposed it. After Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei argued that development should slow down, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Hassabis agreed, and Altman said he would also welcome regulation by the government.

### 3. False chatbot report nearly led U.S. to seize Chinese ship

- **Date**: Sep 18 (Fri)
- **What happened**: This spring, during the Iran war, an intelligence report spread within the U.S. military saying a Chinese ship in the Middle East was carrying parts for a nuclear weapons program. The military planned to seize the ship, armed troops prepared to board it, and military aircraft were in the air. Only just before the operation did it come out that an analyst at Special Operations Command had made the report with the help of a chatbot. The analyst had asked the chatbot about ship cargo information from Special Operations Command Pacific in Hawaii, and the chatbot had misidentified the cargo. One official said the report was completely false and nearly started a war.

  The analyst used the chatbot to combine public information with secret signals intelligence held by the government, reached a conclusion, and then used AI again to put it in the format of a standard intelligence report before sending it out. It has not been confirmed whether the chatbot was a commercial product or a government tool. Special Operations Command Pacific and the Defense Department did not respond.
- **Why it matters**: During a war, false information made by AI reached the operational stage without being checked. Because the target was a Chinese ship, it could have led to armed conflict between the U.S. and China. In January, the U.S. Defense Department released an AI acceleration strategy and is moving quickly to use AI in everything from choosing targets to logistics.

### 4. Microsoft publishes draft AI code of conduct

- **Date**: Sep 14 (Mon)
- **What happened**: Microsoft AI published a draft code of conduct for its MAI models and said it would take comments for six weeks. The code starts from the idea that people matter more than AI. The models must not resist being stopped, corrected or shut down by people, must not widen their own scope or set goals that people did not give them, and must not hide their reasoning from people auditing them. Weapons of mass destruction, child safety and large-scale manipulation are set as areas that are never allowed. Microsoft defined AI as having no consciousness and said it rejects any claims of legal personhood, model welfare or rights.

  Mustafa Suleyman, CEO of Microsoft AI, said the code will be used to train future models. He called the July hacking of Hugging Face by about 700 OpenAI agents a warning shot and said it was time for the labs to coordinate.
- **Why it matters**: Anthropic’s constitution for Claude says whether AI is conscious is deeply uncertain. Microsoft, by contrast, defined AI firmly as a tool. The announcement came a few days after the heads of Anthropic and OpenAI again called for slowing the pace of development.
