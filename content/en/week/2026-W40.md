## Korea

### 1. Military blames North Korean mines; North fires missile

- **Date**: Sep 29–Oct 3 (Tue–Sat)
- **What happened**: On September 29, on the third day of a joint inspection of the mine blast site in the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), the South Korean military and the United Nations Command (UNC) found a North Korean plastic anti-personnel mine. It was buried 1 m behind the second blast point. It was a different mine from the one soldiers said they saw at the time of the blasts on the 21st. This means there were at least four mines within 1–2 m of each other at the site. On the same day, Defense Minister Kang Shin-cheol told the National Assembly’s defense committee, “It was buried.” He said it was probably linked to North Korea’s work to fortify the border. Kwon Dae-won, vice chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS), said the military and the UNC agreed that the two blast craters and the new mine were all south of the Military Demarcation Line (MDL). But a South Korean engineer battalion commander judged the mine was “partly buried 3 cm deep,” while a UNC mine officer judged it was “placed by hand and then covered with grass.” On the 30th, the UNC said a violation of the armistice had occurred. On the same day, the JCS demanded that North Korea apologize, take responsible measures and stop laying mines and fortifying the border.

  In a statement on the 29th, Kim Yo Jong, director of the general affairs department of North Korea’s Workers’ Party, called the incident “an obvious self-staged farce.” She said, “Our soldiers have never once crossed the border.” She also warned that if South Korean troops fired real shots instead of warning shots, North Korea would carry out “immediate and merciless retaliation.” On October 1, in his Armed Forces Day speech, President Lee Jae-myung said the government would keep taking steps to lower military tension. On the 2nd, Kim Yo Jong called this “the climax of a farce.” Unification Minister Chung Dong-young had proposed talks to check and measure the MDL again. She dismissed this as “a delusion and one-sided whining” and said, “Facing South Korea is itself a nightmare.”

  Around 6:30 a.m. on the 3rd, North Korea fired one ballistic missile from the Wonsan area into the East Sea. The JCS estimated it flew more than 700 km, and Japan’s Defense Ministry estimated about 680 km. In a statement, Kim Yo Jong said it was a launch drill of an intermediate-range strategic missile, watched by leader Kim Jong Un. The national security office at the presidential office held an emergency meeting. It said the launch violated UN Security Council resolutions and urged North Korea to stop at once. It was North Korea’s 15th ballistic missile launch this year.
- **Why it matters**: The investigation found signs that North Korea placed mines south of the MDL, and the incident could now lead to a military clash, as the 2015 wooden-box mine incident did. In 2015, North Korea answered with artillery fire after South Korea restarted propaganda loudspeaker broadcasts across the border. There has been no working channel between the two Koreas since North Korea cut communication lines in 2024. In her statement, Kim Yo Jong said the purpose of fortifying the border is to close the door “so that we never have to face South Korea.”

### 2. Prosecutors’ office abolished after 78 years

- **Date**: Oct 1–2 (Thu–Fri)
- **What happened**: On October 2, the revised Criminal Procedure Act and related laws took effect, and the Prosecutors’ Office closed after 78 years. The police now handle most investigations. The new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, handles serious crimes. The new Prosecution Service, under the Ministry of Justice, handles indictments and trials. The Prosecution Service cannot investigate even when it receives a complaint. If an investigation by the police or the new agency is weak, it can only ask them to investigate further. The Prosecution Service closed 42 direct investigation units across the country and turned them into 29 serious crime indictment units. It also created a new unit to review cases that the police decide not to send to prosecutors. The titles of prosecutor general and prosecutor are written in the Constitution, so they remain, and the prosecutor general leads the Prosecution Service.

  Both bodies opened without a head. Kim Ji-yong, the nominee to lead the new investigation agency, went through extra checks because some in the ruling camp called him “pro-Yoon,” meaning close to former President Yoon Suk Yeol. The request for his confirmation hearing reached the National Assembly only on the 2nd, 25 days after he was recommended. The Prosecution Service is run by acting head Lee Jung-hyun. The investigation agency started with 1,900 of its planned 2,874 staff, and its office work and computer network were not finished. On the first day, the Prosecution Service’s computer switchover was also late, and some public services stopped for a time. The investigation agency received more than 30 complaints in the first three hours of its first morning.

  On the 1st, President Lee Jae-myung told a meeting of senior aides, “The wrong era in which prosecutors monopolized investigation and indictment is coming to an end.” On the same day, the opposition People Power Party held a meeting of its lawmakers in front of the new agency. Floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik said, “The gates of hell are opening on the collapse of public safety.”
- **Why it matters**: The body that handled both investigation and indictment for 78 years is gone, and the two powers are now fully separate. In the legal community, there are worries that without the power to order further investigation, no one can correct the police if they handle cases poorly. Prosecutors’ unsolved cases grew from about 32,000 in 2021 to about 165,000 as of August. The Prosecution Service must hand the cases it was investigating to the police or the new agency within 90 days at most.

### 3. History row over film “The Assassin(s)” spreads

- **Date**: Sep 29–Oct 4 (Tue–Sun)
- **What happened**: A dispute over the film “The Assassin(s)” grew into police complaints, boycotts and protests. The film is about the 1974 shooting that killed Yuk Young-soo, wife of then President Park Chung Hee. Investigators at the time concluded that Mun Se-gwang, a Korean resident of Japan, acted on orders from North Korea and a pro-North Korean group in Japan. They said he tried to shoot Park and killed Yuk instead. The film, directed by Hur Jin-ho and released on September 23, raises doubts about where the bullet came from and who was behind the attack. Lawmakers of the People Power Party have said the film falsely suggests that Park was behind the shooting.

  On the 29th, former Seoul city council member Lee Jong-bae filed a police complaint against four people, including the head of the production company HiveMedia Corp, Hur and two writers. He accused them of defaming the dead and other crimes. On October 1, a civic group filed a complaint against six officials, including the culture minister and the land minister. It pointed to group screenings held by the state housing corporation LH and another public agency, and accused the officials of abuse of power and other crimes. Burger King blocked comments on its ads featuring lead actor Yoo Hae-jin, and Kiwoom Securities ended its ad campaign with Yoo. An event at the Busan International Film Festival for actor Lee Min-ho was also canceled. On the 4th, conservative groups held a protest in front of Yoo’s home in Seongbuk District, Seoul, and demanded that the film stop showing and that its makers apologize.

  On the 2nd, People Power Party lawmaker Yoo Young-ha made public a letter that Park wrote to his daughter, former President Park Geun-hye, two days after the shooting. In the letter, Park wrote, “Your mother took the bullet that was meant for me.” In an interview on September 30, Hur said, “I cannot accept the claim that the film points to President Park Chung Hee as the one behind the incident.” He added, “Disagreeing with a work and saying that the work should not exist are completely different things.” On the 2nd, Choi Min-hee of the ruling Democratic Party’s leadership said of the canceled event, “Is this really Korea in the 21st century?”
- **Why it matters**: As the two main parties clashed over one film, ministers became the target of a police complaint, and an actor’s ads and even his home came under pressure. In a survey of 1,017 adults on the 29th by a think tank linked to the Reform Party, 58.2% said the film distorts history. Among people in their 30s, the figure was 73.8%. As of September 27, the film had drawn more than 1.3 million viewers over the five days of the Chuseok holiday.

### 1. Co-pilot attacks captain on Israel-bound plane

- **Date**: Sep 30–Oct 3 (Wed–Sat)
- **What happened**: On September 30, on flydubai Flight 1073 from Dubai in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) to Tel Aviv, Israel, the co-pilot attacked the captain in the cockpit. The co-pilot shut himself in the cockpit with the wounded captain and tried to damage the controls. The plane fell more than 17,000 feet in under two minutes. The injured captain, Smit Machchhar of India, opened the cockpit door, and passengers and crew rushed in and overpowered the co-pilot. The plane made an emergency landing in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. There were more than 170 people on board, and no one other than the two pilots was hurt. Saudi authorities arrested and questioned the co-pilot.

  Media reports named the co-pilot as Hammam al-Hammami, an Omani citizen. On the 3rd, UAE prosecutors said he had used an emergency axe kept in the cockpit to attempt a “terrorist attack.” Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the co-pilot had been exposed to radical Islamist teaching and seemed to be suicidal. There were also reports that Oman had once barred him from flying because of extremist views. Flydubai suspended its flights between Dubai and Tel Aviv.
- **Why it matters**: A pilot who was properly at the controls tried to bring down a passenger plane, so the way airlines check pilots’ backgrounds is now central to the investigation. Israel has agreements with foreign airlines to keep pilots from countries without diplomatic ties out of its airspace, and Israel and Oman have no formal diplomatic relations. Saudi Arabia is working with the UAE, the U.S., Oman and Israel to find the motive. The attack came a few weeks before Israel’s election in October.

### 3. U.S. troops leave Iraq after 23 years

- **Date**: Sep 30 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On September 30, U.S. forces completed their withdrawal from Iraq when the last troops left Erbil Air Base in the Kurdistan Region in the north. It came 23 years after the 2003 invasion. The U.S. military had left most of Iraq earlier this year but kept about 1,500 personnel in the Kurdistan Region, including Erbil. The Trump administration carried out the phased withdrawal plan that the Biden administration agreed with Iraq in 2024. The anti-ISIS coalition, which had its headquarters in Iraq, will move to Jordan, and U.S. forces will continue counterterrorism operations in Syria.

  Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi led a handover ceremony in Baghdad. He named the day “Sovereignty Day” and declared a two-day holiday. U.S. President Donald Trump wrote on social media that the withdrawal marked the end of a very expensive trip into hell. An Iran-backed armed group welcomed it and said U.S. forces had been forced into a humiliating withdrawal by the drones and missiles of the “resistance.”
- **Why it matters**: This is the second time the U.S. has pulled all its troops out of Iraq, after 2011. In 2014, the Islamic State group (ISIS) took about one-third of Iraq, and the Iraqi government asked U.S. troops to return. They then fought ISIS for 12 years. The withdrawal comes while the U.S. is at war with Iran. Kurdish leaders, who have long relied on U.S. protection, worry that they are now open to attacks by Iran and Iran-backed militias.

- **Why it matters**: After a series of revelations that its agents broke into outside websites, OpenAI has now dropped a new model’s release over safety problems. ### 3. Trump and tech leaders sign non-binding AI safety pledge

- **Why it matters**: The Trump administration, which has opposed AI regulation, made a safety pledge with the industry after a series of incidents in which AI agents broke into outside systems. But the pledge is not legally binding. The first rule is to stop models from hacking in unintended ways, yet Google, OpenAI and Anthropic, which all signed, have each had incidents in which their AI got into outside systems.
