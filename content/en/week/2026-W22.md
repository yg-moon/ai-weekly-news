## Korea

### 1. Shinsegae’s Chung Yong-jin apologizes for ‘Tank Day’

- **Date**: May 25–27 (Mon–Wed)
- **What happened**: At a press conference at Josun Palace in Seoul on May 26, Shinsegae Group Chairman Chung Yong-jin said, “I bow my head and sincerely apologize,” and “All responsibility lies with me.” It came eight days after Starbucks Korea held a “Tank Day” event on May 18, the anniversary of the May 18 Democratization Movement. The event used the phrase “tak on the desk,” which recalls a 1987 police cover-up of a student’s death under torture. It was Chung’s second apology, after a statement on the 19th. Following complaints from civic groups and families of May 18 victims, the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency’s public crime investigation unit is investigating Chung and former Starbucks Korea CEO Son Jeong-hyun as suspects on charges of insult and defamation.

  On the same day, Shinsegae Group said its own inquiry had “found no clear evidence to prove intent.” Of the five employees who planned the event, three, including the one who suggested the name “Tank Day,” refused to hand over their phones, citing privacy. The plan was approved by a team leader, a department head, a division head and the CEO, but no one raised a concern at any step.

  Starbucks said that from June 1 to 14 it would drop the rule that customers must use at least 60% of the amount loaded to get the balance back, and would refund prepaid card balances in full. Estimated card payments from May 18 to 24, when the controversy began, were 23.69 billion won, down 8.47 billion won (26.3%) from the week before.
- **Why it matters**: An event approved at four levels, up to the CEO, grew into a controversy over insulting history, and it became a case in which the head of the group apologized in public and faces a criminal investigation. Card payments fell by more than a quarter in a week, so the boycott showed up in actual sales.

### 2. Seosomun overpass collapse during demolition kills three

- **Date**: May 26–29 (Tue–Fri)
- **What happened**: At about 2:33 p.m. on May 26, part of the Seosomun overpass in Seodaemun District, Seoul, collapsed during demolition, killing three people and injuring three. The dead were the site manager of the contractor, Heunghwa Construction, the head of the supervision team and an outside structural engineer. At about 2:30 a.m. that day, the structure sank 2.9 cm while workers were cutting a slab, and work was stopped. At 2 p.m., the three went in between the girders for a safety check, and the girders collapsed.

  The first report to the Seoul city government came at about 7 a.m., four and a half hours after the sinking. In the 12 hours between the sinking and the collapse, 59 trains carrying passengers ran on the tracks under the overpass, and a KTX train with 42 people on board passed five minutes before the collapse. A piece of the concrete deck had also fallen off this section in 2021.

  On the 29th, the police searched the offices of the Seoul city government and others. Oh Se-hoon, the People Power Party candidate for Seoul mayor, protested, calling it “the president’s campaign to kill off Oh Se-hoon.”
- **Why it matters**: Half a day passed after the first warning signs were found, with no control of train service or roads. The project had been ordered by the Seoul city government, and the police search came five days before the local elections, so responsibility for safety management became an election issue.

### 3. Local election early voting hits record 23.51%

- **Date**: May 29–30 (Fri–Sat)
- **What happened**: Early voting turnout for the ninth nationwide local elections, held on May 29 and 30, was 23.51%. That is 2.89 percentage points higher than the 20.62% in the local elections four years ago, and the highest ever for local elections. By region, South Jeolla was highest at 38.95% and Daegu lowest at 18.65%. The Democratic Party said, “The higher the early voting turnout, the more encouraging it has been for our party,” while the People Power Party read it as “anger to judge the government.”

  On the 29th, while voting at a community center in Samcheong-dong, Seoul, President Lee Jae-myung came out of the voting booth and asked an election official, “Is it all right if it’s only half stamped?” He then went back in and finished voting. The People Power Party argued that this counted as showing his ballot and that it should be treated as invalid. On the 30th, it filed a complaint with the police against Lee and the election official for violating the Public Official Election Act. The Democratic Party replied, “It is a forced attack over a small mishap.”
- **Why it matters**: Four days before the election, early voting broke the record for local elections. With a sitting president accused of breaking election law, the voting process itself became the subject of a fight between the ruling and opposition parties.

### 5. Samsung Electronics workers approve tentative wage deal

- **Date**: May 27–28 (Wed–Thu)
- **What happened**: The joint bargaining group of Samsung Electronics’ unions said on May 27 that the tentative agreement had passed with 73.7% in favor (46,142 members) in a vote that closed at 10 a.m. Of the 65,593 members with voting rights, 95.5% voted. The company and the unions held a signing ceremony for the 2026 wage agreement the same day. The tentative agreement was reached on the 20th, through government mediation, just before a general strike.

  Under the deal, members in the memory division can get bonuses of up to 600 million won, while those in the non-memory chip division get around 160 million won and those outside the chip business about 6 million won. The DX division, which makes smartphones and home appliances, protested that its voice had been shut out of bargaining. On the 28th, Choi Seung-ho, head of the Super-Enterprise Union, a union across Samsung companies, said bargaining would be split between the chip (DS) division and the DX division. Choi also apologized for improper remarks and said there would be a vote of confidence in the union’s leadership.

  Samsung Electronics’ presidents issued a statement saying, “We will reflect deeply on labor relations and on management as a whole.” The company said it would invest 5 trillion won over the next five years in Korea’s shared-growth ecosystem and in training talent.
- **Why it matters**: The threat of a strike, which had raised fears of up to 100 trillion won in losses if chip plants stopped, ended with the deal confirmed. But the large gaps in bonuses between divisions left conflict within the unions.

## World

### 1. U.S. and Iran trade claims and strikes as deal nears

- **Date**: May 25–30 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: On May 29, U.S. President Donald Trump wrote on social media that he could approve a deal. Under it, Iran would permanently give up nuclear weapons, open the Strait of Hormuz without tolls, remove mines, and let the U.S. dig up and destroy its highly enriched uranium. He met for more than two hours in the White House Situation Room but announced no decision. A spokesperson for Iran’s foreign ministry said there was no final agreement yet. On the 30th, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said the U.S. was more than able to resume the war.

  Fighting went on during the talks. The U.S. military struck missile launch sites in Bandar Abbas in southern Iran and ships that were laying mines, then shot down four Iranian drones and hit a ground station that controls drones. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it attacked a U.S. military base in Kuwait in response. The U.S. said both rounds of strikes were defensive steps to protect the ceasefire.

  In Iran, access to the international internet began to be partly restored on the 26th after 88 days. It was the longest time any country has been cut off from the international internet.
- **Why it matters**: Seven weeks into the ceasefire, a deal covering the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and Iran’s nuclear program reached the stage of final approval, but the two sides gave different accounts of it. With the war disrupting the world’s energy supply, strikes and retaliation went back and forth instead of a deal being announced.

### 2. Israel seizes Beaufort Castle in Lebanon

- **Date**: May 28–31 (Thu–Sun)
- **What happened**: On May 31, the Israeli military took the 900-year-old Beaufort Castle in southern Lebanon. The advance across the Litani River is Israel’s deepest invasion of Lebanon in 26 years. Israel held the castle during its 18-year occupation, which ended in 2000. Defense Minister Israel Katz said the troops would stay there as part of a security zone, and the Israeli military ordered residents south of the Zahrani River to leave immediately. Lebanese Prime Minister Nawaf Salam accused Israel of a scorched-earth policy, and France asked for an emergency meeting of the UN Security Council.

  On the 28th, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said he had ordered the military to take control of 70% of the Gaza Strip. Under the U.S.-brokered ceasefire last October, the area under direct Israeli control was 53%, and Netanyahu said Israel now controls 60%. In the eight months since the ceasefire, Israeli gunfire and air strikes have killed more than 900 Palestinians.
- **Why it matters**: On both fronts, Israel expanded the land it holds while U.S.-brokered ceasefires were still officially in place. Iran is demanding an end to the war in Lebanon as a condition of a deal with the U.S., so the advance into Lebanon also affects the U.S.–Iran talks.

### 3. Suspected Ebola deaths in Congo reach 223

- **Date**: May 25–29 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On May 29, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated the death rate of the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo at 30–50%. Since the outbreak was declared on the 15th, confirmed and suspected cases have passed 1,000, with 10 confirmed and 223 suspected deaths. The WHO said the actual scale could be larger because the virus may have spread without being detected for some time.

  On the 25th, WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said the spread was outpacing the response and urged neighboring countries to act right away. In Ituri province, the center of the outbreak, residents demanding the bodies of the dead attacked hospitals and treatment centers. Isolation tents were burned and 25 patients ran away. Tedros arrived in Kinshasa on the 28th, said the outbreak could be stopped, and called for a ceasefire between armed groups.
- **Why it matters**: The disease is spreading in eastern areas where armed groups are active, and attacks on treatment facilities are blocking basic disease control such as isolation and safe burial. The WHO has already declared a public health emergency of international concern and classified neighboring countries as high risk.

## AI

### 1. Pope Leo XIV’s first encyclical is about AI

- **Date**: May 25–27 (Mon–Wed)
- **What happened**: On May 25, Pope Leo XIV issued his first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas. Its subtitle is about protecting the human person in the age of artificial intelligence, and it runs to 200 pages. The pope presented the encyclical together with Chris Olah, a co-founder of the AI company Anthropic.

  The encyclical says that when such power is concentrated in a few hands, it becomes opaque and escapes public oversight, and that AI increases the power of those who have economic power, expertise and data. It calls for clear standards and effective oversight in which the affected communities take part, and urges an end to the race to build ever more powerful algorithms and ever larger datasets. It does not mention superintelligence or artificial general intelligence (AGI).

  On the 27th, an analysis by an AI text detector found that some paragraphs of the encyclical may have been written with AI. The Vatican did not respond right away.
- **Why it matters**: It is the first encyclical on AI. Just as Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum in 1891 dealt with the concentration of power during the Industrial Revolution, this encyclical deals with the power of the few who hold AI technology. It came a few days after President Trump put off signing an executive order on government oversight of new AI models before release, and it put the Catholic Church on the side of those calling for oversight of AI.

### 3. Anthropic releases Claude Opus 4.8

- **Date**: May 28 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On May 28, Anthropic released Claude Opus 4.8 at the same price as Opus 4.7. The company said the new model points out what is uncertain about a task up front and makes fewer claims without evidence. In its own evaluations, the rate at which the model passes over flaws in code it wrote without mentioning them is about a quarter of the previous model’s rate.

Anthropic said it may be able to offer a Mythos-class model, which it has released only on a limited basis because of security concerns, to all customers in the coming weeks.
- **Why it matters**: Anthropic released a successor 41 days after Opus 4.7, which some users found disappointing, and it put forward honesty about the limits of its own work alongside performance numbers. Running many agents at once to take on code migrations of hundreds of thousands of lines became a product feature.

### 4. Robinhood lets AI agents trade stocks

- **Date**: May 27 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On May 27, the stock trading app Robinhood released a beta feature that lets users open an account just for an AI agent, which can then buy and sell stocks within a set amount. The agent can read the user’s portfolio and suggest strategies, but it can only place orders using the balance kept in its own wallet. Agents connect through MCP, an open standard that links AI to apps, and users get a notice for every trade and can stop the agent at any time.

  For now it works only with stocks, and Robinhood plans to add options, cryptocurrency, futures and prediction markets. Robinhood Gold card holders can also give an agent a virtual credit card with a monthly limit to buy things. The company warned that users could lose all the money they invest and said it would not be responsible for losses caused by an agent’s decisions.
- **Why it matters**: A major stock trading app has given AI agents the power to place orders with real money. It keeps responsibility for losses with the user and limits the possible damage with a separate account and spending limits.
- **Sources**: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/27/robinhood-now-lets-your-ai-agents-trade-stocks/) / [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/938095/robinhood-ai-agent-stock-trading)

### 5. YouTube to label AI-made videos automatically

- **Date**: May 27 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On May 27, YouTube said it would automatically put an AI label on videos when its own systems detect realistic use of AI, even if the creator does not report it. Since 2024, it had labeled only videos whose creators said they used AI. If C2PA metadata in the content confirms it was made entirely with AI, the label is permanent, and videos made with YouTube’s own AI tools also cannot have the label removed.

  Where the label appears is also changing. For realistic AI videos, the label will move from inside the description to just below the player for regular videos, and to the top of the screen for Shorts.
- **Why it matters**: Right after Google unveiled its Gemini Omni model, which makes videos that even follow the laws of physics, at its developer event the week before, the same company’s video platform took over from creators the job of identifying AI videos.
- **Sources**: [YouTube](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/) / [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/27/youtube-will-now-automatically-label-ai-videos/)
