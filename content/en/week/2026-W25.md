## Korea

### 1. Election body’s panel seeks investigation of 12 officials

- **Date**: Jun 15–19 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On June 19, the fact-finding committee of the National Election Commission, which looked into the ballot shortage in the June 3 local elections, recommended that 12 people be referred for investigation. They include former commission chair Noh Tae-ak, standing commissioner Wi Cheol-hwan (now acting chair) and former secretary general Heo Cheol-hoon. It recommended disciplinary action for six staff members. The committee said the guideline to print fewer ballots was decided in haste, and that the Songpa District election commission’s vote to print 50% fewer ballots led to the shortage. On election day, ballots ran out at four polling stations in Songpa District, Seoul, and voting stopped.

  To keep it from happening again, the committee proposed raising the minimum number of printed ballots to at least 70% of the number of voters. It also proposed making the commission chair a full-time post and putting the commission under duty inspections by the Board of Audit and Inspection. A Constitutional Court ruling exempts the commission from these inspections.

  Noh brought his spouse on all three of his overseas trips as chair, and the reports the commission later made public left this out. A 2024 trip to Germany and Estonia cost 71.94 million won, and a 2025 trip to Denmark and Sweden cost 90.53 million won. Noh first said he had not been told about the 50% cut guideline in advance, then wrote back to the committee that it had been on the agenda of a commission meeting in November 2025.

  On June 15, at an emergency supreme council meeting, the People Power Party decided to file petitions for new elections in all races in the six regions where ballots ran short: Seoul, Gyeonggi, Incheon, Busan, Ulsan and Gwangju–South Jeolla. Protesters angry about the shortage blocked the vote-counting building in Jamsil, Seoul, for the 10th day in a row. On the 15th, President Lee Jae-myung, who was on a trip to Europe, ordered the police to strictly investigate the protesters for stopping and searching people on their own and for obstructing work.
- **Why it matters**: The commission’s own fact-finding committee recommended referring its past and current leaders for investigation and proposed that the commission face duty inspections by the Board of Audit and Inspection, from which a Constitutional Court ruling had exempted it. The committee said, “The election commission needs sweeping reform close to dismantling it.” It took no position on new elections, saying it was for the courts to decide through legal steps such as election petitions. A National Assembly special committee for a parliamentary investigation, launched on the 18th, will investigate for 45 days, starting with a report from the commission on the 23rd. The People Power Party called for an independent special counsel to find who was responsible.

### 2. After JTBC, JoongAng Ilbo also defaults

- **Date**: Jun 15–19 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: Five companies of the JoongAng Group, including the holding company JoongAng Holdings, JTBC, Contentree JoongAng, Megabox JoongAng and JoongAng P&I, applied to the Seoul Bankruptcy Court for court-led rehabilitation. It came after JTBC failed to repay 20.6 billion won in asset-backed loans when they came due on June 12, and its credit rating fell from BBB to CCC. At a press conference on the 15th, JoongAng Group Vice Chairman Hong Jeong-do apologized, calling it “an unavoidable choice,” and said core work such as broadcasting the World Cup in North America would continue without disruption.

  JoongAng Ilbo, the newspaper where the group began, said it would seek a workout, a debt restructuring led by creditors, instead of court receivership. But it could not repay 22 billion won in commercial paper that Hanyang Securities asked to be paid early, because it did not have enough money in its account. It was declared in default on the 18th and in final default on the 19th. On the 19th, JoongAng Ilbo applied for a workout to its main creditor bank, Hana Bank. JTBC also defaulted on 36 billion won of commercial paper on the 19th.

  At the end of last year, JTBC had an accumulated deficit of 703.3 billion won and total equity of 19 billion won on a consolidated basis. In February this year, four months before it applied for rehabilitation, it still sold 93 billion won of unsecured bonds to the public. The Financial Supervisory Service began checking whether JTBC’s corporate bonds and electronic short-term bonds were mis-sold.
- **Why it matters**: Key companies of a media group that owns a newspaper, a broadcaster and movie theaters applied for court management at the same time. The burden grew from broadcast rights for the 2026–2032 Olympics and the 2026 and 2030 World Cups, which cost about $500 million, from Megabox, which has posted operating losses for six years in a row, and from shrinking TV advertising. Korea’s TV advertising revenue fell from 3.0752 trillion won in 2022 to 2.3073 trillion won in 2024. The Seoul Bankruptcy Court will question the company heads on the 23rd before deciding whether to start rehabilitation, and the Korea Media and Communications Commission said it would look at JTBC’s cash problems when reviewing its broadcast license renewal.

### 3. KOSPI tops 9,000 for the first time

- **Date**: Jun 18–19 (Thu–Fri)
- **What happened**: On June 18, the KOSPI rose 199.60 points (2.25%) from the day before to close at 9,063.84, ending above 9,000 for the first time. It came 22 trading days after the index first passed 8,000 during trading on May 15. Chip stocks rose on news that the U.S. and Iran had signed a memorandum to end their war, and foreigners were net buyers of 1.271 trillion won. Samsung Electronics rose 4.62% and SK hynix 6.51%. The day before, at its first meeting under Chair Kevin Warsh, the U.S. Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.50–3.75%, and nine officials expected a rate hike this year.

  The gains were concentrated in large chip stocks. On the 18th, 109 stocks on the KOSPI rose and 791 fell. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix make up 54.4% of the KOSPI’s market value, and their shares had risen 170% and 270% this year through the 17th.

  On the 19th, the KOSPI rose as high as 9,385.59 during trading, then fell to 8,831.72 after news that the U.S. vice president had put his trip to Switzerland on hold. The day’s range of 553.87 points was the fifth largest ever, and the index closed at 9,052.42. The KOSDAQ fell 3.43% that day and dropped below 1,000.

### 4. Lee Hwa-young gets four months for ‘salmon party’ perjury

- **Date**: Jun 20 (Sat)
- **What happened**: Early on June 20, the Suwon District Court sentenced former Gyeonggi Province Vice Governor for Peace Lee Hwa-young to four months in prison for perjury under the Act on Testimony and Appraisal before the National Assembly. The ruling came from Criminal Division 11 (presiding judge Song Byung-hoon). The first trial was held as a jury trial. In October 2024, at a National Assembly hearing on impeaching prosecutor Park Sang-yong, Lee testified that there had been a “salmon and liquor party” in a prosecutor’s office at the Suwon District Prosecutors’ Office to sway his statements. The jurors split four to three for guilty. The court found that the statements of the people who were in the prosecutor’s office were consistent, while Lee’s were not believable because the place and date of the drinking changed many times.

  Lee was also charged with having Kim Sung-tae, former chairman of Ssangbangwool Group, split up donations during President Lee Jae-myung’s Gyeonggi governor race and presidential primary (a violation of the Political Funds Act). On this charge, the court found him not guilty, as the jury unanimously decided. On charges including abuse of power related to aid to North Korea, the court found that the prosecution had abused its power to indict and dismissed the charges on its own. The trial ran for 10 days from the 8th, not counting weekends, the longest since jury trials began in Korea in 2008. Lee Hwa-young’s side said it would appeal.
- **Why it matters**: Based on Lee Hwa-young’s claims, the National Assembly held the impeachment hearing for prosecutor Park and a parliamentary investigation into fabricated indictments, and the justice ministry carried out a special inspection. The People Power Party said the key basis for withdrawing the indictment in President Lee’s North Korea remittance trial had collapsed. The Democratic Party replied that the jury was split four to three and that two of the three charges ended in acquittal or dismissal, so the case should be argued again on appeal. Lee Hwa-young is in prison serving a final sentence of seven years and eight months in the Ssangbangwool North Korea remittance case.

### 5. Special counsel seeks 18 months for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon

- **Date**: Jun 17 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On June 17, the special counsel team for Kim Keon-hee (led by Min Joong-ki) asked for 18 months in prison and a 33 million won forfeiture for Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon, who was charged with violating the Political Funds Act. Both sides agree that Oh and Myung met, that Myung ran polls about Oh, and that Kim sent money to Myung. The questions are whether Oh ordered the polls and whether he had Kim pay for them.

## World

### 1. U.S. and Iran sign memorandum to end the war

- **Date**: Jun 15–21 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On June 17, U.S. President Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding ending a war that had lasted 110 days. The two countries and their allies agreed to stop military operations on all fronts, including Lebanon, immediately and permanently, and to negotiate a final deal within 60 days. Iran agreed not to charge transit fees in the Strait of Hormuz for at least 60 days and to fully restore passage within 30 days. The U.S. agreed to waive sanctions on Iranian crude oil and petroleum product exports as soon as the deal was signed and to lift the other sanctions in step with a final nuclear deal. Iran’s highly enriched uranium will be diluted inside Iran under the supervision of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA).

  The U.S. military lifted its blockade of Iranian ports on the 18th. Iran’s chief negotiator, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said Iran would charge transit fees in the strait after the 60 days, and Saudi Arabia’s foreign minister replied that things must go back to how they were before the war.

  In Lebanon, fighting went on after the deal. On the 18th, Israel released a map of a “security zone” in southern Lebanon that it would keep occupying. After a Hezbollah attack killed four Israeli soldiers on the 19th, Israel carried out more than 150 air strikes, and at least 47 people were killed in Lebanon. U.S.–Iran talks planned in Switzerland that day were canceled, and the U.S. announced that Israel and Hezbollah had agreed to a ceasefire. As the strikes continued, Iran’s military announced on the 20th that it was closing the Strait of Hormuz, and U.S. Central Command replied that ships were still passing. The first face-to-face talks were held in Bürgenstock, Switzerland, on the 21st. But after Trump warned that he would hit Iran hard again if Hezbollah did not stop, Iran’s state news agency IRNA said the delegation had left the building where the talks were held.
- **Why it matters**: The memorandum has no limits on ballistic missiles, which Israel had demanded, and the U.S. accepted dilution inside Iran instead of its demand that all the uranium be shipped out of the country. In the U.S., Republican Senator Ted Cruz and Democrat Susan Rice, a former national security adviser, among others, criticized the deal for giving Iran money and sanctions relief before a nuclear deal. The deal also includes setting up a fund of at least $300 billion for rebuilding Iran and developing its economy. The war killed at least 7,000 people and sent energy prices soaring.

### 5. Bank of Japan raises its key rate to 1%

- **Date**: Jun 16 (Tue)
- **What happened**: On June 16, the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate from 0.75% to 1%, the highest level in 31 years, since 1995. As energy prices jumped because of the Iran war, Japan’s wholesale prices in May rose more than 6% from a year earlier, the fastest rise in three years. Consumer prices rose 1.4% in April, below the 2% target, but the Bank of Japan said medium- and long-term inflation expectations keep rising, so there is a risk that underlying inflation will go above the target.

  It is the second rate hike since Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi took office. Governor Kazuo Ueda was in the hospital for treatment of a liver cyst and did not attend the meeting.
- **Why it matters**: On August 5, 2024, right after a Japanese rate hike, the KOSPI fell 8.77% in one day. When Japanese rates rise, money borrowed in cheap yen and invested in assets abroad can pull out. Japan kept its rates around 0% for almost 20 years, then raised them in March 2024 for the first time in 17 years and has kept raising them since. This hike is also meant to stop the yen from weakening.

### 4. U.S. regulator orders faster grid hookups for data centers

- **Date**: Jun 18 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On June 18, the U.S. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) ordered six power grid operators to quickly handle requests from large power users such as data centers to connect to the transmission grid. All the commissioners voted in favor.

  Grid operators must show that data centers can connect to the grid in a timely and orderly way, and the data centers pay the cost of connecting. Within 30 days, operators must report their spare generating capacity, and within 60 days they must show that their regional power rates are justified or change them. The order also tells them to look at new transmission technologies and to accept more power that data centers generate themselves.
- **Why it matters**: The order speeds up connection but does not deal with the shortage of generating capacity. Power demand from data centers is expected to almost triple by 2035, and at the end of 2023 the power plants waiting to connect to the grid added up to more than the capacity of all existing plants. Companies that could not wait to connect have turned to generating their own power, which is more expensive and complex. Energy Secretary Chris Wright asked the commission to act last October, saying delays in connecting data centers hurt U.S. competitiveness in AI.
