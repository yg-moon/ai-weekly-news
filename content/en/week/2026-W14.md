## Korea

### 1. Cabinet approves 26.2 trillion won ‘war budget’

- **Date**: Mar 31–Apr 2 (Tue–Thu)
- **What happened**: On March 31, at a Cabinet meeting chaired by President Lee Jae-myung, the government approved a 26.2 trillion won “2026 supplementary budget to overcome the Middle East war crisis” and sent it to the National Assembly. It is the first supplementary budget from the Ministry of Planning and Budget, which was launched this year. It is funded without issuing more government bonds, using 25.2 trillion won in extra tax revenue from the boom in chips and the stock market and 1 trillion won from funds’ own resources.

  One of the main programs is 4.8 trillion won in relief payments for damage from high oil prices. They give 100,000 to 600,000 won per person to about 35.8 million people in the bottom 70% by income. The 2.85 million basic livelihood recipients get 550,000 to 600,000 won, and the 360,000 people in low-income households just above that level and single-parent families get 450,000 to 500,000 won. Everyone else eligible gets 100,000 won in the Seoul metropolitan area, 150,000 won outside it, and 200,000 to 250,000 won in areas with shrinking populations. Five trillion won goes to the oil price cap and to securing naphtha supply, and the refund rate of the K-Pass, a public transit fare refund card, rises by up to 30 percentage points for a limited time.

  In a budget speech to the National Assembly on April 2, Lee said, “This crisis is not a shower that passes quickly, but a huge storm that may last we don’t know how long,” and asked for quick passage. He said the government would show zero tolerance for price fixing and hoarding. The ruling and opposition parties agreed to pass the budget at a plenary session on April 10.
- **Why it matters**: A month into the war, the government decided to share the costs of the war in the Middle East directly with households. The payments add up to 40% of last year’s consumption coupons for livelihood recovery (12.1709 trillion won), and the government expects this budget to raise economic growth by 0.2 percentage points.

### 2. Trump says Korea did not help, citing U.S. troops there

- **Date**: Apr 1–4 (Wed–Sat)

He then said Korea had not helped the U.S., even though the U.S. keeps 45,000 troops right next to a nuclear power. The number of U.S. troops in Korea is around 28,500. HD Hyundai Oilbank, S-Oil and GS Caltex are working to load oil at Yanbu using Korean shipping companies.
- **Why it matters**: The president of an allied country publicly criticized Korea, linking the presence of U.S. troops in Korea to the question of sending forces to the strait. Korean refiners rely on heavy crude from the Middle East for more than half of their crude oil. So the government chose to keep up its oil supply through diplomacy with many countries and detour routes rather than by sending forces.

### 3. Oil crisis alert raised; odd-even driving for public bodies

- **Date**: Mar 30–Apr 1 (Mon–Wed)
- **What happened**: On April 1, the government held a resource security council and decided to raise the crisis alert for crude oil to “Alert,” the third of four levels, from midnight on the 2nd. The alert for natural gas will also go up from level 1, “Attention,” to level 2, “Caution.” The trade ministry explained that with the war going on for more than a month, disruptions to crude oil supply and rising global gas prices had become clear.

  In line with this, from the 8th, cars at 11,000 institutions will be allowed on the road only every other day, based on whether their license plate ends in an odd or even number. The rule covers central government agencies, public institutions, local governments, regional education offices and elementary, middle and high schools. At about 30,000 public parking lots run by public institutions, a rule keeping each car out one day in five will apply, and the same five-day rule in the private sector stays voluntary.

  As the supply of naphtha, a raw material, became uncertain, people also began panic-buying the official pay-per-bag garbage bags. After news that manufacturers had only a month’s worth of raw materials in stock, big supermarkets and convenience stores limited how many bags each customer could buy. On March 30, Minister of Climate, Energy and Environment Kim Sung-whan said that in the worst case, ordinary bags would be allowed, and that the price of the bags would not go up.
- **Why it matters**: With the “Alert” level, the government entered the stage of forcibly cutting car use in the public sector. The effects of the oil shortage spread beyond fuel prices to the supply of everyday goods made from naphtha.

### 5. Ministry moves to ban rote teaching at preschool academies

- **Date**: Apr 1 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On April 1, the Ministry of Education announced measures on private education for young children to protect their right to develop. Private academies would be banned from cramming knowledge into children under 3, and for children aged 3 and older who have not yet started school, cramming lessons beyond three hours a day or 15 hours a week would be banned. Ranking children by comparing their results would also be classed as “harmful teaching.” The ministry gave as examples of what would be banned making children fill in an alphabet writing workbook every day or memorize numbers from 1 to 100.

  The plan will be carried out by amending the law on private academies, and the goal is to pass it through the National Assembly this year. If it passes, it would apply from the second half of next year at the earliest. Academies that break the rules would face fines of up to 50% of their sales, and the administrative fine would rise to 10 million won. Rewards for reporting violations would rise to up to 2 million won.
- **Why it matters**: The government plans to limit by law the private education of young children known as the “age-4 exam” and “age-7 exam,” entrance tests for competitive academies. Kang Min-kyu, director general for early childhood policy at the ministry, said, “For English kindergartens, running all-day classes will become difficult.” English-language academies for young children are expected to be the main target in practice.

### 3. 40-plus countries discuss opening Hormuz without the U.S.

- **Date**: Apr 1–3 (Wed–Fri)
- **What happened**: On April 2, British Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper chaired a video meeting of more than 40 countries to discuss all diplomatic, economic and joint steps to get Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. The World Bank warned that if the blockade continues, 9 million people could face food insecurity. After U.S. President Donald Trump said he would leave the strait to other countries, the countries that import oil began working out their response separately, without the U.S.

### 4. Artemis II launches, first crewed Moon trip since 1972

- **Date**: Apr 1–4 (Wed–Sat)
- **What happened**: At 6:35 p.m. (U.S. Eastern Time) on April 1, NASA’s SLS rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying the Orion spacecraft. On board were Commander Reid Wiseman, NASA astronauts Christina Koch and Victor Glover, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency. After checking the life support, communication and navigation systems in Earth orbit, the crew will set off on a flight of about 10 days that loops in a figure eight around the far side of the Moon and back. - **Why it matters**: It was the first time people had headed to the Moon since Apollo 17 in 1972. It was also the first lunar journey by a woman, a person of color and someone who is not American. NASA sees this flight as a step toward landing on the Moon and building a permanent base there.

### 5. Israel makes death the default sentence for Palestinians

- **Date**: Mar 30–31 (Mon–Tue)
- **What happened**: On March 30, Israel’s parliament passed a law making the death penalty the default sentence for Palestinians in the occupied West Bank who are convicted in military courts of deliberate killings classified as terrorism. The death penalty can be imposed even if prosecutors do not ask for it, and by a simple majority of judges rather than a unanimous decision, and it must be carried out within 90 days of sentencing. Palestinians in the West Bank will have no way to appeal or seek a pardon, while people tried inside Israel can have the sentence reduced to life in prison.

  National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, who led the push for the bill, celebrated after the vote by waving a bottle. The Association for Civil Rights in Israel challenged the law at the Supreme Court as unconstitutional. On the 31st, UN human rights chief Volker Türk said the law was discriminatory and that applying it to people in occupied territory would be a war crime. The German government and the European Union also expressed concern.
- **Why it matters**: The law does not apply to Jewish extremists who commit the same crime, so it creates a law under which punishment depends on nationality and ethnicity. Israel’s last execution was of the Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann in 1962.

### 2. Anthropic accidentally publishes Claude Code source code

- **Date**: Mar 31–Apr 2 (Tue–Thu)
- **What happened**: On March 31, a map file pointing to the original source code was included in the npm package for Anthropic’s coding tool Claude Code, making the code public. It came to about 1,900 TypeScript files and more than 512,000 lines of code. Anthropic said it was a release packaging problem caused by human error, not a security breach, and that no customer data or credentials were exposed.

  On April 2, an analysis by the security company Zscaler found that GitHub repositories disguised as the leaked code were spreading Vidar and GhostSocks, malware that steals information.
- **Why it matters**: A company reported to be preparing for an initial public offering made the code of its main product public by its own mistake, and in trying to pull it back, it also blocked repositories belonging to unrelated developers.

### 3. Google releases open model Gemma 4

- **Date**: Apr 2 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On April 2, Google DeepMind released Gemma 4, an open-weight model. It was built from the same research as Gemini 3 and comes in four sizes: effective 2 billion (E2B) and effective 4 billion (E4B) parameters, a 26 billion-parameter mixture-of-experts (MoE) model and a 31 billion-parameter model. Google said the 31B model ranks third among open models on the Arena AI text leaderboard and the 26B model sixth.

  The two larger models handle a context of 256,000 tokens, and the 31B model runs on a 24 GB consumer graphics card when compressed to 4 bits. The two smaller models are for devices such as smartphones and the Raspberry Pi, and they also take image and audio input. The models support more than 140 languages and function calling.

  Google changed the license from its own terms to Apache 2.0. The previous Gemma license banned some uses and let Google cut off the right to use the models if the rules were broken.
- **Why it matters**: As open models from Chinese companies such as Moonshot AI, Alibaba and Z.ai keep growing in number, a major U.S. lab released an open model under a license with almost no limits on commercial use. Google said Gemma has been downloaded more than 400 million times so far.
