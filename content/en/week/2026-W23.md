## Korea

### 1. Democratic Party wins 12 of 16 mayor and governor races

- **Date**: Jun 3–5 (Wed–Fri)
- **What happened**: The Democratic Party won 12 of the 16 races for big-city mayor and provincial governor. It won Gyeonggi, Incheon, the Chungcheong region, the Honam region and Jeju, and also took Busan, North Jeolla and Gangwon, which exit polls had listed as close races. The People Power Party won Daegu, North Gyeongsang, South Gyeongsang and Seoul. In National Assembly by-elections held at the same time for 14 seats, the Democratic Party won nine, the People Power Party four and an independent one. Thirteen of the 14 seats had been held by the Democratic Party.

  The result of the Seoul mayoral race kept changing until the count was over. Right after voting ended, the exit poll by the three main TV networks had Democratic candidate Jung Won-o at 51.4% and Oh Se-hoon at 46.0%. After midnight, Oh quickly closed the gap and overturned the exit poll result, and Jung conceded on the morning of June 4. In the final result, counted through the last ballot box on the 5th, Oh had 49.22% and Jung 48.07%, a gap of 60,259 votes. Oh led in 10 districts, including Gangnam, Seocho and Songpa and districts along the Han River, and Jung led in the other 15. In races for Seoul district heads, the Democratic Party won 17 of 25, reversing the 8 to 17 result of four years ago.

  In the by-election for Busan’s Buk-gap district, Han Dong-hoon, who ran as an independent after being expelled from the People Power Party, beat Democratic candidate Ha Jung-woo (41.24%) with 42.99% (with 99.51% of votes counted). In the re-election for Gyeonggi’s Pyeongtaek-eul district, People Power Party candidate Yoo Eui-dong won, and Rebuilding Korea Party candidate Cho Kuk came third.
- **Why it matters**: In the first nationwide election since the Lee Jae-myung government took office a year earlier, the ruling party took three-quarters of the big-city mayor and governor posts. But the opposition held on to the Seoul mayor’s post, the most hard-fought race, and in the by-elections the People Power Party went from one seat to four. In his victory speech, Han Dong-hoon, who had been expelled from the People Power Party, spoke of “the road to rebuilding conservatism.”

### 2. Ballot shortage halts voting at 22 polling stations

- **Date**: Jun 3–7 (Wed–Sun)
- **What happened**: On election day, 50 polling stations around the country had fewer ballots than voters and used extra ballots sent to them. At 22 of them, voting stopped for a while and then started again. Of these, 19 were in Seoul and 12 in Songpa District. The National Election Commission said it had printed election-day ballots for 50% of the number of voters because early voting turnout has been rising. It had a printing budget for about 110% of voters. Polling station 2 in Jamsil 7-dong, Songpa District, had prepared only 1,900 ballots for 3,856 voters, or 49.3%.

  Two ballot boxes from Jamsil 7-dong (about 2,000 votes) were blocked by protesters surrounding the polling station and reached the vote-counting center only on the morning of June 5. Adding these votes moved one proportional representation seat on the Seoul Metropolitan Council from the Democratic Party to the People Power Party. On the 7th, a protest blocking the counting center in Jamsil to demand a new election went on for a third day, with more than 30,000 people taking part.

  On the 5th, National Election Commission chair Noh Tae-ak and secretary general Heo Cheol-hoon apologized to the public and offered to resign. The commission said it would set up a fact-finding committee made up only of outside members around the 10th and finish its inquiry by the 20th. On the 7th, President Lee Jae-myung called it “a grave matter that damaged the foundation of the people’s sovereignty,” ordered a joint investigation headquarters with prosecutors and police, and asked the National Assembly to hold a parliamentary investigation.
- **Why it matters**: The commission set a cautious printing standard to remove an excuse for election fraud claims that leftover ballots are used to rig elections, and that decision led to voting being stopped. Four years ago, right after the presidential election, the commission’s chair and secretary general both resigned over the “basket voting” controversy about how ballots were handled. Now its leaders have again offered to resign together, and the commission faces a fact-finding inquiry and a criminal investigation with no one at the top.

### 3. Explosion at Hanwha Aerospace plant in Daejeon kills five

- **Date**: Jun 1–2 (Mon–Tue)
- **What happened**: At about 10:59 a.m. on June 1, an explosion at Hanwha Aerospace’s Daejeon plant in Oesam-dong, Yuseong District, Daejeon, killed five people and injured two. Seven people were working at the site at the time. Hanwha believes the explosion happened while workers were wiping valves and tools after filling containers with solid rocket propellant, a sticky substance.

  It is the third explosion at the same plant. Accidents in 2018 and 2019 killed five and three people, so three accidents in eight years have killed 13 people. Hanwha Group issued a formal apology, and Chairman Kim Seung-youn ordered a group-wide task force led by Vice Chairman Yeo Seung-joo to respond.

  At a Cabinet meeting on the 2nd, President Lee Jae-myung said, “It is a serious problem that the same type of accident keeps happening at the same workplace.” He told the Ministry of Employment and Labor to pick out workplaces where the same type of accident keeps happening and report on them separately.
- **Why it matters**: After three explosions in eight years at the same plant, the president ordered that workplaces with repeated accidents of the same type be managed separately and that safety checks at similar workplaces be speeded up. The accident happened two days before the local elections, and the ruling and opposition parties held back from campaigning that day.

### 4. Han Sung-sook named as new prime minister nominee

- **Date**: Jun 7 (Sun)
- **What happened**: President Lee Jae-myung nominated Minister of SMEs and Startups Han Sung-sook to replace Prime Minister Kim Min-seok, who had offered to resign. Presidential Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik said, “With her experience as head of an IT company and as minister of SMEs and Startups, she is the right person to complete the AI transformation, the task of our time, without setbacks, and to lead growth for everyone in Korea.”

  Han was a founding member of the web portal Empas and then worked at Naver. In 2017 she became Naver’s first woman CEO and led the company until 2022. She became the first minister of SMEs and Startups when the Lee Jae-myung government took office.
- **Why it matters**: If confirmed by the National Assembly, Han will be Korea’s first woman prime minister in 20 years, since former Prime Minister Han Myeong-sook in 2006, and only its second ever. The presidential office set out the new prime minister’s task as spreading the growth driven by the chip boom and rising exports to small businesses, the self-employed and neighborhood shops.

### 5. Starbucks boycott over ‘Tank Day’ continues

- **Date**: Jun 1–7 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: From June 1 to 14, Starbucks Korea began giving full refunds of Starbucks card balances of up to 2 million won, no matter how much of the card had been used. Before, customers could get the balance back only after using at least 60% of the amount loaded. Prepaid balances on the cards total more than 400 billion won. The move came as a boycott continued over the controversy that Starbucks Korea’s “Tank Day” marketing on May 18 belittled the May 18 Democratization Movement. Shinsegae Group had already replaced the CEO of Starbucks Korea, and Chairman Chung Yong-jin had apologized to the public.

  Estimated sales kept falling. Based on estimated card payments, the daily average went from about 4.53 billion won before the event to 3.4 billion won in the first week after it (down 25%). It then fell to 2.95 billion won in the four days after Chung’s apology (May 26–29, down 35%). In Gwangju, civic groups held one-person protests in front of stores, while people on the other side answered with a campaign to buy from Starbucks.

  On the 7th, the May 18 Memorial Foundation said it had received a reply from Starbucks’ U.S. headquarters apologizing and calling the event inappropriate marketing that should never have happened. On the 2nd, the National Pension Service replied, “We will review it,” to the foundation’s request that it consider using its shareholder rights at Emart, the parent company of Starbucks Korea.
- **Why it matters**: Sales fell even further after the apology, so an apology from top executives did not stop the boycott. With supporters of the ruling camp boycotting and the other side buying in response, buying coffee became part of a political fight between camps.

## World

### 1. Iran attacks Kuwait airport and Israel

- **Date**: Jun 1–7 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On June 1, Iran said it would stop talks with the U.S. because of Israel’s offensive in Lebanon. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the ceasefire between Iran and the U.S. was a ceasefire on all fronts, including Lebanon. U.S. President Donald Trump said that even if the talks stopped, he would not immediately start bombing again and would keep up the blockade of Iranian ports.

  On the 2nd, the U.S. military used a Hellfire missile to stop a tanker that was heading through the blockade to Kharg Island. On the 3rd, Iran attacked the passenger terminal of Kuwait International Airport with drones, killing one person and injuring 63. It was the first deadly attack in the Gulf region since the April 8 ceasefire. Araghchi said there was no progress in the talks.

  On the 7th, Israel struck Hezbollah strongholds in Beirut’s southern suburbs, killing two people and injuring 11. A few hours later, Iran fired about 10 ballistic missiles at northern Israel, and the Israeli military said they were all shot down or fell in open areas. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it would fire missiles and drones without stopping for seven days. Trump asked Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu not to strike back.
- **Why it matters**: Iran made a ceasefire in Lebanon a condition of its ceasefire with the U.S., and when Israel struck Beirut, it actually attacked Israel. With the talks to end the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz stalled, the targets of attacks widened to include a civilian airport in Kuwait.

### 2. Israel and Lebanon renew ceasefire; Hezbollah rejects it

- **Date**: Jun 3–4 (Wed–Thu)
- **What happened**: On June 3, the U.S. State Department announced that Israel and the Lebanese government had agreed to renew their ceasefire after a fourth round of talks with U.S. mediation. The deal depends on Hezbollah fully stopping its attacks and pulling all its fighters out of the area between the Israeli border and the Litani River. ### 3. Russia attacks with 73 missiles and 656 drones

- **Date**: Jun 2–7 (Tue–Sun)

Long-range attacks by both sides spread to capitals and symbolic places. Ukraine said it was running out of Patriot interceptors, which can stop ballistic missiles.

### 4. Confirmed Ebola cases reach about 400

- **Date**: Jun 1–5 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On June 5, the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said the Ebola outbreak spreading in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda had about 400 confirmed cases and 63 deaths. This outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo virus, for which there is no vaccine and no approved treatment. The World Health Organization has declared it a public health emergency of international concern.

  In modeling released on the 5th, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) projected that cases could grow to between 10,000 and more than 20,000, depending on how quickly patients are isolated. That would be close to the size of the 2014–2016 West Africa outbreak, which had more than 28,000 cases and killed more than 11,000 people.

  A U.S. plan to build a 50-bed facility at Laikipia Air Base in Kenya to isolate and treat Americans exposed to the virus met strong opposition. Two people were killed at a protest in nearby Nanyuki on the 1st, and Kenya’s High Court ordered the plan stopped. In a letter to Congress, former senior CDC officials called for the policy to be dropped, saying it raised serious clinical, ethical, operational and legal concerns.
- **Why it matters**: With no vaccine or treatment, even the CDC’s projections ranged from 10,000 to more than 20,000 cases depending on how quickly infected people are isolated. The plan to put an Americans-only quarantine site in Kenya, which has no cases, led to local protests and deaths.

### 5. North Korea shows new nuclear material plant

- **Date**: Jun 3–4 (Wed–Thu)
- **What happened**: North Korea’s state news agency KCNA reported on June 4 that leader Kim Jong Un had toured a new facility producing weapons-grade nuclear material on the 3rd. Kim said the country had more than doubled its capacity to produce weapons-grade nuclear material over the past five years and confirmed the priority of its plan to “exponentially strengthen the national nuclear forces.” KCNA did not say where the facility is or when it started operating.

  Photos released showed a hallway packed with rows of centrifuges. South Korea’s Joint Chiefs of Staff assessed the facility as a uranium enrichment plant and said it was watching it closely with the U.S. It is the third time North Korea has shown a uranium enrichment facility, after Yongbyon in 2010 and a facility believed to be Kangson in 2024. Experts said it might be a new enrichment facility that had been under construction at Yongbyon.
- **Why it matters**: While the U.S. was negotiating with Iran over giving up its nuclear material, North Korea showed off a facility that produces nuclear material. It is seen as a sign that North Korea has no intention of putting its nuclear program on the negotiating table. In a March report, the U.S. Congressional Research Service estimated that North Korea has enough nuclear material for up to 90 warheads and has assembled about 50.

## AI

### 1. Anthropic confidentially submits IPO filing

- **Date**: Jun 1–4 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On June 1, Anthropic said it had confidentially submitted a draft registration statement for an initial public offering (IPO) to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). It has not set the number of shares or the price and said it would go ahead depending on market conditions. A confidential filing lets a company start preparing to list without making its financial information and risk factors public.

  The filing came less than a week after Anthropic raised $65 billion at a valuation of $965 billion. That is higher than its rival OpenAI’s valuation of $852 billion. Anthropic’s annualized revenue passed $47 billion in May, up from about $9 billion at the end of 2025.

  On the 4th, co-founder Daniela Amodei explained the listing in terms of capital. Amodei said training models and serving inference require huge upfront costs, and that the public market is the right source of money for companies building frontier models.
- **Why it matters**: In the race over whether OpenAI or Anthropic lists first, Anthropic took the first step. In the same week, Alphabet decided to sell $85 billion of stock to invest in AI infrastructure, and its first $40 billion offering drew $45 billion in orders. With SpaceX’s listing set for June 12, large AI-related fundraising came to the public market all at once.

### 2. Trump orders government review of AI models before release

- **Date**: Jun 2 (Tue)
- **What happened**: U.S. President Donald Trump signed an executive order asking AI companies to give the government their newest AI models with strong cyber capabilities up to 30 days before releasing them. Handing them over is voluntary. The order states that this section cannot be read as creating any mandatory government license or pre-approval requirement.

  A draft in May had set the review period at 90 days. Trump called off the signing event a few hours before it was set to take place in late May, and the period was shortened in line with industry demands. The order tells the Justice Department to treat AI-enabled hacking as a priority for enforcement, and tells security agencies to set up a cybersecurity information center that gathers information on vulnerabilities.
- **Why it matters**: It sets up a framework for government review before release at a time when models such as Anthropic’s Mythos, which find software vulnerabilities in large numbers, have raised security concerns in government and the financial sector. But the order cannot force anyone, so whether reviews actually happen depends on companies taking part. OpenAI welcomed it as important progress.
