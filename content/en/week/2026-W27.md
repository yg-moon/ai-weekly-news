## Korea

### 1. Samsung, SK hynix plan 800 trillion won chip plants in Honam

- **Date**: Jun 29–Jul 2 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On June 29, the government held a public report meeting at the presidential office on three mega projects for Korea’s great leap, and announced that companies would invest about 1,500 trillion won in chips, physical AI and AI data centers. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix will each invest 400 trillion won, 800 trillion won in total, in the Honam region in the southwest, and each will build two memory chip plants (fabs).

  Samsung Electronics Chairman Lee Jae-yong said Samsung is planning Gwangju, where it expects support such as power, water and workers, as a candidate site for a new chip complex. Plants for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) will be concentrated in the Chungcheong region, such as Cheonan and Onyang, and robot investment in Gumi, North Gyeongsang Province. SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won said SK plans to invest 1,000 trillion won in AI data centers and 1,100 trillion won in expanding chip supply. At a public report meeting for the Chungcheong region on July 2, Samsung announced it would invest 140 trillion won in Chungcheong.

  The government decided to finish the Yongin semiconductor general industrial complex (SK hynix) and national industrial complex (Samsung Electronics) 12 years and 7 years earlier than first planned. It will spend 81 trillion won to build an advanced packaging hub in Chungcheong, and make the southeast and the Daegu and North Gyeongsang regions hubs for the supply chain of materials, parts and equipment.

  The People Power Party criticized it as “suspected political interference that could shake the future of the industry.” At a Cabinet meeting on the 30th, President Lee Jae-myung said, “It is true that investment in the Honam region is a little large, but compared with the investment piled up over history, it is truly a drop in the bucket.”
- **Why it matters**: A new base for memory chip production is being built in the southwest, outside the Seoul area. The government said the reason was that power and water in the Seoul area have reached their limit. The two companies said supply is short compared with exploding demand. On the day of the announcement, foreigners were net sellers of 7.7557 trillion won on the main market, and Samsung Electronics shares fell 4.86%.

### 2. Coach Hong Myung-bo resigns after World Cup exit

- **Date**: Jun 29–Jul 3 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: The day after Korea was confirmed out of the group stage of the 2026 World Cup in North and Central America, head coach Hong Myung-bo said he would resign, at the national team’s training ground in Zapopan, Mexico. “I truly apologize to the people,” Hong said. “I am stepping down as national team head coach.” His contract ran until the Asian Cup in January 2027.

  In Group A, Korea beat the Czech Republic 2-1 but lost 0-1 to both Mexico and South Africa, finishing third in the group. It ranked 10th among the 12 third-place teams and did not make the round of 32, finishing 34th of 48 countries. It is the second time Hong has stepped down after a poor World Cup, after the 2014 World Cup in Brazil.

  On July 3, the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism said a K-Football Innovation Committee would start on the 6th. Minister of Culture, Sports and Tourism Choi Hwi-young and Park Ji-sung, a member of a FIFA committee, will co-chair it. Lee Young-pyo, Park Joo-ho, Korean Sport & Olympic Committee President Yoo Seung-min, and officials from the Korea Football Association and the K League will take part. It will discuss the structure of football administration and youth development, among other things.
- **Why it matters**: In the first tournament expanded to 48 countries, Korea did not even reach the round of 32. The body to reform Korean football will be led by the minister in charge as co-chair, not by the Korea Football Association.

### 3. Baejae High baseball team banned over “Starbucks” chant

- **Date**: Jun 29–Jul 4 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: On June 29, Baejae High School played Gwangju First High School at the Cheongnyonggi national high school baseball championship at Mokdong Stadium in Seoul. During the game, some Baejae players shouted at the opposing dugout, “Gotta go, gotta go, gotta go to Starbucks.” One student shouted, “Tank Day.” The chant recalls the “Tank Day” controversy, which mocked the May 18 Democratization Movement in Gwangju. Gwangju First High School protested, and the umpire warned Baejae High School.

  On the 30th, the Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education said it would investigate Baejae High School and apologized to the Gwangju First High School team and the people of Gwangju. On July 1, the sports fairness committee of the Korea Baseball Softball Association banned Baejae High School from national tournaments for six months. Baejae High School was automatically eliminated from the Cheongnyonggi tournament and cannot play in national tournaments until December. Penalties for individual coaches and players will be reviewed separately.

  On the 2nd, Lee Byung-tae, vice chair of the Regulatory Rationalization Committee, criticized the penalty on social media, writing that “5·18 has become sacred” and that it was “what North Korea looks like.” On the 4th, the presidential office gave a strict warning, saying, “This is improper conduct that could cause misunderstanding, unlike the government’s firm stance against hatred and mockery.” On the same day, Lee Byung-tae posted again, writing, “I believe that it is not the students’ chant but their punishment that mocks and belittles the Gwangju Democratization Movement.”
- **Why it matters**: After this incident, the association decided to require coaches to brief players on cheering before games, and to create a rule for heavier penalties when an act causes harm to society. With the vice chair of a government committee at the level of the prime minister’s office publicly criticizing the penalty, the students’ chant became an issue about a government official.

### 5. Court ends Homeplus rehabilitation process

- **Date**: Jul 3 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On July 3, Rehabilitation Division 4 of the Seoul Bankruptcy Court ended Homeplus’s rehabilitation process, one year and four months after it began in March last year. The court said the plan “cannot be carried out,” because at least about 200 billion won in operating funds was needed to carry out the rehabilitation plan and it had not been raised. The largest shareholder, MBK Partners, and the largest creditor, Meritz Financial Group, also failed to agree on how to raise the money.

  A revised plan Homeplus submitted on June 30 included cutting its large supermarkets to 67 and its workforce by 50%. Its supermarket chain, Homeplus Express, had already been sold. If Homeplus raises the money within 14 days and files an immediate appeal, the process could be reopened.
- **Why it matters**: If no new buyer or money is found, the only path left is liquidation. In that case, 12,000 employees and 1,000 indirectly employed workers, such as those managing parking and carts and cleaning, would lose their jobs, and tenant store owners, suppliers and investors in its short-term bonds would also suffer losses. MBK Partners bought Homeplus in 2015 for 7.2 trillion won.
- **Sources**: [Kookmin Ilbo](https://n.news.naver.com/article/005/0001858764) / [Chosun Ilbo](https://n.news.naver.com/article/023/0003985534)

### 1. Iran tightens control of the Strait of Hormuz again

- **Date**: Jun 30–Jul 5 (Tue–Sun)
- **What happened**: On July 4, the funeral began in Tehran for former Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, who was killed on February 28, the first day of the U.S.–Israeli attack. The funeral, delayed by the war, runs for six days through five cities in Iran and Iraq. The coffin lay for three days at Tehran’s Grand Mosalla Mosque, and leaders of Iraq, Pakistan, Armenia and Tajikistan and the heads of parliament of 12 countries paid their respects. At the funeral prayers on the 5th, there were chants calling for President Donald Trump to be killed. The new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has not appeared in public for three months, and he did not appear at the mosque that day either.

  Ships that had been stuck in the strait were leaving through a southern route on the Oman side backed by the U.S. On July 2, French President Emmanuel Macron said France would send a mine-clearing fleet to this route together with Britain, and Iran’s foreign ministry opposed it as unjust interference. On the 4th, at least eight ships turned back after direct warnings from the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.
- **Why it matters**: The memorandum set 60 days for nuclear talks, but as of June 30 the nuclear talks had not started. Iran has not given up on charging tolls and has started blocking the route backed by the West as well. International oil prices had come down from a peak of $125 a barrel to about $75.

### 2. Russian strikes on Kyiv kill at least 30

- **Date**: Jul 2 (Thu)
- **What happened**: Early on July 2, Russia attacked Kyiv and other places across Ukraine with about 500 drones and more than 70 missiles. At least 30 people were killed in Kyiv, the third deadliest attack on the capital since the war began. A nine-story apartment building on the left bank of the Dnipro River partly collapsed. More than 50,000 people spent the night in metro stations.

  Of the 77 missiles, 28 were ballistic missiles, and hypersonic Zircon cruise missiles were also used. Ukraine’s defense ministry said more than 90% of the cruise missiles and Shahed-type drones were shot down. Jet-powered drones flying at 500 km/h cannot be shot down by mobile fire teams, so surface-to-air missiles or fighter jets are needed.

  Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said the damage would not have been so great if allies had kept their promises to provide air defense missiles. The defense ministry asked about 40 countries to send the Patriot missiles they have on hand first.
- **Why it matters**: Because of the Iran war, some Patriot missiles meant for Ukraine were sent to the Middle East, and Ukraine lacks interceptor missiles to stop ballistic missiles. Ukraine has recently increased long-range drone attacks on Russian oil refineries, causing fuel shortages in many parts of Russia.

### 3. Venezuela earthquake deaths reach 3,342

- **Date**: Jun 29–Jul 5 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On June 24, earthquakes of magnitude 7.2 and 7.5 struck northern Venezuela one after the other. The death toll rose from 2,295 on July 1 to 3,342 on the 5th, and more than 16,700 people were injured. La Guaira State, on the coast north of the capital, Caracas, was hit hardest.

  An analysis of satellite data estimated that 58,870 buildings were damaged or destroyed. The International Organization for Migration said up to 6.8 million people could need shelter, drinking water and medical help. The UN agreed with the Venezuelan government to provide 10,000 body bags.

  As residents dug through rubble with their bare hands, anger grew that the government’s response had been slow and not enough. At an Independence Day event on the 5th, interim President Delcy Rodríguez said there would be no social unrest.
- **Why it matters**: It is the worst earthquake in Venezuela in more than 100 years. Tens of thousands of people are unaccounted for, so the death toll is expected to rise. A humanitarian crisis has come on top of an economic crisis in a country already struggling.

### 4. Supreme Court blocks order limiting birthright citizenship

- **Date**: Jun 30 (Tue)
- **What happened**: The U.S. Supreme Court ruled that automatic citizenship for almost every child born in the U.S. is guaranteed by the Constitution. An executive order signed by President Donald Trump on the first day of his second term had tried to block citizenship for children born to parents who entered illegally or were in the U.S. on temporary visas.

  Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion, joined by Justice Amy Coney Barrett and the three liberal justices. Roberts wrote that under the Constitution, the children of people in the country illegally or temporarily are also citizens at birth. Justice Brett Kavanaugh agreed with the outcome but based his view on a federal law from the 1950s instead of the Constitution. Justices Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito and Neil Gorsuch dissented.

  Trump called it a bad day for the country and said it could easily be fixed if Congress passed a law banning birthright citizenship.
- **Why it matters**: The Supreme Court confirmed birthright citizenship under the 14th Amendment through its reading of the Constitution. Kavanaugh based his view on a law, leaving room for Congress to change it. Children of temporary visa holders staying legally were also covered by the executive order.
- **Sources**: [CNN](https://us.cnn.com/2026/06/30/politics/takeaways-supreme-court-rebukes-trump-on-birthright-citizenship-barely) / [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/30/nx-s1-5876242/supreme-court-upholds-birthright-citizenship-rejecting-trumps-executive-order)

### 5. Europe heat wave: 2,025 extra deaths in France in a week

- **Date**: Jun 29–Jul 3 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On June 28, the director-general of the World Health Organization (WHO) said heat waves had caused more than 1,300 excess deaths in Europe since June 21. On July 3, France’s public health agency counted 2,025 more deaths, up 29.1%, in the hottest week, June 22 to 28, than in the week before. This is twice the first estimate, which covered only three days, and the agency said the number would rise.

  Belgium counted about 1,200 excess deaths from June 18 to 29, and the Netherlands about 480. Weekly deaths in Paris rose 62%. Most of the excess deaths were people aged 65 and over.

  By June 28, Germany (41.7°C), the Czech Republic (41.9°C) and Poland (40.5°C) had set new national temperature records, and Hungary and Slovakia also broke their national records. Italy’s Veneto region declared a state of emergency because of drought.
- **Why it matters**: More than two-thirds of Europeans experienced temperatures above 35°C. On June 26, World Weather Attribution (WWA) said this heat wave would have been almost impossible 50 years ago. The WHO director-general said Europe’s homes, workplaces and schools were not built for these temperatures.

## AI

### 1. U.S. lifts export controls on Mythos 5 and Fable 5

- **Date**: Jun 30–Jul 1 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: The U.S. Commerce Department removed the requirement that Anthropic get a license to offer Mythos 5 and Fable 5 abroad. On July 1, Anthropic reopened Fable 5 to users around the world. It did not give a schedule for offering it on AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry.

  On June 12, the government had put the two models on its list of export-controlled technology. Foreign nationals could not use them without special approval, which meant that foreign employees of business customers and of Anthropic itself could not use them. Because the rules were hard to follow, Anthropic stopped its public service entirely. It began with a jailbreak technique reported by Amazon researchers.

  Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick said Anthropic had agreed to find and respond to security risks first, to work with the government on the release process for Mythos, Fable and later models, and to report misuse to the government. Anthropic trained a new classifier that blocks more than 99% of that jailbreak technique, and blocked requests are passed to Opus 4.8. It said it would let the government access and test future models before release. Mythos 5 is still offered only to organizations approved by the government.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. government used export controls to block the general release of a private AI model for about three weeks, then lifted them through an agreement with the company. OpenAI’s newest model was also released first only to government-approved organizations. At two companies in a row, the government decided when and to whom a model would be released.

### 2. Anthropic launches Claude Sonnet 5

- **Date**: Jun 30 (Tue)
- **What happened**: Anthropic released Claude Sonnet 5, a mid-size model. It highlights the ability to make plans, use tools such as browsers and terminals, and keep working on tasks on its own. It became the default model for the Free and Pro plans.

  Until August 31, the price is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, rising to $3 and $15 after that. It is cheaper than Opus 4.8, OpenAI’s GPT-5.5 and Google’s Gemini 3.1 Pro. On an agent coding benchmark, it scored 63.2%, higher than the previous model, Sonnet 4.6 (58.1%), and lower than Opus 4.8 (69.2%). On a knowledge work benchmark, it slightly beat Opus 4.8.

  Its rates of behaviors such as helping with misuse, deception, hallucination and flattery are lower than Sonnet 4.6’s. Anthropic said its ability at dangerous cybersecurity tasks is much lower than the Opus models’.
- **Why it matters**: With even mid-priced models presenting agent work as a basic feature, competition is shifting to how cheaply the same work can be done. OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol came out as a preview a week earlier, and Google’s Gemini 3.5 Flash also highlighted agent features.

### 3. Cloudflare to block multi-purpose AI crawlers by default

- **Date**: Jul 1 (Wed)
- **What happened**: Cloudflare announced that from September 15 it will block, by default, “multi-purpose” crawlers that do search, AI agent work and training together on pages that carry ads. It will apply to new customers, new sites created by existing customers, and all existing free customers. Site owners can allow them by changing the setting.

  Cloudflare pointed out that “the world’s largest search engine” gets access to about twice as much information as other AI companies. It said this is because sites must also allow AI use if they want to appear in search. Google has replied that it has a separate crawler for opting out of training, but its search crawler is also used for AI features such as AI Overviews.

  Cloudflare is also expanding its existing service that charges a fee for each crawl into Pay Per Use, which charges when content is actually used.
- **Why it matters**: If the default setting changes, AI companies will have to split their crawlers by purpose or pay for content to reach these sites. CEO Matthew Prince said more than half of internet traffic now comes from bots, not people.
- **Sources**: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/01/cloudflares-new-policy-pushes-ai-companies-to-pay-for-publishers-content/) / [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/960795/cloudflare-is-cracking-down-on-multi-purpose-crawlers)

### 5. Anthropic releases Claude Science for research

- **Date**: Jun 30 (Tue)
- **What happened**: Anthropic released Claude Science, which lets researchers do computational science in one environment. It is not a new model and runs on existing Claude models such as Opus 4.8. It connects to about 60 scientific databases and comes with tools for genomics, protein structure and chemistry.

  A main agent hands work to sub-agents, and a separate checking agent verifies citations and calculations. For every figure, it keeps the code that made it, the environment it ran in and the conversation record. It can run on a lab’s own infrastructure, so data does not have to be sent to Anthropic’s servers.

  At the same event, Eric Kauderer-Abrams, head of life sciences, said Anthropic would develop treatments for neglected diseases itself. Which diseases it would target was not disclosed.
- **Why it matters**: Like the coding tool Claude Code, it moves Anthropic into work tools for specific industries. If it develops drugs, it will compete with the drug companies it sells software to. The checking agent is the same model, so it is not an independent fact check.
- **Sources**: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/30/anthropics-claude-science-bets-on-workflow-not-a-new-model-to-win-over-scientists/) / [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/961311/anthropic-claude-science-ai-drug-development)
