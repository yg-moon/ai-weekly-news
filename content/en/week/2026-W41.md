## Korea

### 1. Military blames North Korean-laid mines and removes them

- **Date**: Oct 5–9 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On October 5, the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) announced the final findings on the mine blast in the western Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) on September 21. It concluded that South Korean soldiers were hurt by North Korean plastic anti-personnel mines. North Korea had laid them south of the Military Demarcation Line (MDL), using its work to “fortify the border” as cover. The blast points were about 10 m south of the MDL. Tests by the National Forensic Service showed that fragments from the soldiers’ bodies matched North Korean plastic anti-personnel mines. The United Nations Command also judged that the mines had been placed by hand more than 12 months earlier. Around 12:51 p.m. the same day, the military blew up the North Korean minefield south of the MDL with explosives. It said that if North Korea does not clear them itself, it will remove all mines that have crossed the MDL.

  In a statement on the 6th, North Korea’s Foreign Ministry called the findings “a self-staged farce” and called the mine removal “a grave provocation.” It said it would respond at once and strongly “if military boots violate our territory by even 1 mm.” At a parliamentary audit on the 7th, Defense Minister Kang Shin-cheol said he believes North Korea has buried hundreds of mines south of the MDL. He said the military is also watching signs that North Korean fences have crossed the MDL in some sections. He added that armed North Korean patrols come down to about 10 m south of the MDL every day, and South Korean troops fire warning shots each time.

  On the 8th (local time), Cha Ji-hoon, South Korea’s ambassador to the UN, told the UN General Assembly’s First Committee that it was a clear violation of the armistice and demanded an apology from North Korea. It was the first time South Korea raised North Korea’s responsibility for the incident at the UN. North Korea’s mission called the work a legitimate exercise of sovereignty and said it would never discuss it with South Korea.
- **Why it matters**: It is the first physical response by the South Korean military to a North Korean mine blast. The two Koreas draw the MDL baseline differently. There are worries that a clash could break out when South Korean troops clearing mines meet North Korean troops doing fortification work near the MDL. Kim Yo Jong, director of the general affairs department of North Korea’s Workers’ Party, issued five statements criticizing South Korea in the 10 days from September 30.

### 2. Chief Justice calls nomination interference unconstitutional

- **Date**: Oct 6–7 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: On October 6, Chief Justice Jo Hee-de told a parliamentary audit by the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee, “The National Assembly and the President cannot be involved in any way in the Chief Justice’s recommendation.” The Democratic Party had called him as a general witness, but he refused to take the witness oath, citing judicial independence. When asked to testify, he took out a prepared paper and spent about six minutes explaining why he had refused the presidential office’s request to recommend a new Supreme Court justice candidate. In doing so, he used the word “unconstitutional” four times. It was the first time since the 1987 constitutional system that a chief justice answered questions at a parliamentary audit.

  Jo read out Article 104(2) of the Constitution, which says Supreme Court justices are recommended by the chief justice, approved by the National Assembly and appointed by the president. He said, “Interfering with the Chief Justice’s recommendation is an unconstitutional act that goes against the division of powers set out in the Constitution and against judicial independence.” He said past consultations with the presidential office on candidates were voluntary cooperation with no basis in the Constitution. He added that the Constitution does not limit recommendations made in writing. On August 18, he had recommended Son Bong-gi, a senior judge at the Daegu District Court, in writing without consulting the presidential office. He refused the office’s August 28 request for a new recommendation on September 22.

  The presidential office repeated that it wants a new recommendation soon. On the 7th, Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok urged Jo to step down, and some party leaders and lawmakers raised impeachment.
- **Why it matters**: Neither the presidential office nor the chief justice is backing down, so the vacancy on the Supreme Court is likely to last longer. For about 50 days since August, the office’s requests and Jo’s refusals have repeated, and Jo has now stated his reasons formally in the National Assembly. The seat of former Justice Noh Tae-ak, who retired in March, had been empty for 218 days as of the 7th, the longest vacancy ever. The presidential office and the Democratic Party have ruled out impeachment and a Constitutional Court case over the powers of state bodies. Some expect the vacancy to last until Jo’s term ends in June next year.

### 3. Samsung quarterly profit tops 100 trillion won, a first

- **Date**: Oct 8 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On October 8, Samsung Electronics reported preliminary third-quarter consolidated results: sales of 195 trillion won and operating profit of 107.4 trillion won. Compared with the same period last year, sales rose 126.6% and operating profit rose 782.5%. It was the first time any Korean company, or any global tech company, has passed 100 trillion won in quarterly operating profit. It was also the fourth record quarter in a row since the fourth quarter of last year. The profit was larger than Nvidia’s operating profit of $63.73 billion (about 85 trillion won) for the May–July 2026 quarter. Samsung’s operating margin was 55.1%.

  The results came from higher demand and prices for memory chips, driven by investment in artificial intelligence (AI) data centers. Brokerages estimate that profits from the memory business covered losses in the foundry, System LSI, and mobile and home appliance units. The growth rate of operating profit from the previous quarter slowed: 184.6% in the first quarter, 56.5% in the second and 20.0% in the third.
- **Why it matters**: No company except Saudi Arabia’s state oil company Aramco, in the second quarter of 2022, has ever posted quarterly operating profit in the 100 trillion won range. Samsung’s profit for one quarter is close to its combined total for the four years from 2022 to 2025 (126.3 trillion won).

### 4. Nuri succeeds on fifth launch with a record 15 satellites

- **Date**: Oct 7 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On October 7 at 12:25 p.m., Korea’s Nuri rocket lifted off from the Naro Space Center in Goheung, South Jeolla-Gwangju. It put its main payload, five small satellites in a cluster (numbers 2 to 6), into the target orbit. By 5:52 p.m. that day, all five had made first contact with ground stations. Of the 10 secondary CubeSats, nine separated. It was the first time Nuri lifted off at its planned time with no delay.

  Nuri carried 15 satellites, more than the 13 on its fourth launch last November, the most ever. The satellites had to be placed within 15 km of a target altitude of 570 km. This was more precise than the fourth launch, which allowed 35 km at 600 km. A new low-shock separation device that uses less explosive was used to release the satellites.
- **Why it matters**: After the first launch failed in 2021, Nuri has now succeeded four times in a row since the second launch in 2022, raising its overall success rate to 80%. The government plans to raise the success rate into the 90% range needed for commercial launches. After the sixth and seventh launches, it plans to launch at least twice a year.

### 5. AI-driven hacking confirmed at 12 financial firms

- **Date**: Oct 5–6 (Mon–Tue)
- **What happened**: Hacking attacks that used artificial intelligence (AI) were found to have hit not only commercial and savings banks but also internet-only banks, insurers and brokerages. On October 6, the Financial Supervisory Service said it had identified 28 internet addresses (IPs) used in the attacks and shared them with the financial sector. The addresses were spread across 12 countries, including the U.S., Japan, Hong Kong and Singapore. KakaoBank and K Bank were also attacked from the same addresses but said no data leaked. Data leaks were confirmed at seven firms: Shinhan, KB Kookmin, Hana and BNK Busan banks, Yegaram and Welcome savings banks, and Hyundai Capital. Including firms known to have been attacked, the total is at least 12. Similar traces of access were also found at one or two insurers and one or two brokerages.

  The data leaks took place from September 27 to 30. At Shinhan Bank, 25,727 records of personal data were taken, including names, contact details, annual income and loan limits. The hackers are believed to have used AI to automate brute-force attacks on weakly protected systems instead of customer banking services. These included a lookup service for loan agents and an internal system for staff. Traces of “ARTEX,” a Chinese-language AI penetration testing tool posted on GitHub in July, were found at the attack sites.

  On the 6th, the National Office of Investigation at the Korean National Police Agency opened a case for violation of the Information and Communications Network Act. It set up a special team of 28 people in four units. This came six days after the hacking became known. The police asked the Financial Services Commission for a legal interpretation on whether the case must be reported to the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency.
- **Why it matters**: An AI tool that anyone can download was used to take data from several banks. In one test, the tool was given the address of a target server. It broke through two weak points in 43 seconds and produced a report in four minutes, and the fee was 20 won. Financial authorities sent the attack IPs to about 500 firms for checks. They see weak management of internal support systems, not the main customer networks, as the cause.

## AI

### 1. OpenAI releases more than 700 papers on open math problems

- **Date**: Oct 6–9 (Tue–Fri)
- **What happened**: On October 6, OpenAI released about 400 mathematical results produced by an unreleased frontier model. They came in more than 700 manuscripts posted on GitHub. The open problems span combinatorics, geometry, number theory, theoretical computer science, algebra, topology, probability and mathematical physics. OpenAI said each result used on average the equivalent of three hours of ChatGPT Pro reasoning. In September, it had said the model solved more than 100 long-standing open problems.

  The level of checking varies. Of 719 manuscripts, 300, or about 42%, have been formalized in Lean, a proof assistant language. Only 10 manuscripts included the model’s chain of thought. A group of mathematicians (AGMAI) issued guidelines in late September. Its first request was to stop testing advanced math problems on proprietary models. But OpenAI said it uses open research problems to evaluate its proprietary models. Researchers at the University of Cambridge and King’s College London found at least two gaps between the natural language proof and the Lean code in OpenAI’s solution to a problem derived from the Navier-Stokes equations.
- **Why it matters**: So many results came out at once that mathematicians said it could take years just to understand them. Mathematician Terence Tao criticized that the people giving the problems to AI do not understand the results well enough to answer questions or engage with the field. Proofs that are not formalized must be checked by humans one by one, so the burden of checking has moved to mathematicians.
