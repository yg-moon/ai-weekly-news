## Korea

### 1. Polling stations short of ballots rise to 140

- **Date**: Jun 8–13 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: On June 8, the National Election Commission said that on election day in the June 3 local elections, extra ballots had to be sent to 140 of the country’s 14,288 polling stations. That is 73 more than it announced on the 5th. Extra ballots were actually used at 91 stations, and at 26 stations voting stopped for a while because ballots ran out. Commission data obtained by People Power Party lawmaker Kim Min-jeon showed that 50 polling stations were short a total of 4,726 ballots, and all 17 stations that were short more than 100 were in Seoul.

  On the same day, Chief Justice Jo Hee-de withdrew his nomination of Noh Tae-ak as chair of the commission, and standing commissioner Wi Cheol-hwan became acting chair. The dismissal of the commission’s secretary general was also approved. The commission set up a fact-finding committee of six outside members to work from the 10th to the 19th. On the 10th, it was found that in the North Jeolla education superintendent race, the count from one polling station had been entered twice in place of another station’s count, leaving out the votes of 1,104 voters. The commission said this did not change who won.

  On the 10th, the Seoul Eastern District Court carried out an on-site inspection to preserve evidence at polling station 2 in Jamsil 7-dong, Songpa District, where voting had gone on until 10 p.m. But the box for ballots marked “1,900 printed,” which the court had ordered preserved the day before, was missing, so the inspection could not be done. The commission said it did not have the box.

  People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyuk called for new elections nationwide, while Seoul Mayor-elect Oh Se-hoon pushed back, saying there should be no new elections unless the violations were serious enough to change the result. The protest blocking the vote-counting center at Olympic Park went on, and on the 10th, President Lee Jae-myung warned that protesters confining and assaulting police officers would not be tolerated.
- **Why it matters**: The ballot shortage came from the commission cutting the number of election-day ballots it sent out based on early voting turnout, and at 26 polling stations voters could not vote on time. The commission’s chair and secretary general both left, and with the missing evidence box and the error in entering the count, the whole running of the election came under investigation.

### 2. KOSPI plunges 8%, triggering a circuit breaker

- **Date**: Jun 8–10 (Mon–Wed)
- **What happened**: On June 8, right after the market opened, the KOSPI fell 8.80% to 7,442.73. At 9:03 a.m., after a drop of more than 8% from the day before lasted for one minute, a level-1 circuit breaker stopped trading on the main market for 20 minutes. It was the ninth circuit breaker ever and the third this year, after two in March caused by the U.S.–Iran war. The KOSPI closed down 8.29% that day.

  Early in the day, Samsung Electronics fell below 300,000 won and SK hynix below 2 million won. Foreigners were net sellers for the 21st trading day in a row. On the 5th, chip stocks had plunged across the board on the New York stock market, and the won-dollar exchange rate went above 1,560 won.

  On the 9th, the KOSPI rose 8.18% and got back above 8,000, but on the 10th it opened down more than 2% again. Forced sales of stocks bought with borrowed money came to 166.1 billion won on the 5th, 139.1 billion won on the 8th and 169.6 billion won on the 9th, topping 100 billion won three days in a row. It was the first time this happened three days in a row since October 2023.
- **Why it matters**: As the index swung 8% a day, individual investors who had borrowed to invest saw their shares forcibly sold at the daily lower limit price. Margin loans, money borrowed to invest, reached 37.929 trillion won on the 9th, close to the record level of May 29.

### 3. Yoon gets 30 years over drones sent to Pyongyang

- **Date**: Jun 12 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On June 12, Criminal Division 36 of the Seoul Central District Court (presiding judge Lee Jung-yeop) sentenced former President Yoon Suk Yeol to 30 years in prison on charges of benefiting the enemy and abuse of power. Former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun was also sentenced to 30 years, former Defense Counterintelligence Commander Yeo In-hyung to 15 years, and former Drone Operations Commander Kim Yong-dae to three years in prison, suspended for five years.

  Yoon and the others were accused of sending drones to Pyongyang several times in October and November 2024 to provoke North Korea and create a reason to declare martial law. The court said, “It was deliberately creating an emergency for the power to declare martial law,” and found that Yoon and Kim Yong-hyun had planned the operation together from the start. Yoon’s side has argued that he did not order or approve the drone operation.
- **Why it matters**: In February, Yoon was sentenced to life in prison for leading an insurrection, and with this ruling, the first-trial court also found that he used a military operation against North Korea to bring about martial law. All hearings were closed to the public, on the grounds of military secrets.

### 5. Defense ministry to break up counterintelligence command

- **Date**: Jun 10 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On June 10, Defense Minister Ahn Gyu-back announced a plan to dismantle the Defense Counterintelligence Command and reorganize its functions. A new Defense Counterintelligence Headquarters, to be set up by the end of July, will handle counterintelligence, defense industry intelligence and cybersecurity. The ministry’s Investigation Headquarters will take over national security investigations and joint investigation powers under martial law, and a new Defense Security Support Group will handle security audits within the military.

  The command’s work of monitoring people’s activities, gathering intelligence for personnel decisions and collecting reputations, which was seen as the basis for its becoming a power center inside the military, will be abolished. Its staff of more than 3,000 will be cut by more than 1,000. The plan follows the recommendation made in January by a special advisory committee of civilians, government and military.
- **Why it matters**: The agency that sent troops to the National Assembly and the National Election Commission and ran teams to arrest politicians during the martial law of December 3 is being dismantled. Since it began in 1977 as the Armed Forces Security Command, it has kept its powers while changing only its name, later to the Defense Security Command and then the Defense Counterintelligence Command. Ahn said, “Even if an unjust power appears, there must be no room to turn the counterintelligence agency into a political tool.”
- **Sources**: [Hankook Ilbo](https://n.news.naver.com/article/469/0000935697) / [Yonhap](https://n.news.naver.com/article/001/0016131426) / [Hankyoreh](https://n.news.naver.com/article/028/0002809020)

## World

### 1. U.S., Iran agree to end war five days after strikes resume

- **Date**: Jun 8–14 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On June 14 (local time), the U.S., Iran and Pakistani mediators said they had reached a deal to end the war. U.S. President Donald Trump said the deal with Iran was done and that he would lift the naval blockade around the Strait of Hormuz. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif said the two sides had agreed to stop military operations on all fronts, including Lebanon, immediately and permanently, and that a signing ceremony would be held in Switzerland on Friday. Trump said that after the signing he would open the strait to clear mines. The nuclear issue was not part of that day’s announcement.

  The week before the deal began with the biggest clash since the April ceasefire. On the 8th, Israel and Iran traded missiles and air strikes, then stopped when Trump demanded it. The next day, a U.S. Apache helicopter patrolling the Strait of Hormuz was shot down, and the U.S. struck Iranian air defenses and radar sites near the strait. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps hit back, targeting U.S. bases in Bahrain and Jordan, and Kuwait also shot down attacks. On the 11th, the U.S. carried on its strikes for a second day, then called off further strikes and said a deal was close.

  In Iran, hardliners called the deal a surrender because it did not guarantee the lifting of sanctions or control of the strait. The negotiators replied that the nuclear issue had been moved to 60 days of follow-up talks and that Iran and Oman would be able to charge transit fees in the strait.
- **Why it matters**: Right after the war began in late February, Iran all but closed the Strait of Hormuz, cutting off a key route for the world’s oil and gas for more than three months, and the U.S. responded by blockading Iranian ports. The deal is the first time both sides said together that they would end the war, but the signing, mine clearing and nuclear talks still lie ahead. Israel is not a party to the deal and struck Lebanon on the same day.

### 3. Magnitude 7.8 earthquake hits Mindanao, Philippines

- **Date**: Jun 8 (Mon)
- **What happened**: At 7:37 a.m. on June 8 (local time), a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck off the coast of Sarangani Province in the Philippines. The epicenter was 13 km southwest of General Santos City. Philippine authorities are checking early reports of 37 people killed, 479 injured and four missing. About 88,000 people were affected, and 20,690 left their homes.

  Buildings collapsed and several hospitals were damaged, so the injured were treated in tents. Tsunami warnings were issued for the southern Philippines, northern Indonesia and the Malaysian state of Sabah, then lifted after more than six hours.
- **Why it matters**: The earthquake struck on the morning of the first day of the new school year, and school buildings also collapsed. The shaking was felt strongly as far as Indonesia’s Sulawesi Island, 420 km away.

### 4. Xi Jinping visits North Korea for first time in seven years

## AI

### 1. U.S. orders Fable 5 and Mythos 5 blocked

- **Date**: Jun 9–13 (Tue–Sat)
- **What happened**: On June 9, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5, the first public release of a Mythos model. In high-risk areas such as cybersecurity, biology, chemistry and distillation, it blocks answers and passes requests to Opus 4.8. Anthropic gave Mythos 5 to approved organizations. Fable 5 costs $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens, twice the price of Opus 4.8. Anthropic also started a new policy of keeping all traffic for 30 days.

  At 5:21 p.m. (U.S. Eastern Time) on the 12th, the U.S. government issued an export control order, citing national security, to keep foreigners from using the two models. It covered all foreign nationals inside and outside the U.S., including Anthropic’s own foreign employees. To comply, Anthropic blocked both models for all users. Other models were not affected.

  Anthropic said the government gave no specific basis for its concerns. It said the jailbreak risk it pointed to was a narrow, non-general case passed on by word of mouth that is also possible with other public models such as GPT-5.5. It said that applying this standard across the industry would stop the release of every frontier model.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. government used export controls to pull back a commercial AI model that had already been released. In India, which Anthropic and OpenAI have called their largest market after the U.S., there were calls to rely less on American models. The action showed in practice that the U.S. government can decide who gets to use frontier AI models.

### 2. Apple unveils AI Siri at WWDC

- **Date**: Jun 8 (Mon)
- **What happened**: At the keynote of its Worldwide Developers Conference (WWDC) on June 8, Apple unveiled Siri AI, which turns Siri into a conversational AI assistant, along with a new Siri app. It answers based on the latest information on the web, the mail, calendar and contacts on the device, and what is on the screen, and it writes in the user’s usual style. It can also operate other apps to some extent. A beta will come out this year.

  The new cloud AI model is based on Google’s Gemini. Apple’s Private Cloud Compute now runs not only in Apple’s own data centers but also on Google Cloud, using NVIDIA GPUs and Intel CPUs. - **Why it matters**: Apple delivered the Siri overhaul it promised in 2024 two years late, and handed its core model to its rival Google. Apple presents privacy as what sets it apart, but processing that used to run only on Apple’s own chips has now spread to an outside supply chain.

### 5. Seattle pauses new data centers for a year

- **Date**: Jun 9–10 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: The Seattle City Council in the U.S. passed an emergency one-year moratorium on new large data centers. It came two months after four companies proposed building five large data centers in the city. The five would have a peak power demand of 369 megawatts, about a third of Seattle’s average daily power use.

  At a public hearing, residents overwhelmingly supported the moratorium, and current Amazon engineers also testified in favor. During the moratorium, the city will study how data centers affect power rates, water, land, jobs and public health.
- **Why it matters**: The city where Amazon has its headquarters used its rules to halt the building of AI data centers. Opposition to data centers over water use and power rates, which has been growing across the U.S., was put into force through a city ordinance.
