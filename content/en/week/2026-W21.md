## Korea

### 1. Samsung reaches tentative wage deal on eve of strike

- **Date**: May 18–22 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On the night of May 20, after talks at the Gyeonggi Regional Employment and Labor Office chaired by Minister of Employment and Labor Kim Young-hoon, Samsung Electronics and its unions signed a tentative agreement on 2026 wages. Until that morning, a second round of mediation by the National Labor Relations Commission had failed over the size of the bonus pool and how to split it between business units. The unions had announced a general strike for the 21st. Right after the agreement, the unions put the strike on hold.

  The agreement creates a new “special business performance bonus” for the chip (DS) division. The pool is 10.5% of business results as defined by both sides, there is no cap on the payout rate, and the full after-tax amount is paid in company shares. One-third of the shares can be sold right away, and the rest cannot be sold for one year and two years. The deal runs for 10 years, and the bonus is paid only in years when the DS division’s operating profit reaches 200 trillion won (2026–2028) or 100 trillion won (2029–2035). Wages rise 6.2%, and the finished-products (DX) division gets company shares worth 6 million won. One estimate found that if operating profit is 300 trillion won, a memory division employee would receive about 600 million won including existing bonuses (based on a salary of 100 million won).

  The conflict continued before and after the deal. On the 18th, the Suwon District Court accepted most of the company’s request for an injunction. It ordered that disaster prevention, power and chemical supply facilities be kept running at normal levels even during a strike, and that the unions pay 100 million won for each day they break the order. On the same day, President Lee Jae-myung said, “Companies’ right to manage must be respected as much as labor rights,” in a remark aimed at the unions’ demand that 15% of operating profit be paid as bonuses. In the union members’ vote on the deal, which began on the 22nd, employees of the DX division who were angry about the gap in bonuses campaigned to reject it. The vote runs until the morning of the 27th.
- **Why it matters**: The deal sets a fixed share of the chip business’s results as the bonus pool for 10 years with no cap. With bonuses differing nearly tenfold between divisions, the conflict between the company and the unions turned into a conflict among union members.

### 2. Starbucks Korea’s May 18 ‘Tank Day’ event sparks outrage

- **Date**: May 18–24 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On May 18, the anniversary of the May 18 Democratization Movement, Starbucks Korea used phrases such as “Tank Day” and “tak on the desk” in a post promoting tumblers. As criticism spread that it mocked the May 18 movement and the 1987 death under torture of student activist Park Jong-chul, the company stopped the event and issued an apology. On the same day, Shinsegae Group Chairman Chung Yong-jin dismissed Starbucks Korea CEO Son Jeong-hyun and the executive who planned the event. On the 19th, he issued a public apology saying, “On behalf of the group, I bow my head and apologize.”

  On the 18th, President Lee Jae-myung wrote, “I am angry at the inhuman, shameless behavior of low-grade merchants,” and “Matching moral, administrative, legal and political responsibility must follow.” On the 23rd, he criticized Starbucks Korea for releasing a “Siren” mug on April 16, 2024, the 10th anniversary of the Sewol ferry disaster, calling it “an immoral act by vicious merchants.”

  On the 20th, a civic group filed a complaint against Chung and Son on charges of insult and defamation, and the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency questioned the group on the 22nd. The justice ministry told the Supreme Prosecutors’ Office to report its purchases of Starbucks products with this year’s budget. The boycott spread to government offices.
- **Why it matters**: One marketing phrase led to the CEO’s dismissal, an apology from the group’s chairman, repeated criticism from the president and a police investigation. It even led a government agency to check its purchases of one company’s products.

### 4. Second round of high oil price relief payments begins

- **Date**: May 18 (Mon)
- **What happened**: At 9 a.m. on May 18, the government began taking applications for the second round of relief payments for damage from high oil prices. The second round covers the bottom 70% by income, about 36 million people, more than 10 times the roughly 3.23 million in the first round, which went to groups such as basic livelihood recipients and single-parent families. People were selected based on the health insurance premiums charged to each household in March this year. A single-person household with an employee plan qualifies if its premium is 130,000 won a month or less (annual income of about 43.4 million won or less). Wealthy people whose property tax base totals more than 1.2 billion won or whose financial income is more than 20 million won are left out.

  The amount depends on where people live: 100,000 won in the Seoul metropolitan area, 150,000 won outside it, 200,000 won in “priority support areas” among regions with shrinking populations, and 250,000 won in “special support areas.” Applications run until July 3, and in the first week, people apply on set weekdays based on the last digit of their birth year. Payments not used by August 31 expire. On the first day, lines formed at community service centers, which had not happened in the first round, and people left out of the program kept filing objections.
- **Why it matters**: Cash-like support to ease living costs pushed up by high oil prices was widened to 70% of the population. Complaints that health insurance premiums, the basis for payment, do not match people’s real situations began on the first day.
- **Sources**: [Yonhap](https://n.news.naver.com/article/001/0016082372) / [Kyunghyang Shinmun](https://n.news.naver.com/article/032/0003446376)

### 5. Supreme Court rules cosmetic tattoos are not medical acts

- **Date**: May 21 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On May 21, the full bench of the Supreme Court (presided over by Chief Justice Jo Hee-de) unanimously overturned the fines given to two tattoo artists charged under the Medical Service Act for tattooing without a medical license. It sent the cases back to the Seoul Western District Court and the Suwon District Court. One had done a scalp tattoo at a hair salon in 2020, and the other had tattooed letters on someone’s arm in 2019. At the first and second trials, they were fined 1.5 million won and 1 million won.

  For 34 years, since a 1992 ruling that eyebrow tattooing is a medical act, the courts had punished tattooing by people who are not medical professionals. In this ruling, the court said, “Tattooing was widely practiced even before medical professionals appeared and developed as an independent field separate from medicine.” It also found that making people get a medical license to do cosmetic tattoos is in effect a ban on tattooing by non-medical people, which blocks their chance to choose their job.

  The Tattooist Act, which allows non-medical people to tattoo, passed the National Assembly last September and takes effect next October. The court added that even before the law takes effect, tattooists who injure someone through carelessness can still be punished under laws such as the Criminal Act.
- **Why it matters**: The basis for criminal punishment that remained until the Tattooist Act takes effect is gone. The court said what counts as a medical act can be judged again as the medical environment and public views change.

## World

### 1. U.S. and Iran near deal on memorandum to end the war

- **Date**: May 18–24 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On May 24, U.S. President Donald Trump said a memorandum of understanding to end the war with Iran had been largely negotiated. Under the reported plan, the ceasefire would be extended by 60 days, and during that time Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz, clear mines and not charge tolls. The U.S. would lift the blockade of Iranian ports it has kept since April 13, release up to $20 billion in frozen assets and ease sanctions. Talks on the nuclear program would begin on June 5 in Pakistan, and fighting would stop on all fronts, including Israel’s offensive in Lebanon.

  The last point of dispute is a U.S. demand to tie the release of at least $6 billion held in Qatar to progress on Iran handing over its highly enriched uranium. A source on the Iranian side said Iran had not agreed to hand over the uranium, and Iranian media reported that the strait would stay under Iranian management. Trump said the blockade would stay in place until a deal is signed and that he would not rush.

  Only a week earlier, new strikes had been under discussion. On the 18th, Trump said he had put off an attack on Iran planned for the next day at the request of the leaders of Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. At the same time, he ordered the military to be ready to launch a large-scale attack at any time. Republican hardliners called the draft deal a disaster.
- **Why it matters**: The war, which began on February 28, turned from the edge of new strikes toward a deal to extend the ceasefire. If the deal is signed, the Strait of Hormuz, a route for the world’s oil, will reopen. But the nuclear issue, including what to do with the highly enriched uranium, was pushed into 60 days of talks.

### 2. Suspected Ebola cases in Congo pass 900

- **Date**: May 18–24 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: Suspected Ebola cases, centered on Ituri province in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, reached 904 on May 24. Suspected cases rose from about 200 when the outbreak was announced on the 15th to 500 on the 19th and about 750 on the 22nd. Death counts differ by agency: the World Health Organization (WHO) said more than 170 on the 22nd, and the Congolese government said 119 suspected deaths on the 24th. The cause is the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment, and it kills 30–50% of those infected.

  The WHO rated the risk within Congo as “very high” and the risk of global spread as low. It also said the virus may have been spreading for months before it was found. In the east, residents set fire to two treatment centers, and authorities banned funerals and gatherings of more than 50 people.

  On the 18th, the U.S. put in place entry restrictions that block travel to and from Congo. Local aid groups said the U.S. withdrawal from the WHO, the dismantling of the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID) and cuts to health aid had weakened disease monitoring and delayed the discovery of the outbreak.
- **Why it matters**: Suspected cases quadrupled in 10 days, and there is no vaccine that can be used. The WHO is considering using experimental vaccines and treatments, while clashes between armed groups and distrust among residents are getting in the way of disease control.

### 3. Russia strikes Kyiv with Oreshnik missile

- **Date**: May 23–24 (Sat–Sun)
- **What happened**: From the night of May 23 to early on the 24th, Russia attacked Ukraine with 600 drones and 90 missiles, 36 of them ballistic. President Volodymyr Zelensky said Kyiv was the main target. Four people were killed in Kyiv and the surrounding region, and about 100 were injured across the country. Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko said every district of the city had been damaged, and water facilities, markets, dozens of apartment buildings and schools were damaged or destroyed.

  Russia’s defense ministry confirmed that it had used the Oreshnik, a hypersonic ballistic missile that can carry either nuclear or conventional warheads. It was the third time it has been used in the war, and it hit Bila Tserkva in the Kyiv region. Russia said the attack was revenge for Ukrainian attacks on civilian sites in Russian territory. On the 22nd, Russian President Vladimir Putin had said Ukrainian drones attacked a dormitory and a school in Starobilsk in the occupied Luhansk region, and had promised revenge.
- **Why it matters**: Russia again used the Oreshnik, which flies more than 10 times the speed of sound and is hard to shoot down, to attack the area around the capital. European leaders condemned the attack, and Russia and Ukraine each blamed the other for attacks on civilian sites.

### 5. Gas explosion at Chinese coal mine kills 82

- **Date**: May 22 (Fri)
- **What happened**: At 7:29 p.m. on May 22, a gas explosion at the Liushenyu coal mine in Qinyuan County, Changzhi, Shanxi Province, China, killed at least 82 people and sent more than 120 to the hospital. There were 247 people underground at the time. The death toll was first reported as 90, then corrected to 82 by local authorities on the 23rd, and two people are missing.

  On the 23rd, local authorities said their investigation found that the mine’s operator had committed serious illegal acts. The operator’s managers were taken into custody, and the State Council sent an investigation team. President Xi Jinping ordered an all-out rescue effort and that those responsible be held to account under the law.
- **Why it matters**: It is the deadliest mine accident in China in 17 years. A day after the explosion, authorities officially confirmed that the operator had broken the law.

### 1. Google I/O unveils Gemini 3.5 Flash and agents

- **Date**: May 19 (Tue)
- **What happened**: At its I/O conference on May 19, Google unveiled a new model, Gemini 3.5 Flash. Google said it is the company’s strongest model for coding and autonomous agent tasks. It said the model beats the higher-tier 3.1 Pro on most coding, agent and multimodal reasoning benchmarks and is four times faster than other top models, and that an optimized version is 12 times faster at the same quality. On stage, Google showed several agents splitting up the work to build an operating system from scratch, and it also released Antigravity 2.0, a tool for building agents.

  Google also announced Gemini Spark, a personal agent. It runs 24 hours a day on a dedicated virtual machine in Google Cloud, connects directly to Workspace apps such as Gmail and Google Docs, and handles long tasks for the user.

  Search is changing more than at any time in 25 years. From this summer, “information agents” set up by users will track changes on the web 24 hours a day and send alerts.
- **Why it matters**: Google is moving AI from chatbots that answer questions to agents that work for hours on people’s behalf. As links are pushed further down in search results, people will visit websites directly less often.

### 2. Musk loses lawsuit against OpenAI

- **Date**: May 18 (Mon)
- **What happened**: On May 18, nine jurors at a federal court in Oakland, California, unanimously rejected Elon Musk’s claims in his lawsuit against OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, President Greg Brockman, OpenAI and Microsoft. After about two hours of deliberation, they found that the claims of breach of charitable trust and unjust enrichment had been filed too late, and the claim that Microsoft helped in the wrongdoing was also rejected. ### 4. OpenAI model disproves 80-year-old Erdős conjecture

- **Date**: May 20 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On May 20, OpenAI announced that its new general-purpose reasoning model had produced an original proof disproving a key conjecture in discrete geometry that Paul Erdős posed in 1946. OpenAI said that for nearly 80 years mathematicians had believed the best solutions were shaped roughly like a square grid, and that the model had found a new family of better constructions. It claimed this was the first time AI had solved a central problem in a field on its own.

  The announcement came with supporting comments from mathematicians, including Noga Alon, Melanie Wood and Thomas Bloom, who runs the Erdős problems website. Seven months earlier, an OpenAI executive had claimed that GPT-5 solved 10 Erdős problems, and when it turned out the model had only found answers already in published papers, Bloom called the claim a dramatic misrepresentation.
- **Why it matters**: A general-purpose reasoning model, not a system built only for math, solved a long-standing problem. OpenAI said the ability to keep up long chains of reasoning could also be used in research in biology, physics and engineering.
