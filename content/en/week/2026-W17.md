## Korea

### 1. Party apologizes over Jang Dong-hyuk’s U.S. meeting claim

- **Date**: Apr 20–25 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyuk returned on April 20, ending an eight-night, 10-day visit to the U.S. that began on the 11th. At a supreme council meeting on the 20th, Jang claimed, “I met key figures from the U.S. Congress, the White House NSC and core think tanks of the State Department,” and “Many U.S. figures expressed concern about the Lee Jae-myung government’s North Korea policy and its unclear position on the Korea–U.S. alliance.” The party said it had pushed back his return from the 17th to the 20th for a meeting with a State Department assistant secretary, and it released a photo that showed only the person’s back, without giving a name.

  On the 23rd (local time), a State Department spokesperson confirmed that the person Jang met was Gavin Wax, chief of staff to Sarah Rogers, the under secretary for public diplomacy. At a press briefing on the 24th, Jang called it “a working-level mistake” and corrected the title to “assistant secretary level.” On the 25th, chief spokesperson Park Sung-hoon apologized, saying, “If we gave you wrong information, it was clearly wrong,” and “If there was an administrative mistake, we have no intention of avoiding responsibility.”

  Calls for Jang to resign followed within the party. On the 23rd, National Assembly Deputy Speaker Joo Ho-young said, “I hope he knows when to step down.” On the 24th, Jang said he would think about “whether stepping down is truly the way to fulfill my responsibility as party leader,” signaling that he would not resign.
- **Why it matters**: With less than 40 days to go before the June 3 local elections, a reply from another country’s government showed that the leader of the main opposition party had exaggerated what his trip abroad involved. With disputes over nominations also going on, whether the party leader would stay became an issue in preparing for the election.

### 3. 40,000 Samsung union members rally, warn of general strike

- **Date**: Apr 23 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On April 23, a joint struggle headquarters of three Samsung Electronics unions held a rally in front of the Pyeongtaek Campus in Gyeonggi under the slogan “Make it transparent and abolish the cap.” The unions said more than 39,000 people took part, and the police and the unions put the number at about 40,000. That is about a third of the company’s roughly 128,800 employees in Korea, and it was the largest rally in the company’s history.

  The unions are demanding that 15% of operating profit go into the bonus pool, with no cap. Based on the 300 trillion won in operating profit that securities analysts expect this year, that would be up to 45 trillion won. The company offered to give more than 10% of operating profit in shares to be held for the long term, but the unions rejected it as a one-time reward. They warned that if no deal is reached, they will hold an 18-day general strike from May 21 to June 7.

  The unions said foundry output on the night shift fell 58.1% and memory output 18.4% on the day of the rally, and estimated that a general strike would cause more than 30 trillion won in damage. Across from the rally, shareholder groups held a protest against the strike.
- **Why it matters**: If the general strike goes ahead, it will be only the second strike since the company was founded in 1969, after one in July 2024. Samsung Electronics holds 36% of the global DRAM market and 32% of NAND, and after the rally, big tech companies abroad began asking whether supply would be disrupted.

### 4. U.S. limits intelligence sharing after minister’s remark

- **Date**: Apr 20–23 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: It came out that the U.S. had stopped providing some of the satellite imagery intelligence on North Korea it had been sharing with the Korean government. The trigger was a remark by Unification Minister Chung Dong-young at the National Assembly’s Foreign Affairs and Unification Committee on March 6. He named Kusong, along with Yongbyon and Kangson, as a site of North Korean uranium enrichment facilities, which neither the Korean nor the U.S. government had officially confirmed. The U.S. reportedly made its protest known to Korea’s diplomatic and security officials before limiting the intelligence.

  On April 20, Chung said, “It is very regrettable that using public materials to explain policy is being branded an intelligence leak,” and countered that Kusong had already been mentioned in sources such as a 2016 report by the U.S. Institute for Science and International Security (ISIS). On the same day, President Lee Jae-myung wrote on X, “It is a clear fact that the existence of the nuclear facility was already widely known around the world through papers and media reports.” He added, “I need to look into exactly why such an absurd thing is happening.” The People Power Party called for the minister to be fired.

  On the 23rd (local time), a senior Trump administration official said the U.S. government expects all partners to fully protect sensitive U.S. intelligence shared through private channels.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. cut back on intelligence sharing over an allied minister’s public remark, and the U.S. government publicly raised its allies’ responsibility for handling intelligence. Korea’s defense ministry said there was no problem with the Korea–U.S. intelligence sharing system or with readiness against North Korea.

### 5. Special counsel seeks 30 years for Yoon over drones

- **Date**: Apr 24 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On April 24, the insurrection special counsel team led by Cho Eun-suk asked for 30 years in prison for former President Yoon Suk Yeol, who is charged with benefiting the enemy and abuse of power. ## World

### 1. Trump extends Iran ceasefire indefinitely

- **Date**: Apr 21–25 (Tue–Sat)
- **What happened**: On April 21, U.S. President Donald Trump said on Truth Social that he would extend the ceasefire with Iran at Pakistan’s request, until Iran submits a proposal and the talks end one way or the other. In a CNBC interview the same day, he had said he expected to be bombing and did not want an extension. He ordered that the U.S. military’s blockade of Iranian ports stay in place.

  On the 22nd, Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps seized two ships trying to leave the Strait of Hormuz, the Panama-flagged MSC Francesca and the Liberia-flagged Epaminondas. It was the first time Iran had taken control of ships since the war began on February 28. Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said reopening the strait was impossible while ceasefire violations such as the U.S. naval blockade continued. On the 23rd, Trump ordered the Navy to fire on Iranian boats laying mines in the strait.

  On the 24th, the White House said it would send special envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner to Islamabad, Pakistan, to meet Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi. Iran said the talks would be indirect. When Araghchi left Pakistan on the 25th, Trump called off the envoys’ trip and said Iran could call any time it wanted.
- **Why it matters**: The ceasefire stopped most of the fighting, but passage through the strait has not reopened. With the strait, which carries a fifth of the world’s oil in normal times, in effect closed, Brent crude is nearly 50% higher than when the war began.

### 3. Coordinated attacks across Mali kill defense minister

### 4. EU finalizes €90 billion loan to Ukraine

- **Date**: Apr 22–23 (Wed–Thu)
- **What happened**: On April 23, the European Union gave final approval to a €90 billion loan to Ukraine and a 20th package of sanctions on Russia. The loan was agreed last December but had been blocked since March, when Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán used his veto together with Slovakia. He claimed Ukraine was deliberately delaying repairs to the Druzhba oil pipeline, which had been damaged by a Russian drone attack. After Ukraine finished the repairs and restarted the flow of Russian oil to Hungary and Slovakia on the 22nd, Hungary lifted its veto.

  The loan will be paid out interest-free, €45 billion each in 2026 and 2027. Each year, €28 billion will go to military spending and €17 billion to the general budget. The 20th sanctions package targets Russian banks and energy companies, as well as companies in the United Arab Emirates, Thailand and China that helped Russia get around sanctions. The EU also banned exports of machine tools and telecommunications equipment to Kyrgyzstan, which had failed to stop such goods from being sent on to Russia. It was the first time the EU had taken such a step.
- **Why it matters**: The loan covers two-thirds of Ukraine’s financing needs for 2026 and 2027. Economists said that without the loan, Ukraine could run out of money around June. Orbán, whose 16 years in power ended in the April 12 general election, did not attend the EU summit that began on the 23rd.

### 1. Unauthorized group gains access to Anthropic’s Mythos

- **Date**: Apr 20–22 (Mon–Wed)
- **What happened**: On April 21, it was reported that a group without permission had gained access to and been using Claude Mythos Preview, which Anthropic had released to only about 40 organizations because it could be used for offensive hacking. The group was made up of members of a Discord channel that hunts for information on unreleased AI models. They used the access rights of an employee at one of Anthropic’s contractors and guessed where Mythos was located from the address format of earlier models. They had reportedly been using it since the day the model was announced. Anthropic said it was investigating the claims of unauthorized access through a contractor’s environment and that there was no evidence yet that its own systems had been affected.

  Earlier, on the 20th, it was reported that the U.S. National Security Agency (NSA) was using Mythos to find vulnerabilities. The Defense Department, which oversees the NSA, labeled Anthropic a “supply chain risk” after the company barred the use of Claude for mass surveillance at home and the development of autonomous weapons, and the two are fighting in court. On the 21st, President Donald Trump said a deal between Anthropic and the Defense Department was possible.

  Examples of what Mythos can do also came out. Mozilla said Mythos had found 271 bugs in Firefox 150. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman criticized Mythos as fear-based marketing.
- **Why it matters**: Anthropic limited who could use the model because it was dangerous, but there were reports that those limits were broken through a contractor’s environment. While a U.S. intelligence agency uses the same model, the Defense Department is arguing in court that Anthropic is a security threat.

### 4. DeepSeek previews V4

- **Date**: Apr 24 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On April 24, China’s DeepSeek released a preview of its next-generation model, V4. It comes in two versions, V4 Pro and V4 Flash, both using a mixture-of-experts (MoE) design with a context window of 1 million tokens. V4 Pro has 1.6 trillion parameters in total (49 billion active), the largest of any open-weight model, and V4 Flash has 284 billion (13 billion active).

  DeepSeek said both models perform about as well as GPT-5.4 on coding competition benchmarks. V4 Flash costs $0.14 per million input tokens and $0.28 per million output tokens. DeepSeek stressed that the models work with Huawei chips, and it did not disclose the training cost or the hardware used for training.
- **Why it matters**: It is DeepSeek’s new model a year after R1 shocked the U.S. AI industry. The day before the announcement, the U.S. government accused China of stealing intellectual property from U.S. AI labs on a large scale through thousands of proxy accounts, and Anthropic and OpenAI have claimed that DeepSeek copied their models through distillation.
