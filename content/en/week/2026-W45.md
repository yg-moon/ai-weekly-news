## Korea

### 1. Ruling party passes “fabricated indictment” counsel bill

- **Date**: Nov 4–6 (Wed–Fri)
- **What happened**: On November 6, the National Assembly passed a special counsel bill to investigate claims that prosecutors, the National Intelligence Service and the Board of Audit and Inspection fabricated investigations and indictments under the Yoon Suk Yeol government. The ruling bloc ended a filibuster by the People Power Party after more than 24 hours, then passed the bill with 167 of the 168 members present voting in favor. The People Power Party did not vote.

  The bill covers 12 cases. Cases linked to President Lee Jae-myung were grouped into six, including the Daejang-dong, Wirye and Baekhyeon-dong development allegations, the Seongnam FC sponsorship allegations and the Ssangbangwool payments to North Korea. Cases from the Moon Jae-in government, such as the alleged leak of THAAD missile defense secrets, were also added. The counsel team can have up to 309 people and investigate for up to 200 days. The Democratic Party, the People Power Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party will each recommend one candidate, and President Lee will appoint one. The counsel’s power to withdraw indictments, which was in the original bill, was removed when the Legislation and Judiciary Committee revised it on the 4th.

  Objections also came from within the ruling bloc. Democratic Party lawmaker Jung Sung-ho, the government’s first justice minister, said, “Shouldn’t there have been a bit more deliberation?” Lawmaker Kwak Sang-eon said, “An investigation of the (sitting) president is unavoidable.” People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyuk said, “Today only Lee Jae-myung lives and the Republic of Korea has died.”
- **Why it matters**: President Lee will appoint the special counsel who will investigate how cases involving him were investigated and prosecuted. Cho Kuk, head of the Rebuilding Korea Party’s policy institute, said a provision giving investigative powers to seconded prosecutors raises constitutional and legal problems. It applies old Criminal Procedure Act rules that were abolished when investigation and indictment powers were separated. Some in the Democratic Party also worried that passing the bill just as budget review began would hurt public opinion.

### 2. KOSPI falls 11% in two days, dips below 5,000

- **Date**: Nov 5–6 (Thu–Fri)
- **What happened**: The KOSPI closed 5.28% lower at 5,383.80 on November 5, then fell 6.02% to 5,059.69 on the 6th. The two-day drop was 10.98%. On the 6th, the index fell more than 8% about three minutes after the open. A circuit breaker stopped all trading for 20 minutes, and the index fell as low as 4,911.28 during the day. It was the first time in nine months, since February 6, that the KOSPI fell below 5,000 during trading. The close was 44.5% below this year’s closing high of 9,114.55 on June 22.

  Large chip stocks led the fall. Samsung Electronics fell 7.57% and SK hynix 9.46% on the 5th, and they fell another 6.35% and 10.44% on the 6th. Foreign investors were net sellers of 3.4 trillion won on the 5th and 2.69 trillion won on the 6th.

Gina Hinojosa, the Democrat elected governor of Texas, had promised to pause new data center construction. On the 6th, the government held an emergency market meeting. It decided to exempt securities firms for three months from the 9th from the duty to keep a 140% collateral ratio on margin loans. This means firms do not have to force-sell investors’ shares right away when collateral runs short.
- **Why it matters**: Korea’s stock market, which relies heavily on chip stocks, fell more than other major markets. On the 5th, Japan’s Nikkei 225 fell 1.79% and Taiwan’s Taiex fell 3.20%. Analysts said slower new data center investment in the U.S. would weigh on the earnings outlook of Korean chipmakers.

### 3. Cheonghae Unit’s area expanded to within 650 km of Hormuz

- **Date**: Nov 5 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On November 5, a meeting of vice ministers approved a revised motion to extend the Cheonghae Unit’s deployment. It widens the unit’s area of operations from the Gulf of Aden off Somalia to the open sea south of Oman, up to the port of Duqm. Duqm is about 650 km from the Strait of Hormuz in a straight line. The mission area will nearly double from about 1,100 km, but it will not include the Gulf of Oman, which borders the strait.

  The government will send one P-8A maritime patrol aircraft with 45 crew and support staff, unmanned mine-clearing equipment and a 30-member explosive ordnance disposal team. The unit will grow from 320 to 395 personnel. A new task was added: “support for restoring freedom of navigation on international sea lanes.” The motion will go to the Cabinet on the 10th and then to the National Assembly. The deployment will run from the Assembly’s approval to December 31 next year.

  The Defense Ministry said piracy in the Gulf of Aden rose from 11 cases last year to 36 so far this year. It also set out principles: the unit will not take part in the war, the patrol aircraft will not watch Iranian waters or the inner Strait of Hormuz, and the unit will operate under Korea’s own command. Kim Joon-hyung, floor leader of the Rebuilding Korea Party, said, “We cannot send a unit dispatched to stop pirates into a sea at war.” The civic group People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy called it “a de facto backdoor troop dispatch.”
- **Why it matters**: The move is seen as Korea’s first de facto military step related to stability in the Strait of Hormuz since the Iran war began. Some read the plan as a compromise between repeated U.S. requests for troops in Hormuz and public opposition at home. Defense Ministry spokesperson Jeong Bit-na said more than 70% of Korea’s crude oil shipments pass through those waters. The mine-clearing forces are meant to prepare for mine removal in the strait after the war ends. The government says a separate deployment motion would be needed to send them into the strait itself.
- **Sources**: [Hankyoreh](https://n.news.naver.com/article/newspaper/028/0002830953) / [Kyunghyang Shinmun](https://n.news.naver.com/article/newspaper/032/0003481031)

### 4. Yoon’s election law sentence upheld on appeal

- **Date**: Nov 4 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On November 4, a criminal panel of the Seoul High Court rejected the appeal of former President Yoon Suk Yeol, who was charged with spreading false information before the 2022 presidential election. His sentence of one year and six months in prison, suspended for three years, was upheld. The court found two statements false. One was his claim that he met the shaman known as “Geonjin,” Jeon Seong-bae, through a party official and never met him with his wife. The other was his claim that he never introduced lawyer Lee Nam-seok to former Yongsan tax office chief Yoon Woo-jin. The court said, “Voters’ right to make a sound choice was violated and the fairness of the election was damaged.”

  Yoon’s side expressed “deep regret” and said it would appeal to the Supreme Court. A special counsel law requires the top court to rule within three months, so the final result is expected around February next year.
- **Why it matters**: If the Supreme Court confirms a fine of 1 million won or more, Yoon’s election will be void. The People Power Party will then have to return 39.7 billion won in campaign costs that the state repaid after the election. No presidential election result has ever been voided, and no party has ever returned presidential campaign costs. Asked whether the party should apologize, People Power Party floor leader Jeong Jeom-sik said, “This is the second trial, and the Supreme Court ruling is still to come.”

### 5. Personal data of 168 on presidential plane exposed

- **Date**: Nov 2–3 (Mon–Tue)
- **What happened**: On November 2, it became known that an Excel file with personal data of 168 people who flew on the presidential plane during President Lee Jae-myung’s June trip to Europe had been exposed online for about five months. The file was uploaded on June 2 to VirusTotal, a paid site that scans files for malware. It held the birth dates, English names, passport numbers and passport issue and expiry dates of the president and his wife, Deputy Prime Minister Bae Kyung-hoon, Foreign Minister Cho Hyun, National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac, staff and reporters. It also held identifying details of presidential security guards and of staff who check the president’s food.

  On the 3rd, the Foreign Ministry said it believed the file was leaked from inside rather than hacked. The file matched the format used by the ministry’s protocol office. The ministry asked the police to investigate and began reissuing passports for all 168 people. The cyber terror unit of the National Office of Investigation opened an investigation.
- **Why it matters**: With the identities of presidential security staff exposed, it is hard to rule out that hostile states used the file to track people or gather intelligence. At a Cabinet meeting on the 3rd, President Lee ordered officials to consider creating a cybersecurity “czar” body to coordinate scattered security functions, after a series of hacking cases at home and abroad. Some saw the order as linked to this leak. The National Intelligence Service began emergency security checks of about 2,100 bodies, including central ministries and local governments, over the hacking of financial firms.

## World

### 1. Democrats win both houses in U.S. midterms

- **Date**: Nov 3–4 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: In the U.S. midterm elections on November 3, the Democratic Party won control of both the House and the Senate. In counts through the 4th, Democrats had won 228 House seats and were leading in 34 more. They took 25 House seats from Republicans. In the Senate, they secured 54 seats, including independents who caucus with them, passing the 51 needed for a majority. Of the 33 Senate races called, Democrats won 20, and eight of those were in states that President Donald Trump won in 2024.

  Records fell in Republican strongholds. Kansas elected a Democratic senator for the first time since 1932. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed became the first Muslim elected to the Senate. In Georgia, Keisha Lance Bottoms became the first Black woman elected governor in U.S. history, and Democrat Gina Hinojosa won the governor’s race in Texas.

  In exit polls, 43% of voters said they voted partly to express opposition to Trump. 71% said there is not enough congressional oversight of Trump. Trump wrote on social media that he was not on the ballot and that if he had been, Republicans would have won in a landslide.
- **Why it matters**: It is the first time in 20 years, since George W. Bush in 2006, that a Republican president has lost both houses of Congress. When the new Congress meets in January, the Trump administration will face investigations and checks from a Democratic Congress for the last two years of its term. Voters named corruption, the cost of living, immigration and the Iran war as key issues. After the election, fears of regulation on AI data centers sent U.S. and Asian stock markets sharply lower.

### 2. Paraguay’s president impeached and held after bank collapse

- **Date**: Nov 3–6 (Tue–Fri)
- **What happened**: On November 3, Paraguay’s central bank took over the digital bank Ueno after it fell below legal solvency standards. Ueno was the country’s fifth-largest bank by deposits. With about 3 million members, it counted nearly two-thirds of Paraguay’s adults as customers. Millions of customers lost access to their accounts and pensions, and $355 million in state pension funds had been invested in the bank.

  President Santiago Peña faced conflict of interest questions after he was identified in 2023 as a shareholder of Ueno’s parent company. Peña said he sold his shares in early 2025. Peña announced his resignation on the 5th, but minutes later the lower house voted 78 to 0 to impeach him and refused to accept the resignation. On the 6th, 44 of the 45 senators voted to remove him. Prosecutors placed him under house arrest on suspicion of breach of trust and other crimes, and Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in the same day as Paraguay’s 53rd president.
- **Why it matters**: Had his resignation been accepted, Peña would have become a senator for life as a former president and could have avoided prosecution. The ruling Colorado Party, which has largely dominated Paraguayan politics since 1948, and its leader, former President Horacio Cartes, backed the impeachment. An arrest warrant was also issued for former central bank head Carlos Carvallo, whose bank was responsible for supervision.

## AI

### 1. Data center backlash in midterms sends tech stocks down

- **Date**: Nov 3–5 (Tue–Thu)
- **What happened**: AI data centers were a key issue in the U.S. midterm elections on November 3. Of 44 local data center moratoriums on the ballot that day, 43 passed. In exit polls, 76% of voters opposed data centers in their own communities. Gina Hinojosa, the governor-elect of Texas, said she would halt all data center approvals until the state legislature passes laws to protect residents. Candidates who opposed rapid data center expansion also won in Maine, Michigan, Nevada, Georgia and South Carolina.

  On the 4th, Meta president Dina Powell McCormick said the Democratic wins would make it harder to build data centers. On the 5th, the Nasdaq fell more than 5% and more than $2 trillion in market value was wiped out, on fears that a Democratic Congress would regulate the AI industry.
- **Why it matters**: Local votes have started to block data center construction. Analysts expect more construction moratoriums, or rules that make data centers pay for their own power grid upgrades. Democratic bills now have better chances, such as Senator Bernie Sanders’ bill for a nationwide moratorium on new data centers. Big tech companies need to keep building data centers to develop AI, and Meta now has data centers in 23 states.

### 2. Anthropic says AI speeds its research; outside review agrees

- **Date**: Nov 2–5 (Mon–Thu)
- **What happened**: On November 2, Anthropic filed a revised IPO prospectus with third-quarter results and new risk factors. Third-quarter revenue rose to about $17 billion from $11.5 billion in the second quarter, but the net loss was $23.29 billion. Compute costs rose from 48% to 58% of revenue.

  A new risk factor warned that using AI to speed up its own research could make its models advance faster than its safety measures. Anthropic said its internal estimates suggest it may soon meet its Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP) threshold for “dramatically accelerated progress.” It said none of its current models has crossed this threshold.

  On the 5th, the AI safety research group METR published a report based on an 11-week review inside Anthropic. METR found that Anthropic’s models are improving at about twice the rate of March 2024 to October 2025, making about two years of past progress in the last year. It found that most of the speed-up comes from AI. Employees using the most capable internal model said they were about 12 times as productive as they would be without AI.
- **Why it matters**: A company filing and an outside review both support the claim that AI is speeding up its own development. METR found no clear signs of diminishing returns or bottlenecks, and saw signs that fully autonomous contributions will soon grow. Anthropic said it does not expect to safely use Claude to design and build the next generation of models without human supervision, and it is considering RSP changes to keep humans in control. However, Anthropic was allowed to redact parts of the METR report before release.

### 4. OpenAI launches security model GPT-6 Cyber

- **Date**: Nov 4–5 (Wed–Thu)
- **What happened**: On November 5, OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, a cybersecurity model. It is first available to some members of Daybreak Red, a program for organizations that use the models for authorized defensive security work, and will reach all members in the coming weeks. It scored 48.5% on ExploitGym, a test of the ability to exploit vulnerabilities, ahead of GPT-5.6 Cyber (32.1%) and the flagship GPT-6 Astra (42.4%). The rating means it can build zero-day exploits for real critical systems without human help. Security workers worry that the flaws AI finds exceed what defenders can handle. OpenAI said thousands of people take part in its program, while potential attackers are far more numerous.
