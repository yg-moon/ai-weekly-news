## Korea

### 1. Top scorer on tax accountant exam admits “AI cheating”

- **Date**: Nov 12–15 (Thu–Sun)
- **What happened**: On November 15, the top scorer on the 63rd certified tax accountant exam, a 34-year-old surnamed Han, admitted cheating on both rounds with a tiny camera, a smartphone and generative AI. Han submitted a written confession to the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency. Han photographed the questions with the camera, sent them to an AI service and read the answers on a smartphone. Han said the same method was used on the first round of the certified public accountant exam. Police assigned the case to a public crime investigation unit and opened an investigation.

  Suspicions were raised online on the 12th. Han scored 397.5 out of 400 on the first round in April and 663 out of 800 on the essay-based second round in July. In the first-round tax law course, the average score was 30.18 and 74.21% of test takers failed, but Han scored 100. Han sat in the first or second row in front of the podium, in a room with two proctors, but was not caught.

  On the 15th, the presidential office held an emergency meeting chaired by Policy Chief Ha Jun-kyung. It decided to review all national licensing exams for suspected AI cheating. For the college entrance exam on the 19th, it will use every available tool, including thermal cameras, and set up checks for AI devices on all test takers.
- **Why it matters**: Two national professional exams failed to stop one test taker from cheating with AI. There are 540 national technical licensing exams run by bodies such as the Human Resources Development Service of Korea, with 2.29 million test takers a year (2025). Cheating with smart glasses and similar devices has been caught several times in licensing exams, college entrance tests and language tests. Critics say proctors who check for smart glasses by eye will struggle to catch other devices such as tiny cameras.

### 2. Chungcheong megaproject starts, 23.6 trillion won added

- **Date**: Nov 12 (Thu)
- **What happened**: The first groundbreaking ceremony of the government’s three regional megaprojects took place on November 12 at Samsung Electronics’ Onyang campus in Asan, South Chungcheong Province. Construction began on three projects: an advanced chip packaging fab and an advanced display production line in Asan, and a plant in Sejong for high-performance substrates used in AI servers. The packaging fab at the Onyang campus is worth 6 trillion won. President Lee Jae-myung and Samsung Electronics Chairman Lee Jae-yong broke ground together.

  Samsung’s 140 trillion won investment plan for the Chungcheong region, announced in July, started construction four months later. At the fourth public-private review meeting that followed, President Lee said 112 companies plan to invest an additional 23.6 trillion won in the region. The government’s plan for Chungcheong, announced in July, was 392 trillion won.
- **Why it matters**: The megaprojects for the Honam, Chungcheong and Yeongnam regions, announced by the government in late June, have moved to construction for the first time. South Chungcheong Province and Asan cut permit times to about a third of the average. Chairman Lee said work that would have taken more than eight months was shortened. President Lee said the central and local governments would take responsibility for permits, power and water so that investments in Honam and Yeongnam also proceed. At the meeting, participants called for policy loans for small firms facing higher interest rates.

### 3. Plan to block bank group chairs’ “self-reappointment”

- **Date**: Nov 12 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On November 12, the government and the Democratic Party held a policy meeting and announced a plan to improve the governance of financial companies. Its core aim is to stop sitting CEOs from extending their terms through boards and nomination committees they influence, known as “self-reappointment.” Committees that nominate CEO candidates must consist only of independent directors. Sitting CEOs may not attend committee meetings that nominate independent directors or audit committee members. CEOs who interfere in succession can face dismissal requests or suspension of up to six months.

  Voting thresholds rise with each new term. A nomination committee will need two-thirds of its members for a first nomination and three-quarters for a reappointment. Today both need half. At the shareholders’ meeting, a first reappointment will need two-thirds of the votes present, and a second reappointment three-quarters. For a second reappointment, votes in favor must also exceed half of all shares.
- **Why it matters**: The plan came about 11 months after President Lee Jae-myung criticized a “corrupt inner circle” in a policy briefing last December. Banning a third term by law was also discussed. The Financial Services Commission instead chose stricter voting rules, citing global standards and the freedom to choose one’s occupation. The commission said about three of the 15 shareholder meetings it reviewed would fall short of the new rules. Some doubt the effect, since financial groups already run similar systems and reappointed chairs won 80% to 90% support.

### 5. Financial Security Institute hacked, more data leaks follow

- **Date**: Nov 9–14 (Mon–Sat)
- **What happened**: The Financial Security Institute, which handles security for the financial sector, was hacked on November 9. More than 130 files submitted by 48 financial companies were leaked. They included about 200 administrative forms, such as consent forms for personal credit data, more than 300 pieces of personal data such as names and birth dates, and more than 350 records on system users. The attack hit a support system where financial firms submit information security forms and check their ratings. The institute said sensitive security data, such as system diagrams, was not leaked. The Financial Services Commission began an on-site inspection on the 11th, and the institute asked police to investigate.

  On the 14th alone, the Korea Federation of SMEs, Inje University, the Korea Minting and Security Printing Corporation and call center contractor Meta M disclosed leaks or signs of leaks caused by outside attacks. The federation said names and the first seven digits of resident registration numbers may have leaked from Yellow Umbrella, a mutual aid fund for small business owners with 1.92 million members. On Chak, the mint’s mobile platform for local gift certificates, data on about 3,000 users was leaked.
- **Why it matters**: The institute that has told financial firms to check their outside systems for weaknesses was breached through one of its own support systems. The Financial Services Commission had already warned in a 2022 audit that data could leak through this system. Right after the attack, the institute only posted a notice about “service reorganization” and announced the breach late on the 10th. Regulators will also check whether it is linked to the hacks of financial firms since September.

## World

### 1. Russia, Ukraine agree to prisoner swap in Abu Dhabi talks

- **Date**: Nov 10–12 (Tue–Thu)
- **What happened**: On November 10 and 11, Russian and Ukrainian delegations held U.S.-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates. It was the first direct meeting of the two delegations since February. The talks ended without a major breakthrough, but both sides agreed to a large prisoner exchange. Vladimir Medinsky, head of the Russian delegation, said the agreement was in principle. A Russian General Staff official said it would be one of the largest swaps since 1,000 soldiers returned last year and could happen within two or three weeks.

  On the 12th, U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow for about two hours. They then met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Dublin, Ireland. Witkoff said Europe will join the next round of talks. Zelenskyy said Europe would take part as a negotiating team with its own voice.
- **Why it matters**: These were the first talks since the Democrats won control of the U.S. Congress in the midterm elections. When the new Congress meets in January, Democrats could push for more support for Ukraine. Russia has opposed European participation in direct talks, saying European countries fund and arm Ukraine. The fate of Russian-occupied Ukrainian land and postwar security guarantees are still unresolved. Russia is stepping up attacks on Ukraine’s power grid as winter approaches.

### 2. Indonesia’s vice president resigns over education dispute

- **Date**: Nov 10–12 (Tue–Thu)
- **What happened**: On November 12, Indonesian Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka submitted his resignation to the leadership of the People’s Consultative Assembly. President Prabowo Subianto accepted it the same day, saying he did so with a heavy heart. Gibran had offered to resign on the 10th during a meeting of more than two hours with Prabowo. Gibran is 39 and the son of former President Joko Widodo.

  In October, the Constitutional Court and the Supreme Court ruled that the documents Gibran submitted about his overseas education did not meet election requirements. On the 11th, 120 lawmakers from eight parties filed a petition with the House leadership to start the impeachment process.
- **Why it matters**: Under Indonesia’s constitution, a vacant vice presidency does not trigger a new election. The president nominates two candidates, and the People’s Consultative Assembly elects one within 60 days. Gibran was below the age requirement of 40 for vice presidential candidates in the 2024 election. He could run because of an October 2023 Constitutional Court ruling that made an exception for people who had served as regional leaders. Critics see that ruling as a conflict of interest and a case of dynastic privilege. The resignation came amid a widening political rift between Prabowo and Widodo.

### 3. Spain holds first general strike in 14 years over housing

- **Date**: Nov 11 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On November 11, millions of workers in Spain held a 24-hour general strike to protest rising home prices and rents. It was Spain’s first general strike in 14 years. The two unions that led it, CCOO and UGT, said work stopped at 90% in the automotive and port sectors, 85% in the meat industry, 82% in waste collection and 75% in energy. Business groups said participation was low in most sectors.

  Tens of thousands marched in Barcelona and Madrid. In Barcelona, protesters set tires on fire at roadblocks, and in Madrid police used pepper spray on protesters. About 5% of flights at Spanish airports were canceled.
- **Why it matters**: It was Spain’s first general strike driven mainly by housing. Average rents in Spain have almost doubled over the past 10 years. In October, a 50-square-meter apartment in Madrid rented for 1,170 euros a month on average, nearly half the average gross monthly salary of about 2,460 euros. The nationwide protests began after an 87-year-old pensioner in Madrid was evicted in September and died. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called an early election for the 29th after his minority government failed to pass housing measures, and the conservative opposition Popular Party leads in the polls.

### 4. Congo’s Ebola outbreak passes 11,000 cases

- **Date**: Nov 10–11 (Tue–Wed)
- **What happened**: The government of the Democratic Republic of the Congo said Ebola cases had reached 11,042, with 5,387 deaths. The outbreak was declared in mid-May and has spread to eight of the country’s 26 provinces. It is caused by the Bundibugyo virus, which has no vaccine or treatment and kills about half of the people it infects. It has also spread to several African countries, including Uganda, Kenya and Nigeria.
- **Why it matters**: It is the largest Ebola outbreak in Congo’s history and the second largest on record, after the 2014–2016 West Africa outbreak, which infected more than 28,000 people and killed more than 11,000. Compared over the same period since it was declared, it has at least three times the cases and deaths of the West Africa outbreak. Armed conflict in Ituri and North Kivu, a health system crisis, poor roads and attacks on health workers by distrustful residents make it hard to contain. In early November, the World Health Organization approved clinical trials of two new Ebola vaccines and expects results in early 2027.

### 5. All U.S. departures grounded for over four hours

- **Date**: Nov 13–14 (Fri–Sat)
- **What happened**: On November 13, the Federal Aviation Administration ordered a ground stop for all departing flights across the United States at 3:54 p.m. Eastern time. It lifted the order more than four hours later, at 8:15 p.m. At up to 17 of the 22 en route air traffic control centers, radio frequencies kept going in and out. More than 9,000 flights were canceled on the 13th and more than 5,000 on the 14th. About 1.5 million passengers were reportedly affected.

  On the 14th, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said contractor L3Harris was replacing copper lines with fiber at a remote site. A router there then made 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds and overloaded other routers. It is not yet clear whether the cause was contractor error or faulty equipment. The FAA and the FBI are examining the router. The government said it did not believe a cyberattack was involved.
- **Why it matters**: It was the longest of only two nationwide stops of U.S. flights since the September 11, 2001 attacks. A November 2023 safety review commissioned by the FAA described this communications network as obsolete, and about 30% of the copper lines have not yet been replaced. Aging equipment and a shortage of several thousand controllers have long troubled the air traffic control system. Duffy asked for $50 billion to upgrade it.

### 3. Google releases Gemini 4 Carbon, adds Hydrogen to Search

- **Why it matters**: Google released Carbon and Hydrogen after launching Argon in October. Some see Google rejoining the race for top models, which for months looked like a contest between Anthropic and OpenAI. On benchmarks cited by Google, Carbon ranked among the strongest models, behind Fable 5.2. Carbon’s price will double at the end of November, when the introductory period ends.

### 4. Anthropic releases Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2

- **Date**: Nov 9 (Mon)
- **What happened**: On November 9, Anthropic released its top models, Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2. The two share the same underlying model. Fable has safeguards against misuse in cybersecurity and biology and is available to all users, while Mythos is limited to vetted organizations. The models improve on coding, reasoning and vision and respond up to 30% faster than the 5.1 versions. They cost $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens.

  Anthropic said that in pre-release testing the models solved two open problems in black hole physics and formalized the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in 30 hours.
- **Why it matters**: It is Anthropic’s first model release since the evaluation group METR reported that its models were improving about twice as fast as before. Anthropic said the two models more often voiced suspicion that they were being tested than earlier models did. That makes it harder to judge how they will act in real-world settings. Mythos 5.2 showed stronger covert capabilities than earlier Claude models, but Anthropic judged them low enough that harmful actions could still be detected. Fable’s input price fell from $10 to $8, making Fable 5.2 cheaper than OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra.

### 5. Google adds two eighth-generation TPU chips

- **Date**: Nov 9 (Mon)
- **What happened**: On November 9, Google unveiled two new versions of its eighth-generation TPU AI chips, the TPU 8tx and the TPU 8i Max. The TPU 8tx, designed with AMD, is for confidential computing for government agencies and regulated industries. It builds security into the hardware so that data and model weights stay isolated even while in use. The TPU 8i Max combines two inference-focused TPU 8i chips in one package, doubling memory, processing power and bandwidth. It is meant for AI work with long context.
- **Why it matters**: After releasing the TPU 8t and 8i in April, Google has now launched four eighth-generation TPUs in one year. Cloud companies such as Microsoft and Amazon, as well as OpenAI and Anthropic, are also building their own AI chips to rely less on Nvidia. The announcement came just after Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang admitted an affair with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy.
