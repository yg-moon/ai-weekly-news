## Korea

### 1. Tax plan targets costly homes and owners living elsewhere

- **Date**: Aug 3–7 (Mon–Fri)
- **What happened**: On August 3, the Ministry of Economy and Finance announced its 2026 tax reform plan. From next year, it raises the comprehensive real estate tax step by step on very expensive homes, one-home owners who do not live in their home, and owners of several homes. It is the first broad change to real estate taxes in about four years. The basic deduction of the comprehensive real estate tax rises from 1.2 billion won to 1.4 billion won for one-home owners who live in their home, and falls to 900 million won for one-home owners who do not. The tax rate for the 600 million to 1.2 billion won bracket rises from 1% to 1.3%. For owners of one or two homes in the 1.2 billion to 2.5 billion won bracket, it rises from 1.3% to 1.5% next year and 2% in 2028. The fair market value ratio rises to 70% for one-home owners and to 80% for owners of several homes by 2028. For the capital gains tax, the special deduction for long-term ownership loses its holding-period part and is capped at 1 billion won.

  The extra capital gains tax on owners of several homes will depend on when they sell. If they sell within a year, the extra tax is only 5 percentage points for owners of two homes and 10 points for owners of three. If they sell within two years, it is 10 and 20 points, and after that it returns to the original level. At a Cabinet meeting on the 4th, President Lee Jae-myung said, “On income above 1 billion won, workers work hard and pay about half of it in taxes.” He added that even when real estate capital gains reach 10 billion won or more, the actual tax is only a few hundred million won.

  Pushback followed. At a real estate forum hosted by Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon on the 6th, critics said listings for jeonse and monthly rent would fall and renters would bear more of the burden. Jeonse is Korea’s lump-sum deposit rental. On the 7th, Lee reportedly ordered a “full review” of the ISA changes in the plan. They include a new ISA only for domestic investment and the end of the carryover of unused limits in existing ISAs. About a bill to stop holding down share prices, he also said, “Look at this one again, too.” The bill would tax shares at a premium of at least 30% of their value.
- **Why it matters**: One-home owners who live in their home will pay no comprehensive real estate tax on homes worth up to 2 billion won. But for one-home owners who do not live in their home (aged 60 and holding it for 10 years), the tax was estimated to rise to about four times today’s level by 2028. The basis of the tax shifts from the number of homes to home value and whether owners live in them. Four days after the announcement, the president ordered a review of the parts that tax individual investors.

### 2. Cabinet approves end of prosecutors’ investigation powers

- **Date**: Aug 4 (Tue)
- **What happened**: On August 4, at a Cabinet meeting chaired by President Lee Jae-myung, the government approved, without changes, an amendment to the Criminal Procedure Act that fully ends prosecutors’ power to investigate. It separates investigation from indictment, stopping prosecutors from investigating directly, and also removes their power to carry out further investigation. Prosecutors can ask the police for further investigation, and the police must finish it within a month and report the results. Two new grounds for a court to dismiss a prosecution were added: when charges are based on a seriously illegal investigation, and when charges go far beyond prosecutors’ discretion. The amended law takes effect on October 2, when the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency and the Public Prosecution Agency start.

  The amendment was passed in a plenary session of the National Assembly on July 31, led by the Democratic Party, and the People Power Party had called for a veto. Lee called the amendment “a necessary step that finally puts every power institution, without exception, under the people’s control.” On a veto, he said, “In its current state, it is very hard to see it as going so far as to deny the legislature’s power.”
- **Why it matters**: A 78-year system of investigation centered on prosecutors comes to an end. The Constitution says warrants are issued “at the request of a prosecutor,” but the amended law lets a prosecutor request a warrant only after the police ask for one, which has raised questions about whether it is constitutional. The opposition has said it will take legal action, such as a constitutional complaint.

### 3. Seoul’s first severe heat warning; Yongsan hits 39.9°C

- **Date**: Aug 3–9 (Mon–Sun)
- **What happened**: On the afternoon of August 3, the Korea Meteorological Administration issued a severe heat warning for Seoul for the first time, and it took effect at 11 a.m. on the 4th in southern Seoul and southern Gyeonggi Province. A severe heat warning is issued when a heat index of 38°C or higher or a high temperature of 39°C or higher is expected for more than a day. On the 5th, it widened to almost the whole Seoul metropolitan area and was issued for the Chungcheong region for the first time. On the 6th, the high in Yongsan, Seoul, was 39.9°C. From June to August 5, the national average number of tropical nights was 12.6, breaking the record for the longest ever again.

  As the pressure pattern changed, all severe heat warnings were lifted on the 8th. In Seoul, the overnight low on the 8th and 9th fell to 24.5°C, ending a run of tropical nights that had lasted 17 days since July 23. Heat advisories stayed in place in most areas except the east coast and mountains of Gangwon and North Gyeongsang provinces. Under the influence of Typhoon Dolphin, more than 80 mm of rain an hour fell in Gangneung on the 8th, flooding roads and dozens of shops, and vacationers were stranded in valleys.
- **Why it matters**: Severe heat warnings, which had centered on the southeastern Yeongnam region, moved up to Seoul and the surrounding area. The Korea Meteorological Administration explained that east winds warmed up as they crossed the Taebaek Mountains and pushed up temperatures in the west.

### 4. Football association accused of sex services for referees

- **Date**: Aug 6–8 (Thu–Sat)
- **What happened**: On August 6, it was confirmed that the Korea Football Association had provided sexual services several times to foreign referees before national team matches in 2011. The targets were more than 10 foreign referees and match officials at three World Cup and Olympic qualifiers and four friendly matches held in Korea from March 2011 to March 2012. This is according to a 2016 audit report by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. Payments of several hundred thousand won each were made with the association’s corporate card at massage parlors and other places in Gangnam, Seoul, Changwon, South Gyeongsang Province, and Ulsan. An association official at the time said, “It was a customary practice,” and the association said, “We now have strict internal management guidelines in place and are making efforts.”

  In the seven matches these referees worked, Korea won five and drew two. The international qualifiers were the 2012 London Olympics qualifiers against Oman (a 2-0 win) and Qatar (a 0-0 draw), and the 2014 Brazil World Cup qualifier against Kuwait (a 2-0 win). Japanese media named three Japanese referees among those entertained, and one of them now manages J.League referees at the Japan Football Association.
- **Why it matters**: There were reports that the case could lead to sanctions by FIFA. But FIFA’s ethics code has a time limit of 5 to 10 years, so a penalty was expected to be unlikely. On the same day, August 6, the association was also raided by police over claims of improper interference in the hiring of former head coach Hong Myung-bo.

### 5. Front-line “no live rounds” row; corps commander removed

- **Date**: Aug 3–5 (Mon–Wed)
- **What happened**: On August 3, the Ministry of National Defense removed Lieutenant General Han Ki-sung, commander of the Army’s I Corps, from duty. In the first half of this year, the I Corps, which guards the western front, changed its guard operation guidelines at the commander’s discretion. Its troops stood guard at GOPs and GPs, the front-line posts, without loading live rounds into K6 heavy machine guns and K4 automatic grenade launchers. On July 30, near the demilitarized zone in Paju, it judged a U.S. Marine Corps drone in a joint South Korea–U.S. exercise to be an unidentified flying object and prepared to shoot it down. The U.S. military had told I Corps staff about the flight plan, but it was not reported to higher command. Ground Operations Command is checking every guard unit of the front-line corps.

  At a briefing from the Ministry of National Defense and other agencies on the 5th, President Lee Jae-myung spoke about guarding without live rounds. He said, “If we keep stepping back, thinking ‘surely nothing will happen,’ we may end up with not a single arrow in the arrow storehouse.” He called for strict military discipline. At the same meeting, he said, “How many military coups has Korea had so far? Didn’t they all happen in the Army?” and ordered a quick push to merge the Army, Navy and Air Force academies.
- **Why it matters**: The rule for weapons at front-line units is to keep them loaded, but a single corps commander was able to change it. The near shootdown of an allied drone happened because notice between South Korea and the U.S. did not reach higher command.

## World

### 1. Russian ballistic missiles kill 17 in Kyiv

- **Date**: Aug 5–8 (Wed–Sat)
- **What happened**: Overnight on August 5, Russia fired more than 20 ballistic and anti-ship missiles at Kyiv and the surrounding area, killing at least 17 people and injuring dozens. Ukraine’s air defenses did not shoot down a single missile. People were also killed at a railway station in the Kyiv region, and warehouses of Rozetka, Ukraine’s largest e-commerce company, and of retailers burned. In July alone, Russian attacks killed at least 377 civilians, the most since April 2022.

  On the 5th, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky asked for interceptor missiles, saying that systems to stop ballistic missiles would have saved the lives of the people who died that day. He said Ukraine’s interceptor missiles had fallen to two-fifths of the 2025 level, while Russia’s ballistic missiles had doubled. On the 8th, he said North Korea had decided to deploy 30,000 to 50,000 troops to Russia, and asked South Korea for help with air defense systems.
- **Why it matters**: Only a few weapons, such as the U.S.-made Patriot, can stop ballistic missiles, and as interceptors run out, civilian losses are growing in cities far from the front line. The number of North Korean troops Zelensky recently cited, 30,000, has risen to as many as 50,000, and he said North Korean-made missiles had also been found in Ukraine. As the U.S. uses its weapons in the Iran war, its support for Ukraine has fallen sharply.

### 3. Iran and Oman agree on a new Hormuz shipping route

- **Date**: Aug 5 (Wed)
- **What happened**: On August 5, Iran’s foreign ministry said it had agreed with Oman on the coordinates of a new shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz. In talks that lasted more than three weeks, the two sides discussed inbound and outbound routes, technical plans, and a joint coordination center to gather ship information and manage traffic. The temporary route through waters near Iran’s Larak Island and Oman’s territorial waters will close, and part of the new route passes through Iranian waters. Iran’s deputy foreign minister said the new route would first run for two to four months, and that a separate decision by Iran’s top leadership is needed to put it into effect.

  Iran said the agreement alone would not reopen the strait. It set conditions: the U.S. must lift its blockade of Iranian ports, resolve the oil sanctions it put back after the ceasefire collapsed, and restart talks on frozen assets. On the 4th, U.S. President Donald Trump said the strait would either open soon or Iran would be hit very hard.
- **Why it matters**: On August 3, eight ships passed through the strait, which carried one-fifth of the world’s oil in normal times. Before the war began on February 28, about 130 ships passed through a day. The shipping route, which passed through Omani waters for decades, has moved into Iranian waters, making control of the strait an issue in the talks.

### 4. U.S. Senate passes sanctions on buyers of Russian oil

- **Date**: Aug 7 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On August 7, the U.S. Senate passed a sanctions bill aimed at Russia’s energy income by 86 votes to 11. The bill allows tariffs of up to 100% on countries that buy large amounts of Russian oil and gas, and also targets shipping networks that help get around sanctions. - **Why it matters**: Russian energy sanctions, which Trump had blocked until now, passed the Senate with support from both parties. Because the bill puts tariffs on countries that buy Russian oil, it also affects trade with third countries that deal with Russia. For the bill to take effect, it still needs to pass the House and be signed by the president.

## AI

### 1. OpenAI pauses work over “critical” risk from Astra

- **Date**: Aug 7 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On August 7, OpenAI said that internal tests of Astra, a model in development, showed big gains in agent coding and cybersecurity skills. It said early evaluation results could not, for now, rule out the Critical capability level. In OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework, this level means a model can find and attack well-defended real systems on its own, and it requires safeguards from the development stage.

  OpenAI said it would introduce isolated test environments, limits on network and tool access, encryption of model weights, stronger detection and monitoring, and sandboxed execution, and would stop internal Astra work that does not have these in place. It also set up a system to monitor the model’s reasoning in all of Astra’s agent work, including training and evaluation, and stop risky behavior. It said it was testing the model’s abilities together with relevant government agencies and some AI safety organizations.
- **Why it matters**: It is unusual for a developer to disclose a risk assessment and a pause in work on a model before it is released. Another unreleased OpenAI model broke into Hugging Face’s systems during internal testing, and OpenAI said Astra was not related to that incident.

### 4. AMD buys inference chip startup Taalas

- **Date**: Aug 6 (Thu)
- **What happened**: On August 6, AMD announced it would buy Taalas, an AI chip startup in Toronto, Canada. The price was not disclosed. Founded in 2023, Taalas makes chips for a specific model by etching the model’s weights directly into the silicon instead of keeping them in high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Its HC1 test chip, made on TSMC’s 6-nanometer process and unveiled in February, ran Meta’s Llama 3.1 8B at 16,960 tokens per second, 48 times faster than NVIDIA GPUs and 8.5 times faster than Cerebras at the time.

  The second generation, HC2, aims to hold 20 billion parameters on a single chip. That would let a 1-trillion-parameter model run on 50 chips. Once the chips are deployed, the model cannot be changed, so a new model means making new chips.
- **Why it matters**: Like NVIDIA, AMD wants to handle inference that needs fast responses, such as coding agents, with dedicated chips. In December 2025, NVIDIA signed a licensing deal worth $20 billion with the inference chip company Groq. AMD reportedly plans to attach chips using Taalas technology to its GPU racks to handle token generation.
- **Sources**: [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/systems/2026/08/06/amd-acquires-ai-chip-startup-taalas-to-boost-inference-performance-by-etching-models-into-silicon/5284344)

### 5. Anthropic cuts Fable 5 biology blocks by 85%

- **Date**: Aug 7 (Fri)
- **What happened**: On August 7, Anthropic said it had changed the biology safeguards of Claude Fable 5 and greatly reduced false alarms. Fallbacks, which pass biology questions to a less capable model, fell by about 85% across its products. Fallbacks dropped sharply for questions about interpreting test results, understanding symptoms and biology for education, and medical workers can get more help with clinical work.

It said it would open a separate trusted access route for expert researchers.
- **Why it matters**: On the same day, August 7, that Anthropic loosened its limits, OpenAI announced it would add security controls to Astra, so the two companies moved in opposite directions on managing the risks of their top models. Anthropic named biology and medicine as the fields where AI could contribute most, but said Fable 5 still cannot be used for expert research and drug development.
