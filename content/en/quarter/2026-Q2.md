## Korea

### 1. Democratic Party wins 12 of 16 mayor and governor races

- **Development**: The June 3 local elections were the first nationwide vote since the Lee Jae-myung government took office a year earlier. In a Gallup Korea poll in early April, support for the Democratic Party was 48% and for the People Power Party 18%, and the People Power Party’s choice of candidates was delayed after the head of its nomination committee resigned.

  When candidate registration closed on May 15, the People Power Party had failed to field a candidate for mayor of Siheung, Gyeonggi, a city of 510,000 people. In the June 3 elections, the Democratic Party won 12 of the 16 big-city mayor and provincial governor posts. But in Seoul, Oh Se-hoon won the mayor’s race 49.22% to 48.07%, and in the National Assembly by-elections the People Power Party went from one seat to four.

  President Lee called the result a “warning” to his government and apologized, and 25 People Power Party lawmakers called on party leader Jang Dong-hyuk to resign, but Jang refused. Democratic Party leader Jung Chung-rae stepped down on June 24. Jung did not say directly that he would run, but the move was seen as a bid for another term at the August 17 party convention.
- **Why it matters**: It was the first time in more than 30 years of local elections that the main opposition party failed to field a candidate in a city of 510,000 people. The Democratic Party leader chosen in August will have the power to choose candidates for the 2028 general election during a two-year term.

### 2. KOSPI tops 9,000, then plunges 9.99% in a day

- **Development**: The KOSPI, which first passed 6,000 on February 25, fell 19.1% in March, then beat its pre-war record on April 21 and passed 6,500 during trading for the first time on the 23rd. On May 6 it rose 6.45% in a single day and passed 7,000 for the first time. With Samsung Electronics and SK hynix leading the rise, it closed above 8,000 on May 26. On the 27th, single-stock leveraged products that follow two times the daily moves of the two stocks were listed for the first time, and nearly 28 trillion won changed hands in them in three days.

  On June 8, the KOSPI fell 8.29% and a circuit breaker was triggered, and forced sales of stocks bought with borrowed money topped 100 billion won for three days in a row. On the 18th, chip stocks rose on news of the U.S.–Iran memorandum, and the KOSPI passed 9,000 for the first time. At that point, the two stocks made up 54.4% of the KOSPI’s market value.

  On June 23, the KOSPI fell 9.99% to close at 8,203.84, the largest drop ever in points, and the two stocks had their largest one-day falls since the 2008 financial crisis. That day, individual investors bought a record 8.59 trillion won in shares, and investors in the leveraged products lost about 25% in one day.
- **Why it matters**: With the index’s gains concentrated in two chip stocks, there were warnings from early May that the index could swing sharply if the outlook for chip profits turned. On June 23 alone, 514 trillion won of market value disappeared from the two stocks. Margin loans, money borrowed to buy stocks, reached 37.9797 trillion won on June 18, the most since the record set on May 29.

### 3. Samsung bonus dispute settled on eve of general strike

- **Development**: In mid-April, the Samsung Electronics unions demanded 15% of this year’s operating profit, which analysts forecast at 300 trillion won, as an uncapped bonus pool. The company said it would use 10% or more of operating profit if it ranked first in Korea, but the unions rejected this as a one-time reward. On the 16th, the company asked a court to ban strike action by the unions. The unions announced an 18-day general strike from May 21, and about 40,000 people gathered at a rally in Pyeongtaek on April 23.

  After mediation by the National Labor Relations Commission broke down on May 13, Chairman Lee Jae-yong apologized to the public on the 16th. On the 17th, Prime Minister Kim Min-seok raised the possibility of emergency adjustment in an address to the nation. On the night of the 20th, the eve of the general strike, the company and the unions reached a tentative deal. For 10 years, the chip division will get a bonus of 10.5% of business results in company shares with no cap.

  The deal was approved by 73.7% of union members on the 27th. Members in the memory division can get up to 600 million won, but those in the finished-products division get about 6 million won. So on the 28th, the union head said bargaining would be split by division and that there would be a vote of confidence in the leadership.
- **Why it matters**: Samsung Electronics holds 36% of the global DRAM market and 32% of NAND, so after the strike was announced, big tech companies abroad asked whether supply would be disrupted. The issue was how much of the profit from the chip boom should be shared as bonuses, and under what rules, and after the deal the conflict turned into a conflict among union members over the gaps between divisions.

### 4. Ballot shortage crisis; election commission chair resigns

- **Development**: Because early voting turnout was rising, the National Election Commission printed election-day ballots for the June 3 local elections for 50% of the number of voters. As of June 8, voting had stopped for a while on election day at 26 polling stations that ran out of ballots.

  Commission chair Noh Tae-ak and secretary general Heo Cheol-hoon apologized and offered to resign on June 5, and on the 7th President Lee Jae-myung ordered a joint investigation team of prosecutors and police. People Power Party leader Jang Dong-hyuk called for new elections nationwide, and protesters demanding new elections blocked a vote-counting center in Jamsil, Seoul, for the tenth day as of the 15th.

  On the 19th, the commission’s fact-finding committee recommended that 12 people, including Noh, be referred for investigation, and proposed that the commission come under the Board of Audit and Inspection’s oversight of public officials. A National Assembly special committee formed on the 18th was set to investigate for 45 days from the 23rd.
- **Why it matters**: The commission set a cautious printing standard to remove an excuse for election fraud claims that leftover ballots are used to rig elections, and that decision led to voting being stopped. After the “basket voting” controversy right after the 2022 presidential election, the commission’s chair and secretary general again stepped down together.

### 5. 26.2 trillion won war budget pays oil price relief

- **Development**: Crude oil supply was still disrupted more than a month after the war began on February 28. On April 1, the government decided to raise its crisis alert for crude oil to “Alert,” the third of four levels, from the next day. It also decided that from the 8th, 11,000 public institutions would let cars run only on alternate days by license plate. The day before, on March 31, it proposed a 26.2 trillion won supplementary budget, funded without new government bonds from 25.2 trillion won in extra tax revenue plus fund resources.

  The National Assembly passed the budget on April 10 without changing the total. Its core is 4.8 trillion won in relief payments for high oil prices, which give 100,000 to 600,000 won per person to the bottom 70% by income.

  Payments began on April 27 with vulnerable groups such as basic livelihood recipients. The money could only be used at shops with annual sales of 3 billion won or less, so only 36% of gas stations accepted it on the first day. From May 1, the government let people use payments received on cards at any gas station in their home area. Applications from the remaining 36 million or so people opened on May 18, with a deadline of July 3, and the money must be spent by August 31.
- **Why it matters**: A month into the war, the government decided to share the costs of the war in the Middle East directly with households. The government’s response to high oil prices was carried out on two tracks together: payments and cutting demand, such as limiting cars at public parking lots to four days in five.

## World

### 1. U.S. and Iran sign memorandum, but clashes in strait go on

- **Development**: In the war that began with U.S. and Israeli attacks on February 28, Iran all but closed the Strait of Hormuz, which carries a fifth of the world’s oil in normal times. In early April, U.S. President Donald Trump threatened to strike Iran’s power plants and bridges, and Iran said it would open the strait only after receiving war reparations.

  On April 7, the two countries agreed to a two-week ceasefire. But after the talks in Islamabad ended without a deal, the U.S. began a blockade of Iranian ports on the 13th. The ceasefire was extended indefinitely on the 21st, but the strait and the blockade stayed in place, and in early June Iran stopped the talks over Israel’s offensive in Lebanon and attacked Kuwait’s airport.

  On June 17, Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding to negotiate a final deal within 60 days and for Iran to restore passage through the strait. The U.S. lifted its blockade the next day. When a container ship was attacked in the strait on June 25, the U.S. struck Iranian facilities again.
- **Why it matters**: The memorandum said Iran’s highly enriched uranium would be diluted inside Iran under the supervision of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), but it left a final deal on the nuclear issue to 60 days of talks. By the time the memorandum was signed, the war had killed at least 7,000 people.

### 2. Israel and Lebanon sign framework deal, strikes continue

- **Development**: After a Hezbollah attack on March 2 drew Lebanon into the Iran war, Israel invaded southern Lebanon by land. On April 8, a few hours after the U.S.–Iran ceasefire was announced, Israel struck about 100 sites in Lebanon at once, killing more than 300 people.

  Israel and the Lebanese government held their first direct talks in decades in Washington on April 14, and the ceasefire that took effect on the 16th was extended again and again, but Israeli air strikes in the south continued. On May 31, the Israeli military crossed the Litani River and took Beaufort Castle, its deepest advance into Lebanon in 26 years.

  On June 3, the two governments agreed to renew the ceasefire on the condition that Hezbollah withdraw, but Hezbollah rejected it. On June 26, the two countries signed a 14-point framework agreement in Washington that includes a partial Israeli troop withdrawal, but the next day the Israeli military again carried out drone attacks in southern Lebanon.
- **Why it matters**: According to Lebanon’s health ministry, 3,516 people were killed from the time Lebanon was drawn into the war until early June, and according to the UN, more than 1 million people registered as displaced. Iran demanded an end to the war in Lebanon as a condition of a deal with the U.S., so the Lebanon front was also tied to the U.S.–Iran talks.

### 3. Big Russian strikes follow every Russia–Ukraine ceasefire

- **Development**: Hundreds of Patriot missiles were used to defend Gulf states early in the Iran war, and Ukraine has said it is running out of the interceptors it needs to stop ballistic missiles. On April 11, a 32-hour Orthodox Easter ceasefire that Russia had declared on its own began, and the two countries exchanged 175 prisoners each.   A three-day ceasefire from May 9 to 11 was brokered by President Trump, but on the 13th and 14th, after it ended, Russian strikes brought down an apartment building in Kyiv, killing at least 16 people. On the 15th, a Russian air attack set fire to the cathedral at the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, and on the 18th Ukraine carried out its largest attack on Moscow since the full-scale invasion.

### 4. Venezuela earthquakes kill at least 1,430

- **What happened**: On the evening of June 24, earthquakes of magnitude 7.2 and 7.5 struck northern Venezuela within one minute of each other, and hundreds of buildings collapsed. It was the country’s worst earthquake in more than 100 years, and La Guaira State, north of the capital Caracas, was hit hardest. By the 27th, the official death toll had risen to 1,430, and nearly 3,500 people were injured. The UN humanitarian chief said more than 50,000 people were missing.
- **Why it matters**: The official death toll almost doubled in a single day on June 26. Until rescue teams arrived, residents had to search with their bare hands and shovels, and criticism of the government’s slow response grew among families of the missing.

### 5. WHO declares Ebola in Congo and Uganda an emergency

- **Development**: On May 15, the health ministry of the Democratic Republic of the Congo announced an Ebola outbreak in Ituri province in the east. The Bundibugyo virus behind this outbreak has no approved vaccine or treatment.

  On the 17th, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the Ebola outbreak spreading in Congo and Uganda a public health emergency of international concern. Suspected cases passed 900 on the 24th, up from about 200 when the outbreak was announced, and in the east residents burned treatment centers and attacked hospitals.

  On June 5, the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said about 400 cases had been confirmed, 63 of them fatal. The same day, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) projected that cases could reach 10,000 to more than 20,000 depending on how fast patients are isolated. The outbreak was still going on at the end of the quarter.
- **Why it matters**: A strain with no usable vaccine reached the capitals of both Congo and Uganda soon after it was found. In the east, where armed groups are active, residents attacked and set fire to treatment facilities, blocking basic measures such as isolation and safe burials.

## AI

### 1. Anthropic’s Mythos: from limited release to government block

- **Development**: On April 7, Anthropic said its new model Claude Mythos Preview had found thousands of serious vulnerabilities in major operating systems and browsers and written attack code without human direction. It did not release the model to the public and offered it to only about 40 organizations. Anthropic was fighting in court with the Defense Department, which had labeled it a supply chain risk in February. Meanwhile, it was reported on April 20 that the National Security Agency, part of the department, was using Mythos to find vulnerabilities.

  On April 21, it was reported that a group without permission had been accessing Mythos with the access rights of a contractor’s employee, and Anthropic began an investigation. On June 2, Anthropic added about 150 organizations in more than 15 countries, mostly in power, water, health care and telecommunications.

  On June 9, Claude Fable 5, the first public release of a Mythos-class model, came out. But on the 12th, the U.S. government issued an export control order, citing national security, to keep foreigners from using it, and Anthropic took Fable 5 and Mythos 5 away from all users. On June 26, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick allowed about 100 U.S. agencies and companies that run critical infrastructure to use Mythos 5 again, but the export control order was not lifted and Fable 5 was not released again.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. government used export controls to pull back a commercial AI model that had already been released, showing in practice that the government can decide who gets to use frontier models. By the end of June, Anthropic was offering Mythos 5 only to organizations allowed by the government. At the government’s request, OpenAI was offering GPT-5.6 only to a small number of partners whose participation had been shared with the government.

### 2. Musk v. OpenAI dismissed as filed too late

- **Development**: Elon Musk sued to reverse OpenAI’s conversion from its nonprofit purpose to a for-profit company and to remove Sam Altman and Greg Brockman, and the jury trial began at the federal court in Oakland on April 28. In his testimony, asked whether his company xAI had trained Grok by distilling OpenAI’s models, Musk answered that it had, in part.

  Brockman testified that Musk demanded full control of the company in 2017. Helen Toner, a board member at the time, testified that when Altman was fired in 2023, the board discussed a merger with Anthropic. Altman, testifying on May 12, replied that they had created one of the largest charitable foundations in the world.

  On May 18, the nine jurors unanimously rejected the claims as having been filed too late, and the judge accepted the verdict. Musk said the ruling rested on a technicality about dates and that he would appeal.
- **Why it matters**: If Musk had won, OpenAI’s for-profit structure could have fallen apart, and the dismissal removed the risk of a lawsuit that could have forced a restructuring of OpenAI ahead of its listing. Musk’s testimony confirmed in public for the first time that U.S. labs also use distillation, training their own models by sending huge numbers of questions to a rival’s model.

### 3. Government review order, then limited GPT-5.6 release

- **Development**: In May, as models like Anthropic’s Mythos that find software vulnerabilities in large numbers raised security concerns in government and finance, U.S. President Donald Trump called off a signing ceremony for an executive order on government review of new AI models a few hours before it was to take place. The May draft set the review period at 90 days.

  In early June, Trump signed an executive order with a shorter period, reflecting industry demands. It asks AI companies to give the government their newest models with strong cyber capabilities up to 30 days before release, and the order says this cannot be read as creating a mandatory government license or prior approval.

  On June 26, at the U.S. government’s request, OpenAI released its new GPT-5.6 family of models as a preview only to a small number of partners whose participation had been shared with the government. OpenAI said this kind of government access process should not become the long-term default and that it would release the models to the public within weeks.
- **Why it matters**: The executive order has no binding force, so whether models are actually reviewed depends on companies taking part, but at the end of June a government request limited which organizations could use a new model first. OpenAI said it was building, with the government, a process to repeat each time it releases a model.

### 4. Anthropic raises $65 billion, then files to go public

- **Development**: Anthropic’s annualized revenue rose from about $9 billion at the end of 2025 to more than $47 billion in early May. The company signed computing deals with Amazon for up to 5 gigawatts and with Google and Broadcom for 5 gigawatts, and agreed to use GPUs at SpaceX’s Colossus 1 and 2.

  On May 28, Anthropic raised $65 billion in a Series H round at a post-money valuation of $965 billion. That is higher than the $852 billion OpenAI was valued at in its March funding round, and Samsung Electronics, SK hynix and Micron also joined as strategic infrastructure partners.

  Less than a week later, on June 1, Anthropic confidentially submitted a draft registration statement for an initial public offering to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. It did not set the number of shares or the price, and on the 4th co-founder Daniela Amodei gave the huge upfront costs of training models and serving inference as the reason for listing.
- **Why it matters**: In the race over whether OpenAI or Anthropic lists first, Anthropic took the first step. The same week, Alphabet decided to sell $85 billion in shares to pay for AI infrastructure, and SpaceX was set to list on June 12, so large AI-related fundraising moved to public markets all at once.

### 5. OpenAI raises $122 billion, then files to go public

- **Development**: On March 31, OpenAI closed a $122 billion funding round at a valuation of $852 billion. SoftBank led the round with Amazon, NVIDIA and Microsoft taking part, and about $3 billion came from individual investors through banks.

  OpenAI said it had $2 billion in monthly revenue and more than 900 million weekly consumer users, and it widened its shareholder base through individual investors and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) to prepare for an expected initial public offering. Anthropic was valued at $965 billion in its May funding round and filed its listing papers first, on June 1.

  A week later, on June 8, OpenAI confidentially submitted a draft registration statement (S-1) to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. OpenAI said it had not yet set a timing and that the filing gave it the option to list quickly.
- **Why it matters**: OpenAI raised $122 billion, but it expects to still be burning $85 billion in cash in 2028. Its investors include NVIDIA, Microsoft and Amazon, from which OpenAI buys chips and cloud services.
