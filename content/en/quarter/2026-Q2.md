## Korea

### 1. Democratic Party wins 12 of 16 mayor and governor races

- **Development**: In early April, support for the Democratic Party was 48% and for the People Power Party 18%, and the People Power Party’s choice of candidates was delayed after the head of its nomination committee resigned. When candidate registration closed on May 15, the People Power Party had failed to field a candidate for mayor of Siheung, Gyeonggi, a city of 510,000 people.

  In the June 3 elections, the Democratic Party won 12 of the 16 big-city mayor and provincial governor posts. But in Seoul, Oh Se-hoon won the mayor’s race 49.22% to 48.07%, and in the National Assembly by-elections the People Power Party went from one seat to four. President Lee Jae-myung called the result a “warning” to his government and apologized, and 25 People Power Party lawmakers called on party leader Jang Dong-hyuk to resign. Jang refused. Democratic Party leader Jung Chung-rae stepped down on June 24. Jung did not say directly that he would run, but the move was seen as a bid for another term at the August 17 party convention.
- **Why it matters**: It was the first nationwide election since the Lee Jae-myung government took office a year earlier. It was the first time in more than 30 years of local elections that the main opposition party failed to field a candidate in a city of 510,000 people. The Democratic Party leader chosen in August will have the power to choose candidates for the 2028 general election during a two-year term.

### 2. KOSPI tops 9,000, then plunges 9.99% in a day

- **Development**: The KOSPI, which first passed 6,000 on February 25, rose 6.45% in a single day on May 6 and passed 7,000 for the first time. With Samsung Electronics and SK hynix leading the rise, it closed above 8,000 on May 26, and the next day single-stock leveraged products that follow two times the daily moves of the two stocks were listed for the first time.

  On June 8, the KOSPI fell 8.29% and a circuit breaker was triggered, and when it first passed 9,000 on the 18th, the two stocks made up 54.4% of the KOSPI’s market value. On June 23, the KOSPI fell 9.99% to close at 8,203.84, the largest drop ever in points, and 514 trillion won of market value disappeared from the two stocks alone. That day, investors in the leveraged products lost about 25% in one day.
- **Why it matters**: With the index’s gains concentrated in two chip stocks, there were warnings from early May that the index could swing sharply if the outlook for chip profits turned. Margin loans, money borrowed to buy stocks, reached 37.9797 trillion won on June 18, the most since the record set on May 29.

### 3. Samsung bonus dispute settled on eve of general strike

- **Development**: In mid-April, the Samsung Electronics unions demanded that 15% of operating profit go into the bonus pool with no cap and announced an 18-day general strike from May 21. About 40,000 people gathered at a rally in Pyeongtaek on April 23. After mediation by the National Labor Relations Commission broke down on May 13, Chairman Lee Jae-yong apologized to the public, and Prime Minister Kim Min-seok raised the possibility of emergency adjustment in an address to the nation.

  On the night of May 20, the eve of the general strike, the company and the unions reached a tentative deal. For 10 years, the chip division will get a bonus of 10.5% of business results in company shares with no cap. The deal was approved by 73.7% of union members on the 27th. Members in the memory division can get up to 600 million won, but those in the finished-products division get about 6 million won. So on the 28th, the union head said bargaining would be split by division and that there would be a vote of confidence in the leadership.
- **Why it matters**: Samsung Electronics holds 36% of the global DRAM market and 32% of NAND, so after the strike was announced, big tech companies abroad asked whether supply would be disrupted. The issue was how much of the profit from the chip boom should be shared as bonuses, and under what rules, and after the deal the conflict turned into a conflict among union members over the gaps between divisions.

### 4. 26.2 trillion won war budget pays oil price relief

- **What happened**: The 26.2 trillion won supplementary budget the government proposed in response to the war in the Middle East passed the National Assembly on April 10. Relief payments for high oil prices of 100,000 to 600,000 won per person began on April 27 for vulnerable groups and on May 18 for the rest of the bottom 70% by income.
- **Why it matters**: A month into the war, the government decided to share the costs of the war in the Middle East directly with households. The government’s response to high oil prices was carried out on two tracks together: payments and cutting demand, such as limiting cars at public parking lots to four days in five.

### 5. Ballot shortage crisis; election commission chair resigns

- **What happened**: Because the election commission printed election-day ballots for 50% of the number of voters, voting stopped for a while at 26 polling stations on June 3. After National Election Commission chair Noh Tae-ak stepped down, a fact-finding committee recommended on June 19 that 12 people, including Noh, be referred for investigation.
- **Why it matters**: The commission set a cautious printing standard to remove an excuse for election fraud claims that leftover ballots are used to rig elections, and that decision led to voting being stopped. After the “basket voting” controversy right after the 2022 presidential election, the commission’s chair and secretary general again stepped down together.

## World

### 1. U.S. and Iran sign memorandum, but clashes in strait go on

- **Development**: In early April, the U.S. threatened to strike Iran’s power plants and bridges, while Iran kept the Strait of Hormuz closed and demanded war reparations. On April 7, the two countries agreed to a two-week ceasefire. But after the talks in Islamabad broke down, the U.S. began a blockade of Iranian ports on April 13, and the strait stayed closed even after the ceasefire was extended indefinitely.

  In early June, Iran stopped the talks over Israel’s offensive in Lebanon and attacked Kuwait’s airport, and after rounds of U.S. air strikes, a deal to end the war was announced on June 14. On June 17, U.S. President Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding to negotiate a final deal within 60 days and for Iran to restore passage through the strait, and the U.S. lifted its blockade the next day. When a container ship was attacked in the strait on June 25, the U.S. struck Iranian facilities again.
- **Why it matters**: The strait, which carries a fifth of the world’s oil in normal times, had been all but closed since the war began on February 28. The memorandum said Iran’s highly enriched uranium would be diluted inside Iran under the supervision of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), but it left a final deal on the nuclear issue to 60 days of talks. By the time the memorandum was signed, the war had killed at least 7,000 people.

### 2. Israel and Lebanon sign framework deal, strikes continue

- **Development**: On April 8, a few hours after the U.S.–Iran ceasefire was announced, Israel struck about 100 sites in Lebanon at once, killing more than 300 people. Israel and the Lebanese government held their first direct talks in decades in Washington on April 14, and the ceasefire that took effect on the 16th was extended again and again, but Israeli air strikes in the south continued. On May 31, the Israeli military crossed the Litani River and took Beaufort Castle.

  On June 3, the two governments agreed to renew the ceasefire on the condition that Hezbollah withdraw, but Hezbollah rejected it. On June 26, the two countries signed a 14-point framework agreement in Washington that includes a partial Israeli troop withdrawal, but the next day the Israeli military again carried out drone attacks in southern Lebanon.
- **Why it matters**: According to Lebanon’s health ministry, 3,516 people were killed between March 2, when Lebanon was drawn into the war, and early June, and according to the UN, more than 1 million people registered as displaced. Iran demanded an end to the war in Lebanon as a condition of a deal with the U.S., so the Lebanon front was also tied to the U.S.–Iran talks.

### 3. Big Russian strikes follow every Russia–Ukraine ceasefire

- **Development**: On April 11, a 32-hour Orthodox Easter ceasefire that Russia had declared on its own began, and the two countries exchanged 175 prisoners each. - **Why it matters**: Hundreds of Patriot missiles were used to defend Gulf states early in the Iran war, and Ukraine has said it is running out of the interceptors it needs to stop ballistic missiles. ## AI

### 1. Anthropic’s Mythos: from limited release to government block

- **Development**: On April 7, Anthropic did not release its new model Claude Mythos Preview to the public, saying it had found thousands of vulnerabilities in major operating systems and browsers, and instead offered it to only about 40 organizations. While the National Security Agency was using Mythos, it was reported that a group without permission had been accessing the model with the access rights of a contractor’s employee, and Anthropic began an investigation. On June 2, Anthropic added about 150 more organizations in more than 15 countries.

  On June 9, Claude Fable 5, the first public release of a Mythos-class model, came out. But on the 12th, the U.S. government issued an export control order, citing national security, to keep foreigners from using it, and Anthropic took Fable 5 and Mythos 5 away from all users. On June 26, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick allowed about 100 U.S. agencies and companies that run critical infrastructure to use Mythos 5 again. The export control order was not lifted, and Fable 5 was not released again.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. government used export controls to pull back a commercial AI model that had already been released, showing in practice that the government can decide who gets to use frontier models. By the end of June, Anthropic was offering Mythos 5 only to organizations allowed by the government. At the government’s request, OpenAI was offering GPT-5.6 only to a small number of partners whose participation had been shared with the government.

### 3. Musk v. OpenAI dismissed as filed too late

- **Development**: On April 28, the jury trial of Elon Musk’s lawsuit seeking to reverse OpenAI’s conversion to a for-profit company and to remove Sam Altman and Greg Brockman began at the federal court in Oakland. In his testimony, asked whether his company xAI had trained Grok by distilling OpenAI’s models, Musk answered that it had, in part. Brockman testified that Musk demanded full control of the company in 2017, and Altman, testifying on May 12, replied that they had created one of the largest charitable foundations in the world.

  On May 18, the nine jurors unanimously rejected the claims as having been filed too late, and the judge accepted the verdict. Musk said he would appeal.
- **Why it matters**: If Musk had won, OpenAI’s for-profit structure could have fallen apart, and the dismissal removed the risk of a lawsuit that could have forced a restructuring of OpenAI ahead of its listing. Musk’s testimony confirmed in public for the first time that U.S. labs also use distillation, training their own models by sending huge numbers of questions to a rival’s model.
