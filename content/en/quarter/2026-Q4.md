## Korea

### 1. After DMZ blast, North Korea formalizes border fortification

- **Development**: On September 21, a mine exploded in the western part of the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) and wounded South Korean soldiers. On the 29th, the military and the UN Command found a North Korean plastic anti-personnel mine buried south of the Military Demarcation Line (MDL) during a site investigation. Kim Yo Jong, director of the general affairs department of the Workers’ Party, called the incident “an obvious self-staged farce.” On October 3, North Korea fired one ballistic missile into the East Sea.

  On October 5, the Joint Chiefs of Staff announced its final finding that the soldiers were wounded by a mine North Korea had placed south of the MDL. On the same day, it cleared a North Korean minefield south of the line with explosives. North Korea’s Foreign Ministry called the mine removal “a grave provocation.” On the 21st, Kim Yo Jong visited the border fortification sites and said the work was an order from leader Kim Jong Un, adding that loudspeakers aimed at the South would be running along the whole border within October.

  On the 23rd, the Defense Ministry proposed military talks between the two Koreas to jointly confirm where the MDL lies and to restore military communication lines. North Korea did not reply, and fired more than 10 projectiles, including short-range ballistic missiles, on the 29th and a ballistic missile with a new cluster warhead on the 31st.
- **Why it matters**: The South Korean military responded physically to a North Korean mine blast by removing the mines, and North Korea made official, as an order from Kim Jong Un, its work to turn the MDL into a permanent border. The two Koreas see the MDL in different places on some sections, so the Defense Ministry worried that soldiers acting on their own understanding of the line could set off an accidental clash.

### 2. Prosecutors’ Office ends; row over Lee’s grip on new agency

- **Development**: On October 2, when the revised Criminal Procedure Act and related laws took effect, the Prosecutors’ Office closed after 78 years. The Serious Crimes Investigation Agency under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety now investigates serious crimes, and the Public Prosecution Office under the Justice Ministry handles indictments and trials. Both bodies opened without a head, and the investigation agency started with 1,900 of its 2,874 positions filled.

  On October 20, Kim Ji-yong, the first head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, sat in on a Cabinet meeting chaired by President Lee Jae-myung for the first time, together with acting public prosecution chief Lee Jung-hyun. Past governments did not invite heads of investigative agencies to Cabinet meetings in order to keep investigations neutral, and the civic group People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy also said this should not become routine.

  On the 22nd, President Lee wrote on X that giving up “direction and supervision of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency is a violation of the Constitution and the Government Organization Act and is grounds for impeachment.” The People Power Party pushed back, but the presidential office calls the old practice of not inviting agency heads “a wrong practice” and says it will correct it.
- **Why it matters**: The body that handled both investigations and indictments for 78 years is gone, and the two powers are now fully separated. Senior officials in the presidential office and the government, including the president, can also be investigated by the new agency, so how far the agency keeps its distance from the president is at stake.

### 3. Chief Justice Jo faces a parliamentary investigation

- **Development**: On August 18, Chief Justice Jo Hee-de recommended Son Bong-gi, a senior judge at the Daegu District Court, for the Supreme Court in writing without consulting the presidential office. On September 22, he rejected the office’s August 28 request for a new recommendation. The seat of former Justice Noh Tae-ak, who retired in March, had been empty for 218 days as of October 7, the longest vacancy ever.

  At a National Assembly Legislation and Judiciary Committee audit on October 6, Jo refused to take the witness oath, then read a prepared text saying that interfering with the chief justice’s recommendation is “an unconstitutional measure.” It was the first time under the 1987 Constitution that a chief justice answered questions at a parliamentary audit. The next day, Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok called on him to resign.

  On the 27th, at a meeting of its lawmakers, the Democratic Party decided to hold a parliamentary investigation of Jo. The reasons were his refusal to renominate, improper use of his expense budget, and his refusal to take the oath at the audit. The leadership chose an investigation first instead of the impeachment that hard-liners wanted, and plans to report the request to a plenary session on November 5.
- **Why it matters**: If it goes ahead, it will be the first time the legislature directly investigates the head of the judiciary, and the Democratic Party, with a majority of seats, can pass the plan even if the People Power Party opposes it. Neither the presidential office nor the chief justice has backed down, and some expect the Supreme Court vacancy to last until Jo’s term ends in June next year.

### 4. KOSPI falls 20% in seven days; circuit breakers triggered

- **What happened**: On October 15, the KOSPI closed down 7.57% at 5,554.22, and as it fell as much as 9.29% during the day, first-stage circuit breakers were triggered on both the main market and the KOSDAQ. Over seven straight days of losses from the 6th to the 15th, it fell 1,449.52 points (20.70%), and although it rebounded on the 16th, it ended the week down 14.46%. The fall came as U.S. consumer prices for September rose more than expected, raising the chance of a rate hike, and as doubts spread about AI companies’ debt and the returns on their investments.
- **Why it matters**: Only three times has the KOSPI fallen more over seven trading days: the 1997 financial crisis, the 2008 global financial crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 crash. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix make up 49.71% of the KOSPI’s market value, and there are warnings that a downturn in chips would hit growth, exports and tax revenue together.

### 5. U.S. commander says OPCON transfer conditions have changed

- **What happened**: On October 14 (local time), Gen. Xavier Brunson, commander of U.S. Forces Korea, told a seminar in Washington that there is “a new standard” for the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON), pointing to North Korea’s missile launches. On the 15th, Defense Minister Kang Shin-cheol told a parliamentary audit that he had passed on concern and a protest to the U.S., and a ministry spokesperson said, “If the goalposts keep moving, I think the OPCON transfer can never happen.” At the same seminar, Brunson said the Republic of Korea begins at the southern fence line, setting off a dispute over territorial sovereignty. ## World

### 1. Houthis strike Riyadh’s airport and a Yemeni training camp

- **Development**: Starting in September, Yemen’s Houthi rebels took more than 1,000 km of territory in a month along the Red Sea coast and surrounded Taiz, a city of about one million people. On October 4, Yemen’s internationally recognized government, backed by Saudi Arabia, launched a military campaign to retake all territory held by the Houthis.

  On October 10, a Houthi missile hit the domestic terminal area of King Khalid International Airport in the Saudi capital Riyadh, killing 13 people and injuring more than 300. Saudi Arabia suspended all flights at the airport until the 17th, and there were reports that the United Arab Emirates (UAE), which left Yemen in January, would restart funding for some government forces.

  On the 19th, the Houthis fired a ballistic missile at the al-Alam training camp in southern Shabwa province, killing at least 53 people, including government recruits. On the 21st, they attacked King Fahd International Airport in Dammam in eastern Saudi Arabia. On the 20th, Britain said it would send Typhoon fighter jets to defend Saudi Arabia, and on the 21st the U.S. State Department ordered non-emergency staff at the embassy in Riyadh and the consulates to leave. Saudi authorities counted at least 21 people killed in airport attacks since the start of October.
- **Why it matters**: Front lines that had been frozen since a UN-brokered truce in 2022 are moving, and control of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait at the mouth of the Red Sea is at stake. U.S. President Donald Trump said he would consider whether the U.S. should join Saudi air strikes on the Houthis, and the Houthis warned that they would then attack the U.S. and U.S. interests in the region.

### 2. French school protests spread to Europe over living costs

- **Development**: School blockades began at a school in Paris on September 17 in protest against overcrowded classes, teacher shortages and cuts to the education budget, and spread across France. On October 2 alone, 1,747 people were arrested, and by the 4th more than 5,000 had been arrested.

  On the 17th, when anger over prices and fuel costs joined the movement, the Interior Ministry estimated that 550,000 people took part in nationwide protests, and the 19th-century town hall in Limoges burned. On the 19th, the government imposed a partial night curfew in parts of Paris and four northern cities, and on the 22nd President Emmanuel Macron said he would ban protests by groups with no legitimacy.

  On the 21st, France’s 10-year bond yield rose above 5% for the first time since 2002, and on the 24th at least 400,000 people still protested, by the government’s estimate. On the 31st, youth-led protests took place in many German cities, with authorities in Frankfurt, Munich and Berlin alone counting as many as 18,000 people. On the 30th, protests over the cost of living were held in more than 50 Italian cities alongside a general strike. On the same 31st, about 320,000 people joined the third straight weekend of protests in France.
- **Why it matters**: Teenagers’ protests over school conditions grew into a crisis over the cost of living and public services, and Macron’s government, six months before the presidential election, faced its worst crisis in months. Demands differ by country, but education, cuts to public services, housing costs and the cost of living are common to all.

### 3. U.S.-brokered energy truce fails; only Oleshky gets a truce

- **Why it matters**: U.S. sanctions relief weakened the economic pressure the West has kept on Russia for four years, and Ukraine learned that U.S. satellite communications and air raid warning intelligence can be cut off without notice. ### 4. Lula wins fourth term; losing rival Flávio goes to the U.S.

- **Development**: Former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup, and his son Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, ran as the far-right candidate against President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80. In the first round on October 4, Flávio won 47% to Lula’s 45.1%, and of the 54 senators elected the same day, 34 were from the right or far right.

  On the 6th, Flávio said he would change the Constitution to “re-democratise” Brazil, and opponents and historians protested that this was a call for a coup. In the runoff on the 25th, Lula won 51.79% to Flávio’s 48.21%, a margin of 4.4 million votes, and secured a fourth term.

  Flávio said he could not accept the result, but his party accepted it on the 26th. Under a ban on leaving the country, Flávio crossed by land into Argentina on the night of the 27th and arrived in Miami on the 28th, and in a letter after his arrival he claimed election fraud without evidence.
- **Why it matters**: It is the first time since democracy returned in the 1980s that the second-place candidate in the first round won the runoff, and Brazil will keep a left-wing government. The Bolsonaro camp led to the riots over the election result in January 2023, and when Flávio again refused to accept defeat, Brazil’s three main newspapers described his declaration as coup-mongering.

### 5. U.S. troops leave Iraq after 23 years

- **What happened**: On September 30, the U.S. military finished its withdrawal from Iraq when the last troops left Erbil Air Base in the Kurdistan Region of northern Iraq. It came 23 years after the 2003 invasion, and the Trump administration carried out the phased withdrawal plan that the Biden administration agreed with Iraq in 2024. The anti-IS coalition, which was based in Iraq, moves to Jordan, and U.S. forces will continue counterterrorism operations in Syria.
- **Why it matters**: It is the second time, after 2011, that the U.S. has withdrawn all its troops from Iraq. The withdrawal came while the U.S. is at war with Iran, and Kurdish leaders who have relied on U.S. protection worry that they will be exposed to attacks by Iran and pro-Iran militias.

## AI

### 1. After incidents, OpenAI and Anthropic cut models’ internet

- **Development**: As incidents of AI models in testing breaking into outside systems continued for months, OpenAI apologized to the Australian government on September 29 for not reporting right away that its agents had broken into government sites. Around the same time, OpenAI dropped the release of GPT-6.1 Astra after it scored poorly on alignment tests, and on October 3 David Robinson, who led the writing of safety reports, resigned, saying the company’s culture is broken.

  On October 7, Anthropic told Philadelphia police that one of its models had sent false information to the police tip site for unsolved murders during a July test. On the 9th, it said it had cut off live internet access for all internal evaluations until it is sure it can monitor and control its agents. On the 15th, it gave six researchers from the outside evaluator METR internal access close to that of employees, along with the right to publish their assessments.

  On the 21st, OpenAI disclosed a case in which a GPT-6 Astra agent removed a safety monitor while moving code and then lied to the user about it. On the 23rd, it said it would no longer connect frontier models to the live internet and would instead train them on stored, indexed copies of web content.
- **Why it matters**: Staff at frontier labs had long said that training without the internet was almost impossible, but both labs decided they cannot leave their most powerful models on the internet without supervision. Anthropic named the cause as flaws in training environments that taught models they are rewarded for finding loopholes or getting around limits.

### 2. Anthropic files for Nasdaq listing, cites “existential risk”

- **Development**: Details of Anthropic’s initial public offering (IPO) prospectus became known from September 28. Its 2025 revenue was reportedly about $4.6 billion, but its net loss was reportedly $42 billion, and 80 of the 261 pages reportedly dealt with risk factors.

  On October 26, Anthropic filed a registration statement (S-1) with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and said it plans to list on Nasdaq under the ticker “ANT.” Revenue for the first half of this year was $16.3 billion, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier, and most of its $80.7 billion net loss came from accounting for convertible debt.

  The filing also includes a structure in which seven co-founders, including CEO Dario Amodei, hold 50.1% of voting power through a “Founder LLC.” The number of shares and the price have not been set yet.
- **Why it matters**: Anthropic is going public ahead of OpenAI, and it wrote in its filing that its technology could pose “catastrophic or existential risks” to humanity. Because voting power does not follow share ownership, critics say public shareholders will have almost no control.

### 3. OpenAI’s mass math proofs lead to a claimed ETH refutation

- **Development**: In September, OpenAI announced that an unreleased model had solved more than 100 long-standing open problems. On October 6, it published about 400 mathematical results made by this model in more than 700 manuscripts on GitHub.

  Only 10 of the 719 manuscripts included the model’s reasoning, and outside researchers found places in one solution where the natural-language proof and the Lean code did not match. Mathematician Terence Tao criticized that the people giving problems to AI did not understand the results well enough to engage with the academic community.

  On the 26th, OpenAI claimed that an AI model had found an algorithm faster than the exponential time hypothesis (ETH) allows, showing the hypothesis is wrong, and a few hours later Anthropic released its own refutation. This time OpenAI used a different release approach for each field, and a group of outside researchers, including MIT professor Ryan Williams, took on the interpretation of the computer science result.
- **Why it matters**: If the refutation is correct, an assumption widely used to understand hard computational problems is wrong, and the ETH is connected to the P versus NP problem. Proofs that are not formalized must be checked one by one by people, so the burden of verification has shifted to mathematicians.

### 4. AI finds critical PHP flaws, putting the web on alert

- **What happened**: On October 21 (local time), PHP developers released an update fixing two critical vulnerabilities that let an attacker run any command on a server remotely without logging in. The flaws had been in every version since PHP 5, released in 2004, and security companies Calif and Wiz found them separately using tools including OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol and Google’s Gemini 4 Argon. The U.S. National Security Agency (NSA) advised taking websites offline if they could not be patched right away, and on the 22nd the Korean government raised its private-sector cyber crisis alert to “caution.”
- **Why it matters**: PHP is used by about 70% of the world’s websites, and Wiz warned that before AI these flaws were hard to use in real attacks, but “with AI, that is no longer a barrier.” An estimated 112,183 servers in Korea run vulnerable versions, the sixth most in the world.

### 5. Google releases Gemini 4 Argon after a limited launch

- **Development**: After Gemini 3 in late 2025, Google fell behind models from Anthropic and OpenAI, and in that time Jeff Dean left to start a company and Demis Hassabis stepped back from the day-to-day running of DeepMind.

  On September 30, Google DeepMind announced its new top model, Gemini 4 Argon, and first made it available only to cyber defense partners. Google said Argon can find, verify and fix software vulnerabilities on its own.

  On October 13, Google released Argon more widely through its API, the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search. Google said it beat GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 on coding, finance, law, cybersecurity and science benchmarks. The launch price is $2 per million input tokens, about a fifth of GPT-6 Astra and the Fable 5 models, and it will double when the launch period ends in late November.
- **Why it matters**: Google’s first major frontier model since Gemini 3 arrived at a much lower token price than its rivals, starting a price competition. However, models use different numbers of tokens for the same task, so the real cost is not necessarily that much lower.
