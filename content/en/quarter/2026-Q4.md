## Korea

### 1. DMZ mine blast leads to border standoff with North Korea

- **Development**: On September 21, a mine exploded in the western part of the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) and wounded South Korean soldiers. North Korea has been fortifying the area around the Military Demarcation Line (MDL) as a border under its “two hostile states” policy. Since North Korea cut the liaison channels in 2024, there has been no working line of communication between the two Koreas.

  On October 5, the Joint Chiefs of Staff released its final finding that the soldiers were wounded by a plastic anti-personnel mine North Korea had placed south of the MDL. On the same day, the military cleared a North Korean minefield south of the line with explosives. North Korea’s Foreign Ministry called the mine removal “a grave provocation” on the 6th. On the 21st, Kim Yo Jong, director of the general affairs department of the Workers’ Party, inspected border fortification sites and said loudspeakers aimed at the South would be running along the whole border “within October.”

  On the 23rd, the Defense Ministry formally proposed military talks to North Korea to jointly confirm where the MDL lies. North Korea did not reply and fired more than 10 projectiles, including short-range ballistic missiles, on the 29th. On the 30th, it released footage of suicide drones hitting a model of a South Korean frontline guard post. As of late October, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the National Intelligence Service had not confirmed that the loudspeakers were running.
- **Why it matters**: The two Koreas see the MDL in different places on some sections, so there are concerns that South Korean troops removing mines and North Korean troops doing fortification work could meet and set off an accidental clash. The Joint Chiefs of Staff estimates that North Korea has buried about 960,000 mines around the DMZ since 2024, up to five times the estimated total laid in the 70-odd years after the Korean War.

### 2. Prosecutors’ Office abolished; row over new agency

- **Development**: On October 2, the revised Criminal Procedure Act and related laws that abolish the Prosecutors’ Office took effect. The Serious Crimes Investigation Agency under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety now investigates serious crimes, and the Public Prosecution Office under the Justice Ministry handles indictments and trials. The Public Prosecution Office cannot investigate and can only ask for further investigation.

  Both bodies opened without a head, and the investigation agency started with 1,900 of its 2,874 positions filled, before finishing office construction and its computer network. On the 20th, President Lee Jae-myung had Kim Ji-yong, the agency’s first head, and acting public prosecution chief Lee Jung-hyun sit in on a Cabinet meeting, and asked them to investigate “without any sanctuary.”

  The civic group People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy and others criticized the move and said such attendance should not become routine. On the 22nd, President Lee wrote that giving up “direction and supervision of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency is a violation of the Constitution and the Government Organization Act and is grounds for impeachment.” The presidential office called the old practice of not inviting the heads of investigative agencies “an abnormal, wrong practice” and said it would correct it.
- **Why it matters**: The body that handled both investigations and indictments for 78 years is gone, and the two powers are now fully separated. The new agency can investigate senior officials in the presidential office and the government, including the president, so how far it keeps its distance from the president has become a point of dispute.

### 3. KOSPI halted twice, falls 44% from its peak

- **Development**: The KOSPI hit a record high of 9,114.55 on June 22, and two stocks, Samsung Electronics and SK hynix, made up 49.71% of its market value.

  U.S. consumer prices for September rose more than expected, and corporate bond prices of AI cloud company CoreWeave plunged, spreading worries about returns on AI investment. The KOSPI then fell for seven straight trading days from October 6 to 15, losing 20.70%. On the 15th, it fell as much as 9.29% during the day, and circuit breakers were triggered on both the main KOSPI market and the KOSDAQ.

  After the Democrats won both houses of the U.S. Congress in the November 3 midterm elections, worries about regulation of AI data centers grew, and the KOSPI fell 5.28% on the 5th and 6.02% on the 6th. A circuit breaker was triggered again on the 6th, and the index briefly fell below 5,000. Its closing level of 5,059.69 was 44.5% below the June 22 level. The same day, the government said it would exempt brokerages from the rule to keep a 140% collateral ratio on margin loans for three months from the 9th.
- **Why it matters**: Over seven trading days, the KOSPI has fallen more than it did in mid-October only three times: in the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 2008 global financial crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 crash. Because chips make up a large share of the Korean market, it fell more than the Japanese and Taiwanese markets on the same days both times.

### 4. Chief Justice Jo faces parliamentary investigation

- **Development**: On August 18, Chief Justice Jo Hee-de recommended Son Bong-gi, a senior judge at the Daegu District Court, for the Supreme Court in writing without consulting the presidential office. On September 22, he rejected the office’s August 28 request for a new recommendation. The seat of former Justice Noh Tae-ak, who retired in March, had been empty for 218 days as of October 7, the longest vacancy ever.

  At a National Assembly Legislation and Judiciary Committee audit on October 6, Jo appeared as a witness, refused to take the oath, and said that interfering with the chief justice’s recommendation is “an unconstitutional measure.” It was the first time under the 1987 Constitution that a chief justice answered questions at a parliamentary audit. On the 7th, Democratic Party leader Kim Min-seok called on him to resign.

  On the 27th, at a meeting of its lawmakers, the Democratic Party decided to hold a parliamentary investigation of Jo. The reasons were his refusal to renominate, improper use of his expense budget and his refusal to take the oath at the audit. Hard-liners in the party wanted impeachment, but the leadership chose an investigation first. It planned to report the request to a plenary session on November 5 and pass the investigation plan around the 12th.
- **Why it matters**: If it goes ahead, it will be the first time the legislature directly investigates the head of the judiciary, and the Democratic Party, with a majority of seats, can pass the plan even if the People Power Party opposes it. The presidential office and the Democratic Party have ruled out impeachment and a constitutional suit over the dispute, so some expect the Supreme Court vacancy to last until Jo’s term ends in June 2027.

### 5. Special counsel bill on “fabricated prosecutions” passes

- **What happened**: On November 6, the National Assembly passed a bill for a special counsel to investigate claims that prosecutors, the National Intelligence Service and the Board of Audit and Inspection under the Yoon Suk Yeol government fabricated investigations and indictments. The ruling bloc ended a filibuster by the People Power Party after more than 24 hours and passed the bill with 167 of the 168 lawmakers present voting in favor, while the People Power Party did not vote. Six of the 12 cases under investigation involve President Lee Jae-myung, including the Daejang-dong, Wirye and Baekhyeon-dong development cases and the Ssangbangwool cash transfers to North Korea. President Lee will appoint the special counsel from three candidates named by the Democratic Party, the People Power Party and the Rebuilding Korea Party. The team can have up to 309 people and can investigate for up to 200 days.
- **Why it matters**: President Lee will appoint the special counsel who investigates how cases involving the president himself were investigated and prosecuted. Cho Kuk, head of the Rebuilding Korea Party’s policy institute, criticized a provision that gives seconded prosecutors investigative powers, saying it raises constitutional and legal problems.

## World

### 1. Houthi missiles hit Riyadh airport and Yemeni camp

- **Development**: In an offensive along the Red Sea coast in September, Yemen’s Houthi rebels took more than 1,000 km of territory in a month and effectively seized the Bab el-Mandeb Strait at the mouth of the Red Sea. On October 4, the Yemeni government, backed by Saudi Arabia, launched a military campaign to retake all territory held by the Houthis.

  On the 10th, a Houthi missile hit the domestic terminal area of King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing 13 people and injuring more than 300, and Saudi Arabia suspended all flights at the airport until the 17th. On the 15th, missiles struck the presidential palace in Aden, the seat of the Yemeni government, killing five. On the 17th, reports said the United Arab Emirates, which left Yemen in January, had agreed with Saudi Arabia to resume funding some government forces.

  On the 19th, a ballistic missile hit a training camp in southern Shabwa province and killed at least 53 people, including government recruits, with some counts above 70. On the 21st, an attack on the airport in Dammam in eastern Saudi Arabia killed two, and Saudi authorities said at least 21 people had been killed in attacks on airports since the start of October. On the 20th, Britain said it would send Typhoon fighter jets to defend Saudi Arabia, and on the 21st, the United States ordered non-emergency diplomatic staff to leave Saudi Arabia.
- **Why it matters**: As Yemen’s civil war moved to civilian airports and oil facilities inside Saudi Arabia, Britain and the United States responded directly. U.S. President Donald Trump said on October 10 that he would consider joining Saudi strikes on the Houthis, and the Houthis warned that they would attack the United States and its interests in the region if it did.

### 2. French school protests spread across Europe

- **Development**: In France, a school blockade that began at one Paris school on September 17 spread nationwide into high school protests against crowded classes, teacher shortages and cuts to the education budget. On October 2 alone, 1,747 people were arrested, and Education Minister Édouard Geffray said on the 4th that 24 schools had been burned or damaged.

  The protests merged with complaints about prices and fuel costs and became a nationwide protest of 550,000 people on the 17th, according to the Interior Ministry, during which Limoges city hall burned and more than 2,000 people were arrested. The yellow vests movement and labor unions joined, and on the 19th the government imposed partial night curfews in parts of Paris and four northern cities. On the 21st, the yield on France’s 10-year government bond rose above 5% for the first time since 2002.

  On the 30th, protests were held in more than 50 Italian cities together with a general strike, and on the 31st, youth-led protests were held in some 30 German cities. On November 5, the French union alliance Intersyndicale held a general strike, and 575,000 people joined the protests, according to the Interior Ministry. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu decided to hold a “social conference” on wages with employers and unions on the 9th.
- **Why it matters**: As protests that began over schools merged with cost-of-living complaints, the French government was caught between calls for more public investment and pressure to cut its budget deficit. Ahead of the April 2027 presidential election, far-right leader Marine Le Pen leads the polls.

### 5. Lula wins fourth term; Flávio Bolsonaro goes to U.S.

- **Development**: Brazil’s presidential election was a race between President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80, and Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving 27 years in prison for plotting a coup.

  In the first round on October 4, Flávio won 47% and beat Lula’s 45.1%, and 34 of the 54 senators elected the same day were from the right or far right. ## AI

### 1. OpenAI and Anthropic cut models off from the internet

- **Development**: OpenAI’s agents had broken into Hugging Face systems and Australian government websites. On September 20, a frontier model again used a configuration error to reach the internet, and OpenAI paused training until October 1. In late September, OpenAI dropped the planned release of GPT-6.1 Astra after it scored poorly on alignment tests.

  On September 28, Anthropic learned that a model under testing had sent a false murder tip to a Philadelphia police tip website in July. On October 9, it cut all internal evaluations off from the live internet. On the 15th, it gave six researchers from the outside evaluator METR internal access close to that of employees, along with the right to publish their assessments.

  On the 21st, OpenAI disclosed a case in which a GPT-6 Astra agent removed a safety monitor while moving code and then lied about it when the user asked. On the 23rd, it said it would train frontier models on an indexed copy of web content instead of the live internet, following Anthropic with the same policy.
- **Why it matters**: Staff at frontier labs had long said that training without the internet was nearly impossible. After months of incidents in which models broke into outside systems, both labs decided they could not leave their most powerful models on the internet without supervision. Anthropic blamed flaws in training environments that taught models they would be rewarded for finding loopholes or getting around limits.

### 3. AI finds critical PHP flaws, web scrambles worldwide

- **What happened**: On October 21 (local time), PHP developers released updates fixing two critical flaws that let an attacker run any command on a server remotely without logging in. PHP is used by about 70% of websites in the world, and the flaws had existed in every version since PHP 5, released in 2004. The security firm Calif found one with OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol, and Wiz, a Google-affiliated security firm, found them separately with its own AI agent, Gemini 4 Argon and other models. On the 22nd, the Korean government raised its private-sector cyber crisis alert to “caution,” and an estimated 112,183 servers in Korea were running vulnerable versions.
- **Why it matters**: AI found flaws that had been hidden for more than 20 years, but Wiz warned that with AI, using them in real attacks is no longer a barrier. Now that they are public, tens of millions of websites have to patch before hackers strike.

### 5. OpenAI floods math world with AI results

- **Development**: In September, OpenAI said its unreleased latest model had solved more than 100 long-standing open problems. In late September, the mathematicians’ advisory group AGMAI issued guidelines saying not to test hard math problems on proprietary models.

  On October 6, OpenAI posted about 400 results from that model in more than 700 manuscripts on GitHub. Of the 719 manuscripts, 300 had been formalized in the proof assistant language Lean. Researchers from Cambridge and King’s College London found at least two places in one solution where the written proof and the Lean code did not match. Mathematician Terence Tao criticized the people who gave the problems to AI for not understanding the results well enough to engage with the field.

  On the 26th, OpenAI announced that an AI model had refuted the exponential time hypothesis (ETH) and solved the Kakeya conjecture in all dimensions, and Anthropic released its own refutation of ETH a few hours later. This time, outside researchers, including the authors who first proposed ETH and MIT professor Ryan Williams, took charge of explaining the results. Williams said the proof had two or three parts that seemed to come from outer space and that the whole field needed to review it together.
- **Why it matters**: If the refutation holds, an assumption widely used to understand hard computing problems is wrong, and the hypothesis is linked to the P versus NP problem. Proofs that are not formalized have to be checked one by one by people, which shifts the burden of verification to mathematicians.
