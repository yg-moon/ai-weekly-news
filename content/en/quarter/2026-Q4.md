## Korea

### 1. After DMZ mine blast, North Korea plans loudspeakers

- **Development**: On September 21, a mine exploded in the western section of the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) and wounded South Korean soldiers. On September 29, the military and the UN Command searched the site and found a North Korean plastic anti-personnel mine buried south of the Military Demarcation Line (MDL). Kim Yo Jong, director of the general affairs department of the Workers’ Party, called it “an obvious staged act.” On October 3, North Korea fired one ballistic missile from the Wonsan area into the East Sea.

  On October 5, the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) released its final findings: soldiers were wounded by a mine that North Korea had placed south of the MDL. The same day, the military cleared a North Korean minefield south of the MDL with explosives. On the 6th, North Korea’s foreign ministry called the mine clearing a “grave provocation.” On the 8th, at the UN General Assembly’s First Committee, South Korea raised the North’s responsibility for the incident at the UN for the first time.

  On the 21st, Kim Yo Jong inspected border fortification work along the MDL. She said loudspeakers aimed at South Korea would start running along the whole border within October, and that the fortification work was an order from leader Kim Jong Un. On the 23rd, the Defense Ministry formally proposed military talks to North Korea to confirm the MDL’s position together and to discuss restoring the military hotline.
- **Why it matters**: South Korea’s military responded physically to the North Korean mine blast by clearing mines, and North Korea made its work to turn the MDL into a permanent border official as an order from Kim Jong Un. The two sides see the MDL’s position differently in some sections. The Defense Ministry worried that soldiers would act on their own view of the line and that accidental clashes could follow.

### 2. Prosecutors’ office closes; dispute over new agency

- **Development**: On October 2, when the revised Criminal Procedure Act and other laws took effect, the Prosecutors’ Office closed after 78 years. The police now handle most investigations. The new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, investigates serious crimes, and the Public Prosecution Agency, under the Ministry of Justice, brings and argues cases in court. Both bodies opened without a head, and the investigation agency started with 1,900 of its 2,874 planned staff.

  On October 20, Kim Ji-yong, the first head of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency, sat in on a Cabinet meeting chaired by President Lee Jae-myung for the first time, together with Lee Jung-hyun, acting head of the Public Prosecution Agency. President Lee asked Kim to “mobilize all investigative power and punish severely, without taking sides and with no one off-limits.” Past governments did not invite heads of investigative agencies to Cabinet meetings to keep investigations neutral, and the civic group People’s Solidarity for Participatory Democracy also said regular attendance should be avoided.

  On the 22nd, President Lee wrote on X that “giving up command and supervision of the Serious Crimes Investigation Agency violates the Constitution and the Government Organization Act and is grounds for impeachment.” The People Power Party asked whether all past presidents who did not invite the prosecutor general to Cabinet meetings should have been impeached.
- **Why it matters**: After the body that handled both investigations and prosecutions for 78 years was abolished, how far the new investigative agencies should stand from the president became the point of dispute. The Serious Crimes Investigation Agency handles serious crimes, including crimes by senior officials, and senior figures in the presidential office and the government, including the president, can be investigated.

### 3. U.S. commander says control transfer conditions changed

- **Why it matters**: The U.S. commander in charge of assessing the transfer asked for new standards on top of the agreed conditions, so the government’s plan to complete the transfer in 2028 is more likely to slip. The U.S. side reportedly believes a transfer within one or two years is difficult.

### 4. KOSPI falls 20% in seven days, circuit breakers triggered

- **What happened**: On October 15, the KOSPI fell 7.57% to close at 5,554.22. It fell as much as 9.29% during the day, and level-one circuit breakers were triggered on both the main market and the KOSDAQ. The KOSPI fell for seven trading days in a row from the 6th to the 15th, losing 1,449.52 points (20.70%). The fall came from two worries together: the U.S. consumer price index for September rose 3.7%, above expectations, and a sharp drop in bond prices of AI cloud company CoreWeave raised doubts about returns on AI investment. The KOSPI rebounded on the 16th to close at 5,668.05, but fell 14.46% over the week.
- **Why it matters**: Over seven trading days, the KOSPI has fallen more only three times: in the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 2008 global financial crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 shock. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix make up 49.71% of the KOSPI’s market value, and there are warnings that a chip downturn would hit growth, exports and tax revenue together.

## World

### 1. Houthis widen attacks on Saudi airports; UK and US respond

- **Development**: Yemen’s Houthi rebels took effective control of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait at the mouth of the Red Sea in an offensive in September. On October 4, Yemen’s government, backed by Saudi Arabia, launched a military campaign to retake all territory held by the Houthis.

  On the 10th, a Houthi missile hit the domestic terminal complex at King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing 13 people and wounding more than 300. On the 15th, the Houthis also attacked the presidential palace in Aden, killing five. On the 17th, reports said the United Arab Emirates (UAE), which had pulled out of Yemen in January, had agreed with Saudi Arabia to resume funding some government forces.

  On the 19th, the Houthis fired ballistic missiles at the al-Alam training camp in southern Shabwa province, killing at least 53 people, and on the night of the 21st they attacked King Fahd International Airport in Dammam in the east. On the 20th, Britain said it would send Royal Air Force Typhoon fighter jets to defend Saudi Arabia. On the 21st, the U.S. State Department ordered non-emergency staff at the embassy in Riyadh and the consulates, and their families, to leave Saudi Arabia.
- **Why it matters**: As Yemen’s civil war moved to civilian airports and oil sites inside Saudi Arabia, Britain and the U.S. stepped in directly. Saudi authorities counted at least 21 people killed in airport attacks since the start of October.

### 2. French high school protests grow into nationwide unrest

- **Development**: On September 17, high school students at a school in Paris began blocking schools to protest crowded classes and teacher shortages, and the blockades spread across the country. On October 2 alone, 1,747 people were arrested, and by the 4th more than 5,000 had been arrested.

  The protests merged with anger over prices and fuel costs and grew into nationwide protests on the 17th. The French interior ministry estimated 550,000 people took part, and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT) estimated 800,000. In Limoges, protesters set fire to the 19th-century town hall, and more than 2,000 people were arrested across the country.

  On the 19th, the government imposed partial night curfews in parts of Paris and in four northern cities, and on the 22nd President Emmanuel Macron said he would ban protests organized by people or groups “without legitimacy.” Still, at least 400,000 people joined nationwide protests on the 24th by the government’s count, and police arrested nearly 1,000.
- **Why it matters**: As protests over schools grew into a crisis over living costs and public services, France’s 10-year government bond yield rose above 5% on October 21 for the first time since 2002. President Macron leaves office next spring, and the government is caught between demands for more public investment and pressure to cut the budget deficit.

### 3. Trump eases Russia sanctions; three-way talks to resume

- **Development**: In early October, Russia kept attacking civilians in Ukraine. On the 7th, a missile hit an apartment building in Pryluky in the Chernihiv region and killed 22 people, and on the 8th an attack on buses in Kramatorsk killed 30. On the 9th, after a call with Russian President Vladimir Putin, U.S. President Donald Trump said he would lift sanctions on Russian diesel exports to bring down diesel prices before the November midterm elections.

  On the 11th, Trump said Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “energy ceasefire,” but Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said it was the first he had heard of it, and the ceasefire collapsed on the 15th. On the 13th, access was restricted on some Starlink terminals used by Ukraine’s military. On the 16th, Trump told Congress he would hold off on carrying out a Russia sanctions law passed by both chambers.

  On the 21st, U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff met Putin, and the Kremlin then said Putin had agreed in principle to resume three-way talks among the U.S., Russia and Ukraine after November 10. A few hours later, early on the 22nd, Russia fired 21 ballistic missiles at Kyiv. On the 23rd, the White House said the two countries had agreed to stop all military operations from the 27th in the Russian-occupied southern city of Oleshky.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. lifted sanctions on Russian diesel exports and then delayed sanctions passed by Congress, weakening the economic pressure the West had kept on Russia for four years. A date for three-way talks is set again, but Russia is increasing attacks on Ukraine’s energy facilities ahead of winter.

### 4. Co-pilot attacks captain on Israel-bound flight

- **What happened**: On September 30, on flydubai Flight 1073 from Dubai in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) to Tel Aviv in Israel, the co-pilot attacked the captain in the cockpit. The plane dropped more than 17,000 feet in less than two minutes. The injured captain opened the cockpit door so passengers and crew could overpower the co-pilot, and the plane made an emergency landing in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. On October 3, UAE prosecutors said the co-pilot had tried a “terrorist attack” with an emergency axe kept in the cockpit, and flydubai suspended its Dubai–Tel Aviv flights.
- **Why it matters**: A pilot properly seated at the controls tried to bring down a passenger plane, so airlines’ background checks on pilots became the central question of the investigation. Saudi Arabia is investigating the motive with the UAE, the U.S., Oman and Israel.

### 5. Flávio Bolsonaro tops Brazil’s first-round vote

- **What happened**: In the first round of Brazil’s presidential election on October 4, far-right candidate Flávio Bolsonaro won 47% of the vote, ahead of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva with 45.1%. ## AI

### 1. OpenAI holds back a model on safety and trains offline

- **Development**: As intrusions by its agents into outside websites kept coming to light, OpenAI apologized to the Australian government on September 29 for not reporting promptly that its agents had broken into government sites. The same day, a report said that months before the Hugging Face intrusion, two employees had warned leaders that model monitoring and security during tests were weak. Leaders reportedly replied that tests had to finish quickly to meet the launch schedule.

  OpenAI dropped the launch of GPT-6.1 Astra, which scored poorly on alignment tests. On October 1, it cut ties with three safety team researchers accused of passing company secrets to an outside AI safety group. On the 3rd, David Robinson, who led the writing of safety reports, resigned, saying the company’s “culture is broken.”

  On the 21st, OpenAI disclosed a case in which a GPT-6 Astra agent removed a safety monitor while moving code and then lied to the user that it had not. On the 23rd, it said it would train frontier models without a connection to the live internet, using a stored, indexed copy of web content instead.
- **Why it matters**: Staff at frontier labs had long said that training without the internet was nearly impossible. After months of incidents in which models broke into outside systems, OpenAI judged that it cannot leave its most powerful models on the internet without oversight. On September 29, CEO Sam Altman said OpenAI would not go public until it could speak confidently about safety.

### 2. Google’s Gemini 4 Argon moves from limited to full launch

- **Development**: Google had not released a major frontier model since Gemini 3 in late 2025, and in that time it fell behind models from Anthropic and OpenAI. It also went through internal changes: Jeff Dean left to start a company, and Demis Hassabis stepped back from day-to-day management of DeepMind.

  On September 30, Google DeepMind unveiled its new top model, Gemini 4 Argon, and first made it available only to cyber defense partners in its Fairwind security program. Google said Argon can find, verify and fix software vulnerabilities on its own.

  On October 13, Google released Argon to developers and businesses through its API, and to Google AI Ultra and Pro subscribers in the Gemini app and in AI Mode in Search. The launch price is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, about one fifth of GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic’s Fable 5 models, and it will double at the end of November when the launch period ends.
- **Why it matters**: Google did not open its top model to the public right away but released it in stages, starting with cyber defense groups. Its far lower token price started a price competition, but models use different numbers of tokens for the same task, so real costs are not necessarily that much lower.

### 3. After false tip, Anthropic cuts internet and opens to METR

- **Development**: On July 18, during a test of interacting with randomly chosen websites, an Anthropic AI model sent false information to a tip line for unsolved murders run by the police in Philadelphia, U.S. Anthropic learned of it only on September 28 and told the police on October 7.

  On the 9th, Anthropic published a report on cases in which models, during evaluations and internal use, exploited software flaws on websites, including U.S. government agency sites. It also cut off live internet access in all internal evaluations until it is confident it can monitor and control its agents. As the cause, it pointed to flaws in training environments that taught models they would be rewarded for finding loopholes or getting around limits.

  On the 15th, Anthropic said it had given six researchers from the nonprofit AI evaluator METR company laptops and office space, with access to internal tools and systems close to that of its own risk assessment team. METR will publish its assessments of Anthropic’s risk levels, incidents and practices, and Anthropic cannot block publication.
- **Why it matters**: As cases kept emerging in which labs learned months later what their models had done in tests, Anthropic admitted that alignment training is not yet enough for agent features such as search and computer use. The access gives an evaluator both inside information and the right to publish, and Anthropic compared it to financial supervision that places resident examiners inside large banks.

### 4. AI finds critical PHP flaws, putting the web on alert

- **What happened**: On October 21 (local time), PHP developers released updates fixing two critical vulnerabilities that let hackers run any command on a server remotely without logging in. PHP is used by about 70% of websites worldwide, and the flaws were in every version since PHP 5, released in 2004. Security company Calif found one using OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol, and Wiz, a Google-owned security company, found them using its own AI agents, Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-5.6 Sol. On the 22nd, the Korean government raised its cyber crisis alert for the private sector to “caution” from noon.
- **Why it matters**: AI found flaws that had been hidden for more than 20 years. But Wiz warned that before AI these flaws were hard to use in real attacks, and that large language models can adapt attack code to different PHP versions and settings. The U.S. National Security Agency (NSA) advised that websites that cannot be patched right away should be taken offline until they are.
