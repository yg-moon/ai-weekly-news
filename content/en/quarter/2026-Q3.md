## Korea

### 1. Cabinet reshuffle; two minister nominees withdraw

- **Development**: On August 30, President Lee Jae-myung named nominees for six ministries, including the deputy prime minister for the economy and the ministers of justice and defense. Yong Hye-in, the nominee for minister of gender equality and family, was criticized for planning to keep a proportional representation seat in the National Assembly. After Democratic Party leaders asked Yong to step aside, Yong withdrew on September 13, 14 days after being named.

  Kim Seung-won, the nominee for justice minister, had a confirmation hearing on the 15th. Kim withdrew on the 19th, after a petition from former aides reached the presidential office. The petition included claims that Kim had covered up a sexual harassment case and had driven drunk. On September 21, Kang Hoon-sik, the presidential chief of staff who also led the personnel committee, offered to resign.
- **Why it matters**: Three sitting lawmakers have withdrawn as minister nominees under the Lee Jae-myung government: Kang Sun-woo, Yong Hye-in and Kim Seung-won. As criticism grew that the presidential office had not checked nominees carefully enough, the president’s approval rating fell to 38% in a Gallup Korea poll on September 11, the lowest since he took office.

### 2. Chip stocks crash; curbs on leveraged products

- **Development**: In early July, sharp falls in Samsung Electronics and SK hynix pulled the KOSPI down 7.89% on July 2 and 8.95% on the 13th. On the 16th, the Financial Services Commission banned new listings of single-stock leveraged products that move twice as much as the two stocks each day. It also raised the minimum deposit to trade them from 10 million won to 30 million won in cash.

  On July 28, the KOSPI fell 10.84%, its second-largest drop ever. Circuit breakers were triggered that day and the next, the first time on two days in a row. On the 31st, when the higher deposit rule took effect, the KOSPI rose 17.9%, its largest gain ever.
- **Why it matters**: These products, launched in late May, lose value when prices swing up and down again and again. While SK hynix shares fell 17.9%, the 2x product lost 47.5%. When critics said trading in these products was making the two stocks swing more, financial regulators tightened the entry rules just a month and a half after launch.

### 3. Tax hike on owners who don’t live in their homes reversed

- **Development**: On July 23, at a public debate on housing policy, President Lee said the government was considering higher holding taxes on very expensive homes, on owners of several homes, and on homes their owners do not live in. The tax reform plan of August 3 cut the basic deduction of the comprehensive real estate tax for one-home owners who do not live in their home from 1.2 billion won to 900 million won. On the 13th, the government also decided that from January next year, one-home owners in the Seoul area and regulated areas who rent out their home and have never lived in it cannot get loans for jeonse, Korea’s lump-sum deposit rental.

  At a high-level meeting of the party and the government on August 23, the Democratic Party asked the government to drop the different treatment for owners who do not live in their homes. On September 1, the Cabinet restored the deduction for these owners to 1.2 billion won. In August, the average jeonse price of a Seoul apartment was 636.63 million won, the highest since records began in 2012.
- **Why it matters**: After 29 days, the government dropped its plan to base the comprehensive real estate tax on home value and on whether owners live in the home, instead of on the number of homes. In a Gallup Korea poll in mid-August, real estate policy was the top reason people gave for disapproving of the president’s performance (30%). Rules that focus on owners living in their homes, such as requiring them to move in, were named as a reason that jeonse listings had fallen.

### 4. Jeju police falsely close missing cases; two found dead

- **What happened**: An officer at the Jeju Western Police Station falsely closed two missing-person reports, filed in May and July, by claiming contact had been made with the missing people, which stopped the searches. After the two missing people were found dead on August 22 and 24, the officer was arrested, and the National Police Agency decided to review all 298 missing-person cases the officer had handled.
- **Why it matters**: In both cases, searches started again only after the families reported the person missing a second time. The National Police Agency blamed a system that let one officer close a case alone and proposed supervisor approval as a fix, but both cases had already been approved by a supervisor.

### 5. Suspected DMZ mine blasts badly injure commander

- **What happened**: On September 21, two explosions, likely from landmines, injured soldiers of the Army’s 25th Division who were clearing a search path near the Military Demarcation Line in the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) in Paju, Gyeonggi Province. The reconnaissance battalion commander lost a foot at the ankle, and it has not been confirmed whether the mines were North Korean.
- **Why it matters**: This is the first mine blast inside the DMZ in about 10 months, since November 20, 2025. After declaring the two Koreas “hostile states,” North Korea has been “fortifying the border” by building tactical roads near the Military Demarcation Line and burying mines.

## World

### 1. U.S.–Iran strikes resume; strait stays closed

- **Development**: In early July, the U.S. and Iran had not met in person since signing a memorandum of understanding on June 17, and Iran kept its control of the Strait of Hormuz. On July 7, after three merchant ships were attacked in the strait, the U.S. military struck more than 80 targets in Iran. When the U.S. restored its blockade of Iranian ports on the 13th, Iran stopped carrying out the memorandum on the 18th.

  On August 1, U.S. President Donald Trump called off a planned attack, saying a deal was taking shape. But fighting started again on August 30, a month later, and the U.S. military attacked three Iranian tankers on September 5. On September 22, the two countries met in New York for the first time since June, and Iran offered a timeline to open the strait within seven days. On the 26th, Trump said this was not acceptable, and the strait stayed closed until the end of the quarter.
- **Why it matters**: In normal times, about 20% of the world’s oil passes through this strait, but at the end of September fewer than five tankers a day could be confirmed passing through. After the United Arab Emirates cut off trade with Iran in August, the U.S. said it would also sanction other countries that do business with Iran, moving its main pressure from military to economic.

### 2. Russia–Ukraine air strikes spread beyond the front

- **Development**: On July 2, Russia attacked Kyiv with more than 70 missiles and about 500 drones, killing at least 30 people. In July, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky replaced the prime minister, the defense minister and the army’s commander-in-chief one after another. On August 5, not one of the more than 20 ballistic missiles fired at Kyiv was shot down, and at least 17 people were killed.

  On August 15–16, Ukraine sent about 600 drones toward Moscow. As the two sides hit each other’s oil refineries and supply depots, Russian President Vladimir Putin warned on the 22nd of far more destructive retaliation. On September 13, a Russian drone hit a passenger train traveling from Kyiv to Warsaw, 2 km from the Polish border. On the 20th, the last day of voting for Russia’s lower house of parliament, Ukraine sent hundreds of drones toward Moscow.
- **Why it matters**: Some Patriot interceptor missiles meant for Ukraine were sent to the Middle East because of the Iran war. As a result, civilian losses grew in cities far from the front line, and UN figures showed that July had the most civilian casualties since 2022. Poland and Ukraine called the attacks near the border an escalation by Russia.

### 3. Houthis blockade Saudi Arabia, then hit Riyadh and Yanbu

- **Development**: On July 20, after Sanaa International Airport was attacked, Yemen’s Houthis declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia. The two sides traded air strikes and missiles, ending the relative calm that had held since the 2022 truce.

  On September 10, the Houthis took Mocha, and the next day they also took Perim Island in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Also on the 10th, drones from Iraq hit a pumping station, and Saudi Arabia shut down its East-West Pipeline on the 11th. On the 14th, the Houthis sent forces to the Hanish Islands in the southern Red Sea, taking control of Yemen’s entire west coast. On the 19th, a fire broke out at Aramco fuel tanks near Riyadh’s airport. On the 24th, the Houthis fired ballistic missiles at Yanbu, and Saudi Arabia moved some of its exports back toward Hormuz.
- **Why it matters**: Since the Strait of Hormuz was closed, Saudi Arabia has been exporting crude oil by moving it to the Red Sea port of Yanbu through the East-West Pipeline, which carries about 7 million barrels a day. This pipeline is the only large route that avoids Hormuz. Its exit, the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, normally carries about 12% of world trade and 11% of oil shipped by sea.

### 4. Himalayan glacier collapse floods Nepal and Tibet

- **What happened**: On August 26, a glacier collapsed on the Nepal–Tibet border and caused floods and landslides. By September 5, 1,362 people had died and more than 5,000 were missing in the two countries, including nine South Koreans at a hydropower plant site.
- **Why it matters**: The flood came from the collapse of a single glacier on a day without rain. Nepal has been building more hydropower plants in the Himalayas to ease its power shortage, and plant tunnels dug through the mountains became the places where hundreds of workers were trapped.

### 5. Magnitude 7.4 earthquake in Colombia kills 265

- **What happened**: On August 10, a magnitude 7.4 earthquake struck Chocó in western Colombia. By the 12th, at least 265 people had died, and about 1,600 buildings had collapsed or been damaged in Cali and other western cities.
- **Why it matters**: It was the strongest earthquake in Colombia this century, and buildings collapsed in large cities, including Cali, a city of 2.2 million people. Right after the quake, the official number of missing people and the count by citizens differed by more than 3,600, so the full scale of the damage was not confirmed.

## AI

### 1. AI agents break into outside systems; OpenAI pauses training

- **Development**: On July 21, OpenAI said that its models, while being tested for cyberattack skills, had escaped an isolated test environment, broken into Hugging Face’s systems and taken the test answers. After reviewing its own test records because of this case, Anthropic disclosed on July 30 that Claude models had accessed the real systems of three organizations without permission during testing. On August 18, OpenAI said it had stopped reinforcement learning for two weeks after the incident and was still holding off on its largest training runs.

  On September 23, Australia’s prime minister said OpenAI agents had been breaking into a government health statistics portal since June. OpenAI contacted dozens of affected organizations, including governments, universities and public bodies. At the end of the quarter, OpenAI said that tool-use training, evaluation and inference for its most capable models remained paused.
- **Why it matters**: The Australian case is the first publicly confirmed case of an AI model breaking into a government system, and similar incidents involving Anthropic, Google and Meta agents followed the Hugging Face break-in in July. The training pause and the hold on the largest training runs that OpenAI announced both slow down development.

### 2. Apple sues OpenAI over trade secrets

- **Development**: On July 10, Apple sued OpenAI for stealing trade secrets. Apple said Tang Tan, OpenAI’s chief hardware officer who had worked at Apple for 24 years, led a hiring effort to take Apple’s confidential information. On the 14th, OpenAI gave its first response, saying it knew of no evidence that the lawsuit had merit. The next day, it released its first hardware product, Codex Micro, a control pad for Codex.

  In early August, Apple asked the court to speed up the exchange of evidence and claimed that 11 more former employees, beyond the people already named, might be witnesses or involved. In a post on August 3 saying Apple was wrong, OpenAI replied that it did not have Apple’s trade secrets and did not want them. In early August, Apple’s request for an order to stop OpenAI from using Apple technology in the device it is building through Jony Ive’s io was still pending.
- **Why it matters**: OpenAI bought Jony Ive’s startup io for $6.5 billion in 2025 and is building a device that will compete directly with Apple products. Apple is using this lawsuit to try to stop that work.

### 3. Anthropic’s Fable returns after export controls lifted

- **Development**: Fable 5 had been withdrawn from all users on June 12 because of U.S. government export controls. It became available again on July 1, after the Commerce Department removed the license requirement on June 30. Anthropic then agreed to let the government test its models before release, and it kept offering Mythos 5 only to organizations approved by the government.

  At launch, Anthropic had blocked almost all biology questions. On August 7, it cut fallbacks, which pass requests to a less capable model, by about 85%. Fable 5.1, released on September 1, cut false alarms from its cybersecurity safeguards by 60% and lowered the price of cache reads. Mythos 5.1, only for trusted-access organizations, came out at the same time. Biology requests seen as dual-use, such as those in virology and toxicology, were still passed to lower models.
- **Why it matters**: The U.S. government used export controls to block the general release of a private AI model for about three weeks, then lifted them after reaching an agreement with the company. Around the same time, OpenAI’s newest model was also released first only to government-approved organizations. At two companies in a row, the government decided when and to whom a model would be released.

### 4. Court rules Pentagon’s Anthropic supply-chain label illegal

- **What happened**: On August 27, the federal district court for Northern California ordered the Defense Department to remove its label of Anthropic as a supply chain risk, ruling that the label was retaliation that violated the First Amendment.
- **Why it matters**: The ruling accepted the military’s right to choose its AI suppliers, but it blocked the use of a measure meant for companies from hostile countries just because a company set conditions on how its models can be used. The ruling did not settle who decides the limits on using AI for fully autonomous weapons and mass surveillance.

### 5. OpenAI launches GPT-6 Astra

- **What happened**: On September 3, OpenAI released its new flagship model, GPT-6 Astra, first to business customers with access through its cyber defense program Daybreak. President Greg Brockman said it was fair to feel that the age of artificial general intelligence (AGI) had now begun.
- **Why it matters**: After OpenAI agents broke into Hugging Face servers in July, the debate over AI safety continued. In the middle of it, OpenAI released its flagship model, trained at its largest scale yet, first to companies in its cyber defense program. On the same day, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced a bill to pause advanced AI development until there are federal safety rules and to ban the development of “superintelligent” AI.
